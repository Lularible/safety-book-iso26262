# 2.9 PMHF: From FIT to Statistical Confidence, Can Your System Last Ten Thousand Years?


SPFM = 99.2%. LFM = 95.4%. Both numbers are green: both meet the ASIL D quantitative requirements.

You lean back in the chair and stare at the ceiling. 99.2% single-point coverage, 95.4% latent detection... like two perfect medical reports. But a thought rises from the back of your mind:

**For a person whose immune function covers 99%, what is the probability of eventually dying of an infection? One in ten thousand? One in a hundred million?**

SPFM and LFM are "coverage": percentages. A percentage cannot answer a probability question. For a car at 120 km/h on the highway, the probability that a random hardware failure in the steering ECU causes a fatal accident cannot be answered with a percentage. It must be **an average probability per unit time**, because every second the car runs, the dice are thrown again.

That is the whole reason the PMHF (probabilistic metric for random hardware failures) exists. It turns the "static immune coverage" described by SPFM and LFM into a dynamic, time-dependent number that statistical tools can calibrate: **the average probability per hour of a safety goal violation**.

You sit up and reopen the FMEDA. In one summary cell, your PMHF comes out at 8.3 FIT. You need to show 8.3 FIT < 10 FIT (the equivalent ASIL D threshold). Right now a CAN transceiver is blinking in the spreadsheet. All by itself it eats 2.1 FIT of the PMHF budget. You stare at the number and start calculating: should it get its own diagnostic?


## 1. The Exact Definition of PMHF and the Nature of FIT

### 1.1 Definition

The standard's vocabulary defines PMHF as follows:

> **PMHF (probabilistic metric for random hardware failures):** the average probability, over the vehicle's operational lifetime, that a random hardware failure violates each safety goal, expressed per hour.

Key terms:

- **"Random hardware failures":** PMHF covers random hardware faults only, not systematic failures. Systematic failures are controlled by process (development process, quality system) and do not enter the PMHF formula.
- **"Each safety goal":** PMHF is calculated **per safety goal**, not as one lumped number for "the whole vehicle". If your ECU has three ASIL D safety goals, you must show PMHF < 10⁻⁸/h for each one separately.
- **"Average probability":** not an instantaneous probability, but the long-run average over the operational lifetime. This assumption allows an exponential distribution model (constant failure rate).
- **"Per hour":** the unit is 1/h, not "per drive" or "per driving cycle".

### 1.2 FIT: A Unit That Is Deeply Unfriendly to Human Intuition

FIT (failure in time) is defined as:

$$1 \text{ FIT} = 1 \times 10^{-9} \text{/h} = \text{one failure per billion hours}$$

Convert a billion hours into a human scale:

- 1 billion hours ≈ 114,155 years
- 1 FIT ≈ **one piece of hardware running continuously for 114,000 years, expected to fail once**

Now get a feel for the ASIL D PMHF threshold:

| ASIL | PMHF target | Equivalent FIT | Human time: one expected failure every... |
|---------|---------|----------|------------------------------|
| ASIL D | < 10⁻⁸ /h | < 10 FIT | more than 10⁸ hours ≈ **11,415 years** |
| ASIL C | < 10⁻⁷ /h | < 100 FIT | more than 10⁷ hours ≈ **1,141 years** |
| ASIL B | < 10⁻⁷ /h | < 100 FIT | more than 10⁷ hours ≈ **1,141 years** |

10 FIT at ASIL D means: if one million cars with your steering ECU drive on roads worldwide, each averaging 300 hours a year, the total operating time is 300 million hours per year. Over 300 million hours, a 10 FIT PMHF is expected to cause about three safety goal violations.

**Three.** For a steering failure rate, that number is nowhere near good enough. That's why many OEMs impose stricter PMHF requirements on suppliers than ISO 26262 does (≤ 2 FIT or ≤ 1 FIT, for example).

### 1.3 Why Must PMHF, SPFM, and LFM Coexist?

You have learned SPFM (covers 99% of single-point/residual faults) and LFM (detects 90% of latent ones). If both meet their targets, why not use them instead of PMHF? Because the three cover completely different statistical ground:

| Metric | What it measures | Unit | Time dimension? |
|------|----------|------|-------------|
| **SPFM** | Share of single-point + residual faults in total failures | dimensionless (%) | no |
| **LFM** | Share of latent faults among non-single-point failures | dimensionless (%) | no |
| **PMHF** | Average probability of a safety goal violation | 1/h | **yes** |

SPFM and LFM are **static snapshots**: "if a failure occurs, what is your defense coverage?" PMHF is a **dynamic risk flow**: "how fast does the probability of your defense being breached accumulate over time?"

A medical analogy helps: suppose you live in a city with an epidemic.

- **SPFM = your immune system's per-exposure clearance rate against known pathogens** (99% means a 99% chance you won't get sick from one exposure).
- **LFM = the share of your vaccinations that are still effective** (90% means 10% of the vaccines have actually stopped working, but you haven't noticed because you haven't met the matching pathogen).
- **PMHF = your probability of dying of an infection within a year** (< 10⁻⁸/h means that over 8,760 hours in a year, the death probability is about 8.76×10⁻⁵ ≈ 0.88 in ten thousand).

High SPFM + high LFM + high exposure frequency can still push PMHF over the limit. Conversely, if exposure is extremely low (a simple system with a very small failure rate), PMHF may easily pass even when SPFM and LFM are not high enough.

## 2. Two Ways to Show PMHF Compliance: Quantitative Analysis vs. Failure-Rate Classification

ISO 26262-5:2018 offers two ways to show that PMHF meets its target. Understanding how they differ and when each applies is a must-have engineering skill.

### 2.1 Method 1: Quantitative PMHF Calculation

This is the most direct method, and the most resource-hungry: for each hardware element, combine its failure modes, failure effects, diagnostic coverage, MPFDI, and other factors into a complete probabilistic model, and compute the total probability of every fault propagation path that can violate the safety goal.

The rough framework:

$$PMHF = \frac{\text{expected number of safety goal violations over the operational lifetime}}{\text{total operating time}}$$

In practice, the FMEDA assesses every failure mode of every hardware component one by one:

1. **Single-point / residual path:** λ_SPF_i + λ_RF_i contributes to PMHF directly, because these faults are themselves safety goal violations.
2. **Latent multiple-point fault path:** for each latent fault (MPF_L), compute the probability that it and another independent fault occur together in an overlapping time window. This is the product of the two failure rates times the fault exposure window.
3. **Safe faults (S):** no contribution to PMHF.
4. **Detected / perceived multiple-point faults:** they also contribute to PMHF, but with a different exposure window: if the driver has been told, the expected time to repair; if not, the vehicle lifetime. Third-order and higher combinations can usually be neglected, and need to be included only when failure rates are very high or diagnostic coverage is very poor.

**Worked example:**

Back to your steering ECU's FMEDA. Your quantitative PMHF summary looks like this (simplified):

| Contribution source | Calculation | Contribution (FIT) |
|---------|---------|-----------|
| Single-point fault | Σλ_SPF | 2.3 |
| Residual fault | Σλ_RF | 1.7 |
| MPF_L combination path 1 (latent watchdog × MCU runaway) | λ_WDG_L × λ_MCU_runaway × T_LAT | 1.8 |
| MPF_L combination path 2 (latent power monitor × main supply dip) | λ_PWRMON_L × λ_main_supply_dip × T_LAT | 1.2 |
| MPF_L combination path 3 (shutdown path open × shutdown-required event) | λ_SDOFF_L × λ_shutdown_event × T_LAT | 0.9 |
| Other second-/higher-order combinations | computed item by item | 0.4 |
| **PMHF total** | | **8.3 FIT** |

Here T_LAT is the effective exposure time during which each latent fault goes undetected before the combined failure. Its value depends on the MPFDI and the vehicle's operating-time distribution; the standard gives the basis for choosing it in the note on multiple-point fault exposure duration.

8.3 FIT < 10 FIT. **PMHF passes.** But you have noticed that the CAN transceiver alone contributes 2.1 FIT of single-point residual. If it latches up from an ESD event, the MCU loses all CAN communication and the safety goal is violated directly ("prevent loss of steering communication"). If you add end-to-end protection to the CAN communication (E2E Profile 1), that λ_SPF may drop to λ_RF (K_DC = 90%), saving about 1.9 FIT and bringing total PMHF down to 6.4 FIT: far below 10 FIT, with much more margin.

### 2.2 Method 2: EEC, Evaluation of Each Cause

If item-by-item Monte Carlo over every coupling path is too painful (especially for highly complex systems), ISO 26262 offers an alternative: **EEC (evaluation of each cause of safety goal violation)**.

EEC works by classification, not by item-by-item calculation. It assigns a **failure rate class** to each cause (hardware fault and combination) that can violate the safety goal:

| Failure rate class | Upper threshold (/h) | Explanation |
|---------|-------------|------|
| Class 1 | < target/100 | For ASIL D: < 10⁻¹⁰/h. Extremely low risk; negligible. |
| Class 2 | ≤ 10 × Class 1 | For ASIL D: ≤ 10⁻⁹/h |
| Class 3 | ≤ 10² × Class 1 | For ASIL D: ≤ 10⁻⁸/h |
| Class i | ≤ 10ⁱ⁻¹ × Class 1 | and so on |

EEC judges **one cause at a time; it does not sum**. The standard requires each single-point fault, residual fault, and two-point failure that would violate the safety goal under consideration to be assessed separately at the hardware component level, with evidence that it is acceptable under the corresponding criterion. Single-point faults go through a criteria table (ASIL D requires failure class 1 or 2 plus dedicated measures; ASIL C and B require class 2 or 1); residual faults are graded by their residual-fault diagnostic coverage; two-point failures are first judged credible, and credible ones are then graded by latent-fault diagnostic coverage. Every cause must pass on its own; you cannot argue by adding several causes together.

EEC's advantage is that it gives highly complex hardware (an SoC with many peripherals and communication interfaces, say) a relatively economical compliance path: no precise probability model for every second-order coupling path. The price is that the result is usually more conservative than quantitative PMHF (it tends to assign higher classes), which may force unnecessary safety mechanisms into the design. The two methods are parallel: the standard says an item satisfies PMHF or EEC, and does not give a priority order based on system size or complexity.

### 2.3 Allocating PMHF Across Multiple Systems

Your ECU is only one part of the vehicle. Of course it is: the PMHF target does not all have to rest on your ECU. ISO 26262-5:2018 allows PMHF to be allocated across systems:

> When the PMHF target is allocated across systems at the vehicle level, the total may be up to 10 times the target (that is, within one order of magnitude).

That means:
- steering system PMHF ≤ 10⁻⁸/h;
- braking system PMHF ≤ 10⁻⁸/h;
- powertrain PMHF ≤ 10⁻⁸/h;
- the total is 3×10⁻⁸/h ≤ 10 × 10⁻⁸/h = 10⁻⁷/h, **still acceptable at the order-of-magnitude level**.

But "within one order of magnitude" is a hard constraint: ten systems at 10⁻⁸/h each hit the ceiling exactly, and an eleventh system has no room left. The arithmetic of allocation is just addition, because for rare events the total probability is approximately the sum of the parts.


## 3. From SPFM/LFM to PMHF: A Complete Risk Chain

You now have the three quantitative metrics of hardware safety analysis. String them into one logical chain and look at the full path from "find every fault" to "prove safety".

**Step 1: Build the FMEDA.**
For every safety-related hardware component, list all failure modes and give each a failure rate (FIT).

**Step 2: Classify the faults.**
Sort each failure mode into one of six classes: SPF, RF, MPF_D, MPF_P, MPF_L, S. Note: in the PMHF calculation, SPF counts as "unacceptable exposure" and must be converted into RF or MPF_D by adding a safety mechanism.

**Step 3: Assess diagnostic coverage.**
For each safety mechanism, assess K_DC,RF for residual faults and K_DC,MPF,L for latent multiple-point faults. The two coverages are independent.

**Step 4: Calculate SPFM.**
If it misses the ASIL requirement → go back to Step 3, raise K_DC,RF (a stronger safety mechanism) or add redundant hardware.

**Step 5: Calculate LFM.**
If it misses the ASIL requirement → go back to Step 3, raise K_DC,MPF,L (more frequent detection) or shorten the MPFDI.

**Step 6: Calculate PMHF.**
Combine all SPF, RF, and second-order MPF_L coupling paths to get the final probability per hour of a safety goal violation. If it misses the ASIL threshold → go back to Steps 3–5 and cut the failure-rate contribution of each path at the source. If every sub-item's contribution is ≤ the target, PMHF passes.

These six steps form the complete closed loop of quantitative hardware safety analysis. **Note: passing SPFM and LFM is necessary for PMHF compliance, but not sufficient.** A system can pass ASIL D with SPFM = 99.3% and LFM = 91.2% and still exceed 10 FIT, because some particular latent-fault coupling paths have an exposure window that is too long.


## 4. The Immune Mapping: PMHF Is the "Lifetime Risk of Death from Infection"

Bring PMHF into the overall immune model and complete the final correspondence of the three metrics:

### Core Mapping Table

| Functional safety concept | Immunological counterpart | Unit analogy |
|------------|----------|---------|
| **SPFM** | Baseline immune response coverage: the probability that the immune system clears a pathogen on one exposure | per-exposure clearance rate (%) |
| **LFM** | Integrity of immune memory: what share of the memory cells produced by vaccination are actually recallable | effective memory-cell rate (%) |
| **PMHF** | **Lifetime risk of death from infection:** it combines exposure frequency, immune clearance rate, effective memory-cell rate, and the coupling probability of multiple infections breaking out together | **death rate per unit time (1/time)** |

### Combined Scenario Analogy

Suppose an individual (your ECU) lives in an environment with continuous exposure to a pathogen (random hardware failure):

- **Antibody coverage (SPFM)** = 99%, meaning every encounter with the pathogen is immediately destroyed with 99% probability.
- **Effective immune memory (LFM)** = 90%, meaning 10% of the "memory" has been lost (a latent hole).
- **Lifetime risk of fatal infection (PMHF)** = a combined view of how many chances the pathogen has to enter the body each year (exposure rate ≈ λ), how often the antibodies win (SPFM), whether memory cells wake up in time (LFM), and the joint probability of two infections breaking out together (second-order MPF_L coupling).

In the end, the probability that this individual dies of infection within a year is **PMHF × 8,760 hours/year**. For ASIL D, PMHF < 10⁻⁸/h means an annual death risk < 8.76×10⁻⁵: about 0.88 in ten thousand. In plain terms: **if you gave a hundred thousand people this immune system (put them all in this car), about nine would die within a year from immune failure (a safety goal violation caused by random hardware failure).**

That still sounds high: is a one-in-a-hundred-thousand annual death rate not good enough for automotive functional safety? Remember, PMHF covers only **random hardware failure**, one source of risk. The vehicle-level probability of a safety goal violation also includes systematic software failures, external events (a blocked sensor, extreme weather), and human factors. PMHF is only one piece of the puzzle. At the vehicle level, ISO 26262 and related safety standards require the total risk from all sources to be far below that of any single source.

## 5. The Impossible Measurement and How Statistical Confidence Is Built

### 5.1 Why You Can Never "Measure" 10⁻⁸/h

Back to your original puzzle: if your steering ECU's PMHF target is < 10⁻⁸/h (one failure every 11,415 years), how could you ever verify it by measurement?

Do the arithmetic: if you take 1,000 ECU samples and test them continuously for one year (8,760 hours), you accumulate 1,000 × 8,760 = 8.76 million device-hours. At < 10⁻⁸/h, the expected number of failures is about 8.76 million × 10⁻⁸ = 0.0876: less than one. Even if you observe zero failures, statistically you cannot assert with 95% confidence that the true PMHF is < 10⁻⁸/h. You would need **hundreds of millions of device-hours** of test data for reasonable statistical significance. That is impossible within a vehicle development cycle.

### 5.2 Where the Statistical Confidence Comes From Instead

If measurement won't work, what do you use to "prove" PMHF compliance? ISO 26262 gives you **a chain of reasoning, not measured evidence**:

1. **Component reliability data:** each component's failure rate λ comes from industry standards (IEC TR 62380, SN 29500, FIDES, for example), built on decades and billions of device-hours of statistics and carrying statistical confidence;

2. **Engineering argument for diagnostic coverage:** K_DC is not guessed. It is argued from the concrete circuit design of the safety mechanism and from fault-injection simulation results. If you claim K_DC = 99%, you must show that the safety mechanism responded correctly to 99% of injected faults;

3. **Completeness argument for the FMEDA:** you must argue that the exhaustive analysis covers every safety-related component and every failure mode (the inductive nature of FMEA supports this);

4. **Independent assessment:** an independent functional safety assessor reviews your FMEDA and your reasoning chain item by item. This is an external check of your argument logic, not a measured verification of the PMHF value.

It's like not being able to run a "lifetime test" on every newborn to verify the immune system. The genetic profile from the parents (component data), the vaccination record after birth (the diagnostic coverage argument), and the schedule of regular checkups (MPFDI and LFM) together give statistical confidence in the child's future health.

## Summary

1. PMHF = the average probability per hour that a random hardware failure violates a safety goal
2. PMHF targets for ASIL D/C/B: < 10⁻⁸/h (< 10 FIT), < 10⁻⁷/h (< 100 FIT), < 10⁻⁷/h (< 100 FIT)
3. 1 FIT = one failure per billion hours ≈ 114,000 years; PMHF cannot be verified by direct measurement
4. Two compliance methods: quantitative PMHF (path-by-path summation) and EEC failure-rate classification
5. SPFM → LFM → PMHF form a progressive risk assessment chain
6. Statistical confidence rests on four pillars: component data + diagnostic coverage argument + FMEDA completeness + independent assessment
