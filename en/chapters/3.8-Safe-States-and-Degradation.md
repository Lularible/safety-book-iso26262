# 3.8 Safe States and Degradation: A Survival Strategy After Failure

You're driving an SUV equipped with an EPB (electric parking brake) at 120 km/h on the Shanghai–Kunming Expressway. Both hands on the wheel, right foot on the accelerator. Everything is normal.

Suddenly, the EPB controller's CAN reception stops. On the instrument cluster, the EPB fault warning lamp lights amber. You know that lamp well: the last time you saw it was three days ago, at 3 km/h in the residential parking lot, when the EPB automatically applied the parking brake. That scenario was safe; the car was essentially stationary, and the parking brake simply held it firmly in the parking space.

But now it's different. You're doing 120 km/h. If the EPB now follows its "default safety reaction" and applies the parking brake, the rear wheels lock instantly and the vehicle spins out. According to vehicle-level hazard analysis under ISO 26262, this is an ASIL C/D fatal scenario.

But if the EPB chooses to "do nothing," that's not acceptable either. You will probably reach your destination in 10 minutes and need to park on a slope. If the EPB has failed completely, you cannot apply the parking brake, and rolling away can also cause a fatal accident.

This is the highest-level dilemma in functional safety design: the safe state. At every point in time after a fault occurs, the system must be in an operating mode that "does not create unreasonable risk." But what counts as "safe" is not absolute: it is scenario-dependent, time-dependent, and condition-constrained.

Functional safety design is not about finding a "perfect system that never breaks"; it is about designing "a system that doesn't kill people after it breaks." In the vocabulary of functional safety, "safe" is never an adjective (describing a property of the system itself) but a verb (describing the system's behavior after a fault).

## The Formal Definition of a Safe State: Not a State, a Process

### ISO 26262's Definition of a Safe State

ISO 26262-1:2018 defines a safe state as:

> **Safe State**: Operating mode, in case of a failure, of an item without an unreasonable level of risk.

Translated: **the operating mode of an item, in the event of a failure, that does not present an unreasonable level of risk.**

This definition reads a bit convoluted; it needs unpacking. In ISO 26262 terminology, "safe state" specifically means the operating mode the item enters **after a fault occurs**. Its core is "acceptable risk," not "no fault." Normal fault-free operation is called normal mode in the standard, not safe state. A safe state is an operating mode that is **actively entered after a fault triggers** and has been designed and verified. An EPB system in normal operation (no fault) is in the "available" mode, which is of course also safe, but the standard does not call it a "safe state." An EPB system that enters a mode of "warning lamp on, parking brake locked in its current position" after a CAN communication fault—that is the safe state as the standard defines it. It is not healthy (it has a fault), but it is safe (it will not lock the rear wheels at 120 km/h).

### The Fault-Response Time Chain

A safe state is not reached instantly: it is a strictly time-constrained process. ISO 26262 defines a series of time parameters that constrain fault response:

**FDTI (Fault Detection Time Interval)**
The time from fault occurrence to fault detection. FDTI includes:
- communication timeout (for example, a CAN frame not updated for 500 ms = timeout)
- corruption detected by CRC/E2E checking
- an illegal transition detected by program-flow monitoring
- an access violation exception raised by the memory protection unit (MPU)

**FRTI (Fault Reaction Time Interval)**
The time from fault detection to the system entering the safe state. FRTI includes:
- the Safety SW-C receiving the fault notification and triggering the corresponding safety response
- the actuator receiving the command and performing the physical action (such as opening the motor bridge or applying the brake)
- the time constant of the physical process (such as the motor rotor's inertial coast-down time)

**FHTI (Fault Handling Time Interval)**
FHTI = FDTI + FRTI. That is, the entire time span from fault occurrence to the system entering the safe state.

**FTTI (Fault Tolerant Time Interval)**
This is the most critical parameter in the safety concept. FTTI is the shortest time span from a fault in the item to a possible hazardous event, assuming safety mechanisms do not activate. If FHTI > FTTI, a hazardous event may occur; no matter how well the safety mechanism is designed, a response that is too slow is no response at all.

Mathematical constraint:
```
FHTI = FDTI + FRTI < FTTI
```

This inequality is the "first constraint" of the entire safety architecture design. Every fault-detection mechanism (E2E timeout, CRC checking, program-flow monitoring) and every fault-response mechanism (degradation, shutdown, warning) must be designed under the premise that "the FTTI constraints of all hazardous scenarios are met."

### A Real FTTI Case

Back to the EPB scenario. The hazardous event is "a rear-wheel lock at high speed causes loss of vehicle control."

Hazard analysis shows that the physical time from the start of rear-wheel lock to complete loss of control is about **120 ms** (depending on the lateral grip between tire and road and the vehicle's yaw inertia). That 120 ms is the FTTI for this scenario; the safety mechanism must detect the fault and prevent rear-wheel lock within 120 ms.

FTTI is not decided by the software engineer; physics decides it. FTTI depends on vehicle dynamics, tire tribology, human reaction time, and other physical constants. The only thing the software engineer can decide is how to compress FDTI and FRTI inside FTTI. When FTTI is too short (say, the 10 ms range in racing), software detection is no longer fast enough, and hardware-level safety paths are required (for example, a comparator that directly shuts off the MOSFET gate driver, bypassing the software stack).

## The Hierarchy of Safe States: Not Shutdown, Degradation

In real vehicle functional safety design, a "safe state" is not a single state but a **hierarchy of degradation levels**. Take the EPB:

### Level 0: Full Function
- CAN communication normal, all sensors and actuators normal.
- the EPB provides all functions: dynamic parking brake assist, auto hold (AutoHold), hill start assist (HSA), emergency braking.

### Level 1: Reduced Performance
- trigger condition: one sensor fails (say, a caliper temperature sensor), but the core actuator is normal.
- degradation response: disable advanced functions that depend on temperature compensation (such as adaptive brake-force adjustment), keep basic parking and emergency braking.
- effect on the driver: almost imperceptible. Only a hidden diagnostic trouble code (DTC) is logged in the cluster, read by service equipment at the next maintenance.
- safe-state type: **functionally limited safe state**—still running, but with a reduced feature subset.

### Level 2: Emergency Operation (EOT)
- trigger condition: core communication fault (say, a CAN frame missing beyond the timeout threshold), or core sensor fault (say, the caliper position sensor), but the actuator (motor) is still controllable.
- degradation response: the EPB enters emergency operation mode. The system can apply the parking brake on an explicit driver request (for example, holding the EPB switch for more than 3 seconds), but all automatic functions (such as AutoHold) are disabled.
- safety parameters:
  - **EOTTI (Emergency Operation Tolerance Time Interval)**: the maximum time span for which emergency operation may be maintained, and also the maximum value of EOTI (the time span for which emergency operation is actually maintained). For example, 300 seconds (5 minutes). After it expires, the transition to the specified safe state must be completed; which endpoint that is, is specified by the design—the standard does not limit it to shutdown.
- effect on the driver: an amber/red warning lamp in the cluster, possibly with an acoustic prompt. The driver must act within EOTTI (for example, pull onto the shoulder). If the driver does not act, the system enforces the final safe state when EOTTI expires.

### Level 3: The Final Safe State
- trigger condition: FHTI exceeds FTTI, EOTTI expires, or the core actuator itself fails (say, the motor drive bridge shorts).
- degradation response:
  - **if vehicle speed < 3 km/h (vehicle stopped or very slow)**: the EPB applies the parking brake, the calipers lock, and the vehicle stops.
  - **if vehicle speed ≥ 3 km/h (vehicle moving)**: the EPB **must not** apply the parking brake. Alternative safe state: the drive bridge goes into neutral (or drive torque drops to zero), the brake lamps come on, the hazard warning lamps flash, and the conventional hydraulic brake system slows the vehicle until it stops. After stopping, the EPB applies the parking brake.
- this is the last line of defense: the safe state is not "what the system does" but "what the system deliberately does not do." The EPB's safe state at high speed is "forbidden to act"; the safest state for a braking system is not to brake.

The highest wisdom of safe-state design is the wisdom of restraint. Engineering instinct says "apply the brake immediately when the braking system fails," but physics tells you that locking the rear wheels at 120 km/h is far worse than no braking. A safe state is not a single action but a decision tree that depends on operating conditions; it must be enumerated during design through hazard analysis, not improvised by software after the fault occurs.

## Architectural Implementation of Degradation Strategy: Physically Separating the Safety Path

Implementing a degradation strategy usually involves physically separating two control paths:

### Functional Path
- the normal control loop, passing through the complete software stack (application layer→RTE→basic software layer→MCAL→hardware).
- it has the highest performance and the fullest functionality, but also the longest latency (the full software stack may add microseconds to milliseconds).

### Safety Path (Hardware Safety Path)
- a bypass path that connects fault-detection hardware directly to the safety actuator.
- it skips most of the software stack and relies only on hardware comparators, logic gates, and dedicated safety state machines (such as the SMU (Safety Management Unit) in the Infineon AURIX).
- latency is extremely low (usually nanoseconds to microseconds), but functionality is extremely limited—usually only "shut down" or "hold the current position."

Example: the safety shutdown path of an EPS motor.

```
Normal path: application layer computes torque → RTE → PWM driver → motor bridge → motor
Safety path: bus current sensor → hardware comparator → directly shut off MOSFET pre-driver → motor bridge opens
```

When the EPS software computes an abnormally high torque command (say, because the stack overflow described earlier skipped the limit function), the functional path faithfully outputs that command. But the hardware comparator on the safety path independently monitors bus current. Once current exceeds the preset hardware threshold (say, 150 A), the comparator directly triggers the shutdown pin of the MOSFET pre-driver and opens the motor bridge **within 200 nanoseconds**, completely bypassing the possibly faulty software stack.

This is like the immune system's **complement pathway**. It does not depend on the three-stage "recognize-activate-attack" cascade of T cells or B cells; it directly activates the membrane attack complex (MAC) on the bacterial surface, instantly punching holes in the bacterial membrane. The complement pathway is a kind of "hardware safety path": low latency, high robustness, and single function (it only attacks bacteria; it does no antigen presentation or immune memory).

## Recovery Strategy: Returning from a Safe State to Normal Operation

After the system enters a safe state, when can it recover?

The standard lists the conditions for recovery from a safe state as part of the warning and degradation strategy:
1. the fault has disappeared (for example, CAN communication is restored).
2. the reason the fault disappeared is confirmed (not a transient stroke of luck, but a systematic recovery).
3. the system has passed a self-test (a power-on self-test (POST) or a re-run of the periodic self-test).
4. the recovery operation itself introduces no new risk (in some scenarios (such as driving at high speed), automatic recovery is never allowed).

### The Four Recovery Modes

**Mode A: Auto-Recovery**
- condition: the fault cause is cleared and the current operating scenario permits recovery.
- typical scenario: a short EMI disturbance on CAN causes a timeout; after communication recovers, normal function returns automatically.
- constraint: a maximum number of automatic recoveries must be set. Lock out after 3 consecutive recoveries, because repeated recovery means the root problem was never solved.

**Mode B: Driver-Confirmed Recovery**
- condition: the fault is cleared, but the system requires the driver to confirm recovery through a specific action (such as pressing the EPB switch or restarting the vehicle).
- typical scenario: the EPB entered a safe state at low speed; even if the fault has disappeared, the driver must actively release the parking brake to prevent accidental release.

**Mode C: Service Recovery**
- condition: the fault can be recovered only after service equipment (a diagnostic tool) clears the DTC through a UDS service.
- typical scenario: a permanent core sensor fault (say, a burned-out caliper position sensor). Even after the sensor is replaced, the DTC must be cleared with the diagnostic tool to prevent secondary faults from unauthorized repair.

**Mode D: Non-Recoverable**
- condition: the fault is permanent and cannot be repaired (say, a burned ASIC or non-erasable Flash memory).
- typical scenario: the lockstep core diagnostics of the safety MCU detect a permanent processor-core fault; firmware locks the MCU in the fault state, and recovery requires replacing the whole ECU.

## Driver Warning Strategy: Human-Machine Interaction Safety

A safe state is not just a state machine inside the MCU; it must inform the driver at the right time and in the right way. ISO 26262 and ISO 21434 (cybersecurity) both have explicit requirements for the warning strategy:

### Warning Levels

**Level 1: Information — visual/auditory**
- trigger condition: function degraded, safety unaffected.
- display: amber indicator in the cluster, a short acoustic prompt (one beep).
- timing requirement: displayed within 500 ms of fault detection.

**Level 2: Warning — visual/auditory/haptic**
- trigger condition: safety function limited; the driver needs to act, but there is no immediate threat.
- display: red warning lamp/pop-up text, continuous acoustic warning (a beep every 1 second), optional steering wheel vibration.
- timing requirement: activated within 200 ms of fault detection.

**Level 3: Critical Alert — full sensory mobilization**
- trigger condition: about to enter the safe state; the driver must respond within a very short time.
- display: red flashing lamp + full-screen warning text, high-frequency beeping, strong steering wheel vibration, brake pedal pulsing.
- timing requirement: activated within 100 ms of fault detection.

The warning itself can become a hazard. If every CAN fault triggers a full-sensory critical alert, the driver will start ignoring it after the third false alarm (warning fatigue). Warning strategy design must balance sensitivity (no missed alerts) and specificity (no false alerts).

## The Immune-System Metaphor: Fever and Coma as Biological Degradation Modes

The human body's immune response strategy is astonishingly isomorphic to a vehicle's degradation strategy:

### Fever = Reduced Performance

When the thermoregulatory center detects an infection (through inflammatory factors such as IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α), it raises the temperature set point and the body starts to "run a fever." During fever:
- skeletal muscle contracts involuntarily to produce heat (shivering) → consumes a lot of energy
- the liver accelerates synthesis of acute-phase proteins → resources shift from normal functions (digestion, growth) to defense
- cognitive function declines ("brain fog") → computational resources shift from high-level cognition to immune regulation

This is the organism's "Level 2 degradation": normal functions (digestion, movement, thinking) are cancelled, and resources concentrate on "core safety functions" (immune defense). Our EPB in Level 2 cancels automatic functions and keeps emergency braking—exactly the same strategy.

### Coma = The Final Safe State

When the injury is too severe (massive brain trauma, severe metabolic poisoning), the brain puts the body into a coma: no response to external stimuli, but heartbeat, breathing, and blood pressure are maintained. This corresponds to the vehicle's final safe state:
- stop all active functions (pass out, decouple from the outside world)
- maintain only the minimum vital functions (heartbeat/breathing = minimal electrical system power/communication)
- allow external intervention to wake it (medical emergency = diagnostic tool + service equipment)

**But**: the human body has one exquisite capability that vehicles do not: **wound self-healing**. An organism can actively repair damage during the safe state (coma)—nerve regeneration, tissue repair. Today's automotive systems have almost no self-healing ability; once they enter a safe state, they can only wait for external repair.

This points to the next frontier of functional safety: **self-healing systems**. But that is already beyond the scope of ISO 26262.

Safe-state design is the "second kind of engineering" in functional safety. The first kind is "how to keep the system from failing"; the second is "how not to kill people after it fails." A safe state is not a static metric; it is a four-dimensional decision matrix: scenario-aware (above/below 3 km/h is completely different), time-constrained (FHTI < FTTI is an iron law), hierarchical (reduced performance→emergency operation→final safe state), and conditional (driver behavior affects state transitions).

## Summary

1. A safe state is a scenario-dependent conditional decision: the EPB's safe state at 120 km/h is "forbid applying the parking brake," while at 3 km/h it is "apply the parking brake"—the wisdom of restraint matters more than the courage to act.
2. The first constraint of the safety architecture is FHTI < FTTI: FTTI is decided by vehicle dynamics and physics (for example, about 120 ms from rear-wheel lock to loss of control), and the sum of FDTI and FRTI must fit inside that window.
3. The degradation strategy has four levels: full function→reduced performance (hidden DTC)→emergency operation (EOTTI-limited; the driver must act within 5 minutes)→final safe state (vehicle speed decides the action).
4. The hardware safety path (200 ns range, comparator directly shuts off the MOSFET) and the functional path (millisecond range, through the full software stack) are physically separated, ensuring that the safety action can still be enforced when software fails.
5. Recovery strategies fall into four modes: auto-recovery (with a limit on consecutive recoveries), driver-confirmed recovery, service recovery, and non-recoverable. The warning strategy is also graded by severity into information/warning/critical alert.
