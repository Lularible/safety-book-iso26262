# 2.5 Safety Goals and the Functional Safety Concept: From "It Must Not Fail" to "How It Should Fail Safely"

HARA is over. In front of you is a list: 22 hazardous events, each with S, E, and C ratings, each with an ASIL at the end of the row. Six are ASIL D, four are ASIL C, eight are ASIL B, three are ASIL A, and one came out QM.

You hand the list to the project manager. He scans it and asks a question that silences you:

"Okay. So what? What do we do next?"

You freeze for a second. Yes, HARA tells you where things can go wrong and how bad it can be. But it does not tell you **what counts as safe**.

What you need is to turn the row "unintended steering torque on the highway = S3 + E4 + C3 = ASIL D" into an operational, verifiable, technology-neutral safety commitment.

That's the **safety goal**.

## The Immune Metaphor: From Threat Identification to an Immune Strategy

Back to the immune system. Through a pattern-recognition receptor (PRR), your body detects a pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP), such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from a bacterial cell wall. You judge this to be an "ASIL D" threat: it can cause severe systemic infection (S3), it is widespread in the environment (E4), and the body's baseline defenses help somewhat but are not enough (C3).

Then what? The immune system needs an **immune strategy**, which is the safety goal.

"Stop the pathogen from spreading in the blood." That is a safety goal. It is not a concrete technical solution (fever or not? make antibodies or not?). It is a top-level functional objective. It answers the question "what does safe mean".

From the safety goal, the immune system unfolds the **functional safety concept**:

- Fever: raise body temperature to suppress pathogen replication (this is "fault detection": the response strategy after the pathogen is detected).
- Vasodilation: let immune cells reach the infection site faster (this is "degraded operation": the ability to keep part of the function through an auxiliary path).
- Complement activation: tag the pathogen and promote phagocytosis (this is "fault response": a concrete safety mechanism).
- Pain and fatigue: force you to rest (this is "driver warning": a status report to the layer above, consciousness).
- If all of the above fail, launch a systemic immune storm: the last line (this is "emergency operation": the degradation measure inside the final fault tolerant time interval).

See? The immune strategy and the functional safety concept follow exactly the same logic: **state clearly what must be done, without prescribing how**. Whether to make antibodies or activate T cells is the "technical safety concept", the immune system's implementation layer. At the functional safety concept level you only need to say "the pathogen must be cleared, with an FTTI of 24 hours".

## The Safety Goal: Defining "Safe" in One Sentence

The standard's vocabulary defines a safety goal as follows, with the detailed requirements in Part 3:

> A safety goal is a top-level safety requirement and the input to the functional safety concept. Each safety goal is associated with one or more hazardous events and expresses the functional objective needed to achieve or maintain the safe state of the item.

Let's break that down:

1. **Top-level safety requirement:** the safety goal sits at the top of the safety requirement pyramid. It is not a technical requirement, not an architectural constraint, not a test case. It is the emperor: the one who gives the order.

2. **Associated with hazardous events:** every safety goal comes from one or more hazardous events identified in HARA. The association must be traceable. You must be able to say exactly which HARA row this safety goal came from.

3. **Functional objective:** a safety goal says what must be achieved functionally, not how it is implemented technically. It lives at the "what" level, not the "how" level.

4. **The safe state of an item:** in ISO 26262, an item is the system unit to which functional safety is applied. It can be an entire steering system or a single ECU.

### How to Write a Safety Goal

A safety goal is not just any sentence. It must have these properties:

**1. Technologically agnostic**

A safety goal does not prescribe which sensor, which MCU, or which communication protocol. It describes the safety requirement at the functional level. "The steering system shall prevent unintended steering torque above 100 N while the vehicle is operating." That is a good safety goal, because it does not prescribe which motor or which control algorithm. "The steering system shall use redundant torque sensors and cross-check them with a lockstep MCU." That is not a safety goal. That is a technical safety requirement (TSR).

**2. Verifiable**

A safety goal must have verifiable acceptance criteria. How do you know it is met? There must be a quantifiable metric. "The steering system shall prevent dangerous steering behaviour." That cannot be verified. What is "dangerous"? What is "prevent"? "The steering system shall prevent unintended steering torque above 100 N while the vehicle is operating": 100 N can be measured, "unintended" can be defined, and met/not met can be judged.

**3. Traceable**

Every safety goal must trace to one or more specific hazardous events in HARA. That means recording the source explicitly in the safety goal document. Every later safety activity, functional safety requirements, technical safety requirements, test cases, must trace back to the safety goal. Traceability is the most common nonconformity in functional safety audits, because many teams gradually "forget" the link between requirements and their source as development goes on.

### FTTI: The Hard Time Constraint on a Safety Goal

Many safety goals carry a **fault tolerant time interval (FTTI)**.

FTTI is defined as **the minimum time interval from the occurrence of a fault to the possible occurrence of a hazardous event, with no safety mechanism acting**.

This is not a simple parameter. FTTI means: if you do not detect the fault and enter the safe state within the FTTI, you have failed. FTTI is your "safety reaction time budget".

A typical example: unintended torque output from the steering system. From the torque sensor detecting an anomaly to the actual torque that can change the vehicle's heading, the safety time window is 200 ms. If the driver does not intervene, the vehicle leaves its lane after 200 ms. That 200 ms is the FTTI. Your entire safety mechanism, fault detection, confirmation, and transition to the safe state, must finish inside that window.

Where does the FTTI come from? From **the deep study of the controllability parameter in hazard analysis**. C3 means the driver can hardly avoid the harm inside the given time window. That window sets the upper bound on the FTTI. If you claim C3 but the FTTI is 10 seconds, the two contradict each other. Ten seconds is enough for most drivers to react, so the controllability rating should be reassessed.

### Combining Several Hazardous Events into One Safety Goal

The standard lets you combine several similar hazardous events under one safety goal. For example:

- Hazardous event 1: on a straight highway, the EPS outputs torque to the left unexpectedly (S3 + E4 + C3 = ASIL D).
- Hazardous event 2: during a highway lane change, the EPS outputs torque to the right unexpectedly (S3 + E3 + C3 = ASIL C).
- Hazardous event 3: during a low-speed turn on a city road, the EPS assists in the opposite direction (S2 + E3 + C2 = ASIL A).

All three point to the same safety goal: "While the vehicle is operating, the steering system shall prevent unintended steering torque from exceeding the safe range."

Note the combining rule: when several hazardous events share one safety goal, the safety goal inherits the **highest ASIL**. In this example, it takes ASIL D.

But combining has limits. You cannot merge brake failure and steering failure into one safety goal: "ensure the vehicle does not lose control". They involve different functional domains, different time constraints, and different safe state definitions. The premise for combining is that the safety goals are similar. If two events have completely different safe states (one is "cut the power", the other is "maintain the power"), they cannot be combined.

## The Functional Safety Concept (FSC): Unfolding the Safety Goal

A safety goal is a statement. It tells you what is safe. But with only that statement, the development team still doesn't know what to do.

The **functional safety concept (FSC)** unfolds that statement into concrete safety functions: **safety behaviour described in functional language, independent of both hardware and software**.

The clause on the functional safety concept in ISO 26262-3 defines what the FSC must contain:

### 1. Fault Detection and Fault Response Strategy

The FSC must state what type of mechanism the system uses to detect faults, and what it does once a fault is detected.

For example, from the safety goal "the steering system shall prevent unintended steering torque", these FSC-level requirements can be derived:

- FSR-01: The system shall continuously monitor the difference between the steering torque command and the actual output. When the difference exceeds threshold X, it shall be judged a torque control fault.
- FSR-02: The system shall continuously monitor the plausibility of the torque sensor signals (the deviation between two independent channels shall not exceed threshold Y).
- FSR-03: After confirming a torque control fault, the system shall transition from normal operating mode to the safe state within T1.

Note the language of these FSRs: they say what shall be monitored, what shall be detected, and that the system shall transition to the safe state. They do not say which ADC to sample with, which CRC to use, or which GPIO to pull low to disable the enable pin.

### 2. Safe State Definition

The **safe state** is one of the most central concepts in the functional safety concept.

ISO 26262-1 defines the safe state as **the operating mode of the item, maintained by safety mechanisms when a fault occurs, in which there is no unreasonable risk**.

The key phrase is "no unreasonable risk", not "zero risk". A safe state is not a "perfect state". It is a state of acceptable risk.

Different systems have different safe states:
- The steering system's safe state may be: assist torque is gradually reduced and finally cut, leaving mechanical steering.
- The braking system's safe state may be: switch to a backup braking mode (such as EPB-assisted braking) and light the brake warning lamp.
- The BMS's safe state may be: open the high-voltage contactors immediately, leaving the high-voltage system de-energized.
- The automated driving system's safe state may be: execute a minimal risk maneuver and pull over.

A system may have **several safe states** for different fault severities. A mild fault can enter a "degraded operation" safe state (function remains, performance is limited); a severe fault must enter an "immediate shutdown" safe state.

### 3. Fault Tolerance and Degradation

The FSC does not assume every fault can be managed perfectly: some faults will reduce system performance. The FSC must define:
- which faults can be tolerated (keep running, with degraded function)
- how far it degrades (performance, accuracy, available functions)
- how long it may run in the degraded state (the emergency operation time window)

This is the immune system's "fever strategy": the body does not simply "shut down" (die) because of a viral infection. It raises its temperature (degraded operation), suppressing viral replication while keeping other vital functions going. If the infection gets worse, it escalates to a more severe immune response (further degradation or a different safe state).

### 4. Driver Warning and Interaction

If achieving the safety goal depends on driver action (say, "stop immediately when the warning lamp comes on"), that dependency must be written explicitly into the FSC, and there must be evidence that:
- the driver can recognize the warning (the lamp is conspicuous enough, the sound is clear enough)
- the driver has enough time to react (the warning must be issued before the FTTI runs out)
- the driver has the right controls (if takeover is required, the steering wheel and brake pedal must be available)

### 5. Functional Safety Requirements (FSRs) and Allocation to the Item Architecture

Every functional safety requirement in the FSC must be **allocated** to an element of the system architecture. Even if the architecture details are not settled, allocate at least at the "functional block" level.

For example: FSR-01 (monitor torque difference) is allocated to the "torque monitoring function block", which is expected to sit inside the "EPS controller". That feeds the later **technical safety concept** and the **HSI (hardware-software interface)** definition.

## FTTI and FHTI: The Time Chain of Safety Reaction

At the FSC level, the FTTI is refined further into the allocation of the **fault handling time interval (FHTI)**.

FHTI equals FDTI plus FRTI. It is the time inside the total FTTI budget that is left for **the safety mechanism to detect and respond to the fault**.

A typical allocation:
- FTTI = 200 ms (the total safety reaction budget)
  - fault occurrence to fault detection: ≤ 20 ms (diagnostic test time interval)
  - fault confirmation (to reject transient false positives): ≤ 5 ms
  - fault response (transition to the safe state): ≤ 50 ms (FRTI)
  - margin: 125 ms (a conservative margin to cover uncertainty in the analysis)

This time budget lands on the FHTI already at the functional safety concept level. Finer allocation moves down to the technical safety concept as the architecture levels descend. The FSC must state the FTTI value and what it constrains for the FSRs: "any implementation of an FSR must ensure that the time from fault occurrence to entering the safe state does not exceed the FTTI".

## Verifying the FSC

ISO 26262 requires the functional safety concept to be verified. The verification report is one of the FSC work products.

The core questions of FSC verification:
1. Is each FSR consistent with the safety goal, and sufficient?
2. Taken together, do the FSRs cover the safety goal sufficiently?
3. Do any FSRs conflict?
4. Are the fault detection and fault response strategies defined in the FSC feasible at the functional level?
5. Is the safe state definition sound and acceptable in the relevant scenarios?
6. Is the FTTI allocated correctly?

Verification usually takes the form of a review, performed by experienced functional safety experts and safety assessors independent of the FSC development team. For high ASILs (C and D), the review is stricter and more formal.

## From Safety Goal to FSR: A Complete Worked Example

Let's show the whole flow with a concrete example.

### Step 1: HARA Identifies the Hazardous Event

- **Hazardous event HE-014:** while driving on the highway, the EPS controller's program runs away because the watchdog failed, continuously outputting maximum left-turn torque (about 50 Nm). The vehicle drifts left at about 0.3 g lateral acceleration and, with no driver intervention, will be completely out of its lane within 2 seconds.
- **S:** S3 (fatal)
- **E:** E4 (steering is always in use)
- **C:** C3 (unexpected steering at highway speed is extremely hard to control; a normal driver cannot correct the heading and stabilize the vehicle in such a short time)
- **ASIL:** ASIL D
- **FTTI analysis:** vehicle dynamics simulation shows that at 120 km/h and 0.3 g lateral acceleration, the vehicle takes about 1.8 s to leave a 3.75 m lane completely. A normal driver reaction (about 1.0–1.5 s of perception, decision, and action) leaves under 0.3 s of margin. The safety mechanism must complete detection and response within 200 ms to leave the driver enough takeover margin. **FTTI = 200 ms**.

### Step 2: Write the Safety Goal

**SG-01 [ASIL D]:** While the vehicle is operating, the steering system shall prevent unintended steering torque that causes the vehicle to depart from its intended path. If an excessively large unintended steering torque occurs, the system shall detect the fault and transition to the safe state within the FTTI (200 ms).

### Step 3: Unfold the Functional Safety Concept

**Safe state definition:**
Safe state SG-01-SS is defined as: "The EPS cuts the drive force to the assist motor, returning the steering system to pure mechanical steering, and notifies the driver through the steering fault warning lamp on the instrument cluster."

**FTTI allocation:**
- fault detection window: ≤ 50 ms
- fault confirmation window: ≤ 10 ms
- safe state transition window: ≤ 40 ms
- total FHTI: ≤ 100 ms
- FTTI margin: 100 ms

**Functional safety requirements:**

| FSR | Requirement | Allocated to | ASIL |
|---------|---------|--------|------|
| FSR-01 | The system shall continuously monitor the program execution integrity of the EPS controller while the vehicle is operating | Program-flow monitoring function block | ASIL D |
| FSR-02 | The system shall continuously monitor the deviation between the motor's actual output torque and the commanded torque while the vehicle is operating | Torque monitoring function block | ASIL D |
| FSR-03 | When FSR-01 or FSR-02 detects a fault (a monitored value exceeds the preset fault threshold), the system shall trigger the safe state transition after fault confirmation | Fault response management function block | ASIL D |
| FSR-04 | The safe state transition shall cut the motor drive force within 40 ms, putting the steering system into pure mechanical mode | Motor drive control function block | ASIL D |
| FSR-05 | After the safe state is triggered, the system shall give the driver a visual warning through the steering warning lamp on the instrument cluster | Warning management function block | ASIL D* |
| FSR-06 | The system shall verify the availability of all safety mechanisms during the vehicle start-up self-test; if a safety mechanism is found to have failed, it shall prevent the vehicle from entering the "drivable" state | Start-up self-test function block | ASIL D |

*Note: the ASIL is inherited from the associated safety goal, so FSR-05 defaults to ASIL D. To write it as "ASIL B(D)", you must go through ASIL decomposition: the decomposed requirements are implemented by sufficiently independent elements, and each decomposed requirement satisfies the original safety requirement on its own. The warning is an auxiliary notification to the driver; on its own it cannot cover the safety goal, so it is developed at ASIL D here.

## Summary

1. The safety goal is the "constitution" of top-level functional safety: concise, technology-neutral, verifiable, traceable, and bound to an explicit time constraint
2. A safety goal inherits its ASIL from the associated hazardous events; events can be combined only when their safety goals are similar
3. The functional safety concept unfolds the safety goal into concrete FSRs: fault detection strategy, safe state definition, degradation concept, and driver interaction
4. Every FSC requirement must be allocated at the functional architecture level
5. The FSC needs formal verification
