# 2.6 The Technical Safety Concept and System Architecture: From "What" to "How"

Two pages lie in front of you. The left page is the functional safety concept: 14 FSRs (functional safety requirements) written in functional language. The template usually looks like this:

> FSR-03: When a torque control fault is detected, the system shall trigger the safe state transition after fault confirmation.

The right page is blank. You need to fill it in: the **technical safety concept (TSC)**.

You pick up a pen and write the first line:

> TSR-03a: The lockstep comparator inside the EPS main MCU shall, when the main core and the checker core compute different results, raise a fault signal to the SMU (safety management unit) within no more than 5 clock cycles.

Then you stop. A question hits you: **FSR-03 says nothing about a lockstep core, an SMU, or clock cycles. Where did you conjure these technical details from?**

That's the essence of the technical safety concept. It does not conjure technical details. It **grafts the functional safety intent onto a concrete technical architecture**. The FSR says "the system shall detect a torque control fault and trigger the safe state transition". You choose a dual-core lockstep architecture, and the FSR then naturally "grows" technical safety requirements: the lockstep comparator, the SMU, the independent GPIO path.

**The functional safety concept asks "what". The technical safety concept answers "how".**


## The Immune Metaphor: From Immune Strategy to Molecular Pathway Implementation

Let's return to the immune system, this time down at the molecular level.

The functional safety concept corresponds to the immune system's "immune strategy":

- "Detect and clear the pathogen" (the safety goal)
- "Complete clearance within 24 hours, with fever as an auxiliary measure during that time" (an FSR-level requirement)

The technical safety concept corresponds to the immune system's **molecular pathway implementation**:

- "After Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) recognizes LPS, it activates the MyD88-dependent pathway and, through a phosphorylation cascade, activates the NF-κB transcription factor…" (these are technical details: TSRs)
- "Complement C3 is cleaved by C3 convertase into C3a and C3b; C3b deposits on the pathogen surface as an opsonin marker…" (this is architecture design: the concrete mechanism description in the TSC)
- "The release of the cytokines IL-1β and TNF-α is regulated by a negative feedback loop, so an excessive inflammatory response does not damage healthy tissue…" (this is the HSI: interface constraints between different systems)

How did the immune system get from "strategy" to "molecular pathway"? Through billions of years of evolution. Every gene mutation was a "technology selection". TLR4 recognizes LPS and not some other molecule not because someone "designed" it, but because over a long evolutionary process this particular molecular recognition mechanism proved effective.

Your technical safety concept does not have billions of years to try things out. In weeks to months, you must use engineering analysis, known architecture patterns, and safety analysis to prove the solution you chose. This particular lockstep core, this particular SMU, this particular safe-state switching path: they must satisfy the functional safety concept **in every operating scenario and against every relevant failure mode**.

## Definition and Core Elements of the Technical Safety Concept

The standard's vocabulary gives the term; Part 4 expands it:

> A technical safety concept is the combination of technical safety requirements (TSRs) and the system architecture design. It specifies the technical implementation of the safety mechanisms in the system architecture so that the functional safety requirements are satisfied, and it serves as input to the detailed hardware and software design.

A few key points:

1. **TSC = the set of TSRs + the system architecture design.** It is not a single document. It is the sum of the technical safety requirements and the system architecture design that carries them.
2. The TSC is the bridge between the FSRs and the detailed hardware/software design. FSR → TSR (derivation) → hardware design / software design (allocation).
3. The TSC's technical granularity should be fine enough that hardware and software engineers can start their detailed design directly from it.

### From FSR to TSR: Deriving Technical Safety Requirements

A TSR is not a "translation" or "copy" of an FSR. The relationship is **derivation**: the FSR states the target state; the TSR states what technical conditions are needed to reach it in the selected technical architecture.

Take one FSR:

FSR (functional level): "The system shall monitor the deviation between the motor's actual output torque and the commanded torque, and shall judge it a fault when the deviation exceeds a threshold."

In the selected technical architecture (one main MCU, two independent torque sensors, a three-phase motor drive bridge, and an independent monitoring MCU), this FSR may derive into the following TSRs:

- TSR-A: The main MCU shall sample the raw data of torque sensor 1 and torque sensor 2 at no less than 1 kHz and cross-check the two signals for consistency (permitted deviation ≤ 3% of full scale).
- TSR-B: The main MCU shall reconstruct the actual motor torque from phase-current sampling and compare it with the commanded torque at no less than 1 kHz. When the deviation exceeds the preset threshold (T_threshold) for longer than T_debounce, it shall set a fault flag.
- TSR-C: The independent monitoring MCU shall read the phase currents through its own ADC channel, independently reconstruct the motor torque, and compare it with the commanded torque received over independent SPI communication. When the deviation exceeds T_threshold_independent, it shall trigger the safe state through an independent hardware path.
- TSR-D: SPI communication between the main MCU and the independent monitoring MCU shall be protected by CRC-16, update at no less than 1 kHz, and a communication failure (three consecutive frames with a CRC error detected within the specified time window) shall set a communication fault flag.

Note the details of these TSRs:
- They are **technology-specific** (1 kHz sampling, 3% deviation threshold, CRC-16, three consecutive frames).
- They are **allocatable to hardware or software** (ADC channels and SPI interfaces are hardware concerns; sampling logic and threshold comparison are software concerns).
- They **do not prescribe a specific hardware part number or the internal implementation of a software algorithm** (no MCU model, no filter algorithm).
- They keep **traceability to the FSR**.

## System Architecture Design

The system architecture design in the technical safety concept is not an ordinary "split the system into modules" diagram. It is a system architecture design **with safety as its core objective**. It must show the following properties (ISO 26262-4):

### 1. Modularity

Safety-related functions and non-safety-related functions should be clearly isolated at the architecture level. You cannot run air-conditioning control software and ASIL D EPS control software on the same MCU without partitioning protection, unless you can prove that the air-conditioning control cannot interfere with the EPS control in any way. That is FFI (freedom from interference).

Modularity also means that if a safety function has to change (say, tuning a safety threshold), the change should not ripple through the whole system. It should stay inside clearly defined module boundaries.

### 2. Adequate Granularity

The architecture decomposition must be fine enough that:
- each element's safety requirements can be allocated unambiguously
- each element's safety properties can be verified independently
- the interfaces between elements can be defined precisely
- failure propagation paths can be traced

An architecture that is not fine enough is a nightmare for safety analysis. When a fault occurs, you cannot tell where it came from or whom it affected.

### 3. Simplicity

"Simple" here does not mean "easy". It means "no unnecessary complexity". Every safety mechanism you add is another possible failure point in the system. A good safety architecture should introduce as little extra complexity as possible while still meeting the safety requirements.

The immune system follows the same principle: it does not prepare a dedicated detection mechanism for every possible pathogen. A limited set of pattern-recognition receptors (TLRs) covers the vast majority of threats. That's simplicity: cover the most scenarios with the fewest mechanisms.

### 4. Interface Definition and FFI

The system architecture design must define internal and external interfaces precisely, ensuring that:
- one element's behavior cannot adversely affect another safety-related element
- a low-ASIL (or QM) element cannot interfere with the correct execution of a high-ASIL element
- access to shared resources (memory, buses, I/O pins) is controlled

For ASIL D systems, FFI is usually achieved by:
- **Physical separation:** the safety-related MCU and the QM-function MCU are two different chips.
- **Time separation:** safety-related tasks and QM tasks execute in different time slots (if they share one MCU).
- **Memory protection:** an MPU (memory protection unit) or MMU ensures that other software cannot access the memory region of safety-related software.
- **Communication protection:** all safety-related communication uses end-to-end (E2E) protection, including but not limited to CRC, sequence numbers, and timeout detection.

## The HSI (Hardware-Software Interface) Specification

The HSI (hardware-software interface) specification is a core work product of the technical safety concept. It lays out and defines the specification of every element of interaction between hardware and software, so that the software and hardware teams share one understanding of what the other side will do and what it expects.

ISO 26262-4 requires the HSI specification to cover:

### Hardware Operating Modes and Configuration Parameters

Software needs to know which hardware registers control which behavior, which operating modes are available, and how to switch between modes safely. A typical example: the MOSFET drive bridge in the EPS controller has a "safe disable" mode, triggered by a specific bit pattern in a particular register. The HSI specification must state precisely what the register is called, how the bit field is defined, how long the hardware takes to complete the mode switch after the register is written, and what signal the hardware outputs to confirm the switch.

### Shared Resources and Mutual Exclusion

If the main core and the monitoring core share an ADC module, the HSI specification must define the mutual exclusion between them: who uses the ADC in which time window, and how simultaneous access is prevented from corrupting data.

### Timing Constraints

How long may the hardware take to respond to a software request? How short must the software's latency be when handling a hardware interrupt? These timing constraints must be stated in the HSI specification, because they directly affect FTTI allocation and verification.

For example: the motor current sampling interrupt latency must be less than 50 µs (the maximum delay from the hardware trigger to the software entering the ISR and starting to read ADC data). Otherwise the 1 kHz control-loop frequency required by the TSR cannot be guaranteed. This timing constraint binds the hardware (interrupt controller priority configuration, upper bound on interrupt response latency) and the software (no long non-interruptible critical section inside the ISR).

### Hardware Diagnostics and Error Reporting

What self-diagnostic capabilities does the hardware offer? How does software access the diagnostic results? If the hardware's BIST (built-in self-test) finds a fault during startup, how does the hardware notify the software? Through the value of a specific status register, or through a dedicated fault signal pin?

All of this belongs in the HSI specification.

## Safety Analysis: FTA and FMEA

The technical safety concept cannot persuade a safety assessor with "I think this design is pretty safe". You need **structured, systematic safety analysis** to show that, in the selected technical architecture, every identified fault and every potential fault propagation path is covered by a safety mechanism.

ISO 26262-4 recommends different safety analysis methods for different ASILs:

### FTA (Fault Tree Analysis)

**Applicability:** highly recommended for ASIL C and D; may be considered for ASIL B.

FTA is a **deductive** analysis: start from the top-level hazardous event and work downward to "what combination of faults could cause this harm". It answers: "how did this hazardous event happen?"

FTA puts the hazardous event at the top of the tree (the top event) and decomposes it downward through logic gates (AND, OR, and others) into intermediate events and basic events (bottom events). The analysis continues until all bottom events are found: the most fundamental fault causes that the design can directly control.

For example, for the top event "the EPS outputs 50 Nm of left-turn torque unexpectedly at highway speed":

```
Top event: EPS unexpectedly outputs 50 Nm of left-turn torque (AND gate)
├── Sub-event 1: the steering command is wrong (OR gate)
│   ├── Bottom event 1.1: the torque sensor outputs a wrong reading
│   ├── Bottom event 1.2: the steering angle signal on the CAN bus is corrupted
│   └── Bottom event 1.3: a command parameter in the main MCU's RAM is flipped
└── Sub-event 2: the safety mechanism fails to stop execution of the wrong command (OR gate)
    ├── Bottom event 2.1: the independent monitoring MCU and the main MCU fail at the same time (common-cause failure)
    ├── Bottom event 2.2: the GPIO hardware path for the safe state switch is broken
    └── Bottom event 2.3: an SMU configuration error prevents the safe state from being triggered
```

The value of FTA is its **structure**. You can quantify how many bottom events must be controlled to prevent the top event, and whether they are joined by AND or OR. If several bottom events under one AND gate share a common cause (say bottom events 1.3 and 2.1 both trace to a clock fault in the same crystal oscillator), you can catch it in time and take design action (such as using an independent clock source).

### FMEA (Failure Mode and Effects Analysis)

**Applicability:** all ASILs (from ASIL A to ASIL D).

FMEA is an **inductive** analysis: start from each failure mode of each component at the bottom and work upward to "what consequence does this failure cause". It answers: "if this component fails this way, what happens?"

The unit of analysis in FMEA can be:
- **System-level FMEA:** the unit is a system function block; the focus is functional failure.
- **Hardware FMEA:** the unit is a discrete component (resistor, capacitor, IC pin) or a functional component (MCU, sensor module); the focus is hardware failure modes.
- **Software FMEA:** the unit is a software function, variable, or interface; the focus is software failure modes (a variable out of range, a function call in the wrong order, and so on).

An example hardware FMEA entry:

| Component | Failure mode | Effect (local) | Effect (system level) | Severity | Detection | Existing control |
|------|---------|----------------|------------------|--------|---------|---------|
| Torque sensor T1 signal line | Open circuit | ADC reading drops to 0 V | Torque monitoring sees the T1/T2 deviation exceed the limit and triggers the safe state | S3 (if undetected) | ADC range check + T1/T2 cross-check | The cross-check in TSR-C; enters the safe state within 50 ms of detecting the deviation |

### How FTA and FMEA Complement Each Other

FTA works top-down and helps ensure you "miss no possible combination of causes".
FMEA works bottom-up and helps ensure you "miss no failure mode of any component".

Together they cover the three-dimensional space of safety analysis.

For an ASIL D system, both are usually required, and their results must be cross-checked: every bottom event identified by FTA should have a corresponding entry in FMEA; a high-severity failure mode found in FMEA but not covered by FTA must be traced back into the FTA to see whether something was missed.

## TSC Work Products

According to ISO 26262-4, the technical safety concept phase produces these work products:

| Work product | Content | Note |
|---------|------|------|
| **Technical safety requirements specification** | All technical safety requirements derived from the FSRs | Every TSR must trace back to at least one FSR |
| **Technical safety concept** | System architecture design + allocation of TSRs to architecture elements | Includes the architecture diagram with safety mechanisms and the allocation matrix |
| **System architecture design description** | The system architecture design document | Shows modularity, granularity, simplicity, and interfaces |
| **Hardware-software interface specification** | Detailed description of all interface agreements between hardware and software | Covers modes, configuration, shared resources, and timing constraints |
| **Safety analysis report** | Results of FTA and/or FMEA | Covers analysis scope, assumptions, findings, and corrective actions |
| **TSC verification report** | Verifies that the TSC satisfies the FSRs, that allocation is correct, and that the analysis is complete | Usually performed as a review |

## Verifying the Technical Safety Concept

Before the technical safety concept is released to the hardware and software teams as input, it must be verified. Verification covers:

1. **Consistency between TSRs and FSRs:** does every TSR correspond to at least one FSR? Taken together, do the TSRs implement the FSRs sufficiently?
2. **Sufficiency of the system architecture:** does it show modularity, adequate granularity, and simplicity?
3. **Coverage by safety mechanisms:** did the safety analysis identify all relevant failure modes? Do the safety mechanisms cover them sufficiently?
4. **Effectiveness of FFI:** can QM and low-ASIL elements really not interfere with high-ASIL elements?
5. **Completeness of the HSI specification:** is every interaction between hardware and software covered?
6. **Achievability of the FTTI:** can the timing constraints allocated in the TSRs be met by the selected technical solution?

Verification usually takes the form of a **review**, performed by experts independent of the TSC development team. For ASIL C and D the review must be more formal and systematic: an explicit checklist, scoring criteria, and recorded and tracked results.

## Summary

1. TSC = the set of TSRs + the system architecture design: it turns functional safety requirements into technical implementation specifications
2. TSRs are derived from FSRs, and their granularity should stop at "interface agreements" rather than "implementation choices"
3. The system architecture must show modularity, adequate granularity, and simplicity; the key is FFI isolation between high and low ASILs
4. The HSI specification is the "contract" between hardware and software
5. Safety analysis (FTA + FMEA) is systematic: FTA top-down, FMEA bottom-up, the two complement each other
6. Every TSC work product must be independently verified
