# 2.3 HARA: From Driving Scenarios to ASIL

You stand at the whiteboard, black marker in hand.

On the left of the board is an ECU block diagram for electric power steering (EPS): torque sensor → MCU → CAN transceiver → power motor → steering rack. Every line is labeled with a data flow. Every box is labeled with a hardware part number.

In the middle you start listing operational situations: "highway, 120 km/h, rain"; "city parking garage, 5 km/h, dry and level"; "mountain downhill, 60 km/h, bend radius 150 m"; "vehicle stationary, service mode, on a lift".

On the right you draw three columns: **S (severity)**, **E (exposure)**, **C (controllability)**.

Spread across the table is your analysis material: field failure data from the previous product, third-party road-use statistics, driver reaction-time studies from several academic papers, and two safety recall reports from the same industry.

You have to answer one question, using precise parameters and data only, not intuition: "If the EPS steering angle calculation goes wrong and, in some scenarios, makes the wheels move in a way the driver does not expect, how serious is that? What level should it get?"

This is **HARA (hazard analysis and risk assessment)**. It is the most important technical activity in the ISO 26262 safety lifecycle. It decides whether your system is QM (just manage it under quality management) or ASIL D (the highest automotive safety integrity level). It decides the workload and cost for the whole development team over the next two to three years. It decides how a development budget of several million is allocated.

You have to make that decision today, on this whiteboard.

## The First Step of HARA: Item Definition

Before you assess any risk, you must answer a question that looks simple but matters enormously: **where is the boundary of the analysis? What exactly is "this item"?**

The item definition must cover:

**Functional boundary:** what vehicle-level function does this item implement? For our EPS: "based on the torque the driver applies to the steering wheel and the vehicle state, provide assist steering torque through a motor to help the driver steer".

**Physical boundary:** which hardware and software does this item include? Torque sensor (input) → EPS ECU (processing) → motor drive → power motor → steering rack (output). The CAN bus interface to the rest of the vehicle's ECUs is also inside the boundary.

**External interfaces:** how does this item interact with the outside? Steering wheel (driver input), wheels (output to the road), electronic stability control (ESC, via CAN), vehicle speed sensor (input signal).

**Operating modes:** which modes does this item have? At minimum: normal assist, fault degradation, park assist, service/diagnostic, initialization/self-test.

The precision of the item definition directly affects HARA quality. Draw the boundary too narrow, say only "the software on the EPS MCU", and you miss the harm from sensor faults, communication faults, and mechanical faults. Draw it too wide, say "the whole vehicle's steering function", and you drag suspension and tire wear into HARA even though they are outside the EPS design scope, and the analysis sprawls.

The item definition does one more thing: it marks the boundary of your safety responsibility. Harm caused by a fault inside the item is yours to cover. For abnormal behavior on an external interface (say the vehicle speed sensor sends a crazy value), you must define a safety mechanism to respond. But it is not your job to guarantee the speed sensor never fails. That is the safety responsibility of the item that provides it.

## Operational Situations: HARA's Scenario Library

With the item defined, the next step is to identify operational situations. They are the basis for judging E (exposure) and C (controllability): without a scenario, you cannot say under what circumstances a hazard occurs, how likely it is, or whether the driver can control it.

Operational situations should at least consider:

**Road type:** highway, city road, rural road, mountain road, tunnel, bridge.

**Road surface:** dry, standing water, snow, ice, gravel, potholes.

**Weather:** clear, rain, snow, fog, night, strong low sun.

**Driving maneuver:** straight, turning, lane change, overtaking, braking, accelerating, reversing, hill start.

**Traffic:** congested, free-flowing, oncoming traffic or none, pedestrians/cyclists or none, construction zone.

**Vehicle state:** fully loaded, empty, towing, low fuel, low tire pressure, warning light on.

**Driver state:** normal, tired, distracted, novice, elderly.

You don't need to lose sleep over the astronomical number of combinations. That's a common beginner mistake in HARA. What you need is to **identify the situations that qualitatively change the S/E/C parameters**.

For example: "highway, 120 km/h, straight" and "parking garage, 5 km/h, turning" are fundamentally different. Exposure and controllability are worlds apart. "Clear, dry highway" and "rainy, slippery highway" differ too: controllability drops sharply in rain. But "dry highway at 22 °C" and "dry highway at 23 °C" differ in no meaningful way. Don't agonize over that kind of micro-variation.

## Hazard Identification: Translating Malfunctioning Behavior into "What's Wrong with the Car"

With the scenario library in hand, now identify hazards systematically. This is the part of HARA that demands the most imagination. You have to reason from "what fault did some system function have" to "what harm could that do to this car and the people around it".

Engineering practice often uses a set of guide words (from HAZOP analysis) to work through malfunctioning behavior. ISO 26262 requires systematic analysis of malfunctioning behavior, but does not mandate a particular set of guide words. This set is the most common in the industry:

| Guide word | Meaning | EPS example |
|---|---|---|
| **Loss of Function** | The function that should be there is completely gone | Steering assist disappears entirely; the wheel becomes extremely heavy |
| **Unintended Activation** | A function that should not be active starts by itself | The driver's hands are on the wheel, and the EPS applies torque to the left by itself |
| **Incorrect — too high** | The output is larger than intended | The driver turns the wheel slightly, and the EPS applies maximum assist |
| **Incorrect — too low** | The output is smaller than intended | The driver turns hard, and the EPS gives very little assist |
| **Incorrect — opposite direction** | The output direction is opposite to intent | The driver wants to turn left, and the EPS applies torque to the right |
| **Function Stuck** | The output freezes at some value | The EPS holds a fixed assist torque and cannot respond to changes in driver input |
| **Incorrect — delayed** | The output timing is wrong | The driver turns the wheel, and the EPS responds 200 ms later |

With these guide words you can systematically check the potential malfunctioning behavior of every item function in every operating mode.

## Parameter S (Severity): How Fatal Is This Hazard?

Parameter S assesses: **if this hazardous event happens, how severe is the injury it could cause to the driver, passengers, and other road users?**

ISO 26262-3 gives precise levels:

| S level | Description | Core wording from the standard | Immune analogy |
|---|---|---|---|
| **S0** | No injuries | No injuries | Contact with a non-pathogenic bacterium: nothing happens at all |
| **S1** | Light and moderate injuries | Light and moderate injuries | A common cold: uncomfortable but not life-threatening |
| **S2** | Severe and life-threatening injuries (survival probable) | Severe and life-threatening injuries (survival probable) | Severe pneumonia: ICU needed, but survival is likely |
| **S3** | Life-threatening injuries (survival uncertain), fatal injuries | Life-threatening injuries (survival uncertain), fatal injuries | Ebola hemorrhagic fever or rabies: high mortality, survival uncertain |

Parameter S is set by the physical injury potential of the hazard itself and **does not consider safety mechanisms**. You assess severity under the premise that "the hazardous event has already happened". You cannot say "our system has safety mechanism XX, so severity drops to S1". S assesses the **potential for injury**, not how much risk your safety mechanism removes. Whether a safety mechanism can reduce risk is a question for the safety concept design later.

Examples, back to our EPS:

- At low speed in a straight line, the EPS loses assist (the wheel gets heavy): S0. The driver can still steer with effort at low speed; at worst, sore muscles.
- Unintended activation producing a slight steering pulse: S1. It may cause mild muscle strain or a slight drift, but no crash.
- Unintended activation producing a moderate lateral deviation at highway speed: S2. The car leaves its lane and hits an oncoming vehicle or the guardrail; the injury may be life-threatening, but a belted occupant will probably survive.
- Unintended activation producing a large opposing torque in a fast bend in the rain: S3. Loss of control, rollover, or a high-speed crash; survival is uncertain.

S3 is often called "the S3 bar". Once your hazard assessment reaches S3, it does not become S2 because the occupant is belted. The S3 criterion is: **even with reasonable passive safety (seat belts, airbags), the injury can still be life-threatening with uncertain survival**.

## Parameter E (Exposure): How Often Does This Dangerous Situation Occur?

Parameter E assesses **the share of average driving time spent in the operational situation, or how often it occurs.**

ISO 26262-3 gives precise definitions:

| E level | Description (frequency-based; see the informative annex on exposure) | Wording from the standard |
|------|------|------|
| **E0** | Incredible | Extremely rare; almost never happens |
| **E1** | Very low probability | Less than once per year (for each driver of that model) |
| **E2** | Low probability | A few times per year |
| **E3** | Medium probability | Once a month or more |
| **E4** | High probability | Happens on almost every drive |

> The normative table gives only the verbal descriptions for E0–E4 (Incredible / Very low / Low / Medium / High). The frequency figures above come from the informative annex on exposure. The time-share references for E2/E3/E4 (< 1% / 1–10% / > 10%) apply to specific vehicle classes; here we use the more general frequency descriptions.

Parameter E assesses **how often the operational situation occurs**, not the probability of the hazard. "Highway driving" is an operational situation; E assesses "the share of average driving time spent on the highway", not "the probability that the EPS fails on the highway".

Typical references:
- Highway driving (> 100 km/h): usually 10%–30% of driving time → **E4**
- Mountain roads: depends on the region, perhaps 1%–5% → **E2 or E3**
- Mountain road at night in rain: < 1% → **E2**
- Servicing on a lift: a few tens of minutes per service, far below 1% → **E1** (but if you are the mechanic, the exposure during service cannot be ignored)

## Parameter C (Controllability): Can the Driver Avoid the Harm?

Parameter C assesses: **when the hazardous event occurs, how far can the driver or other people at risk control the vehicle and avoid the specific harm?**

This is the hardest of the three to assess. You need to consider:

- the time the driver takes to notice the anomaly
- the time to judge it correctly
- the time to take corrective action
- the vehicle's response time to the correction
- whether all of the above can be completed inside the available time window

The C levels in ISO 26262-3:

| C level | Description | Exact meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **C0** | Controllable in general | An ordinary driver can usually control it; it isn't even a notable anomaly |
| **C1** | Simply controllable | More than 99% of drivers can avoid the harm |
| **C2** | Normally controllable | 90% to 99% of ordinary drivers can usually control the harm |
| **C3** | Difficult to control or uncontrollable | An ordinary driver usually cannot, or can hardly, control the harm |

Two common traps in controllability assessment:

**Trap 1: overestimating the driver.** Most drivers are not test drivers. Their reaction time falls in the 0.6–1.5 s range (that's the data from a large body of experimental research, not your intuition). In a typical "unexpected lateral deviation" event, the driver must notice the car drifting (0.3–0.8 s) → understand what is happening (0.2–0.5 s) → decide how to correct (0.2–0.5 s) → execute the correction (0.3–1.0 s) → the vehicle responds (0.1–0.3 s). Even in the best case, the total from fault to correction is close to 1 s. At 120 km/h, 1 s covers 33 m. That means the car may already be half a lane off.

**Trap 2: ignoring that the malfunctioning behavior can itself disturb the driver.** If the EPS applies torque to the left unexpectedly, the first thing the driver feels is "the wheel is moving on its own". That is a distraction and a scare in itself. The driver may first fight the abnormal force on the wheel (which adds reaction delay), and only then realize that braking or correcting is needed. This startle effect must be considered in the controllability assessment.

Back to the EPS steering angle deviation example:
- Parking garage at 5 km/h: plenty of time, low speed, enclosed environment → **C1**
- Highway at 120 km/h, straight: quick recognition needed, narrow correction window, high speed and severe consequences → **C2**
- Rainy bend at 120 km/h: road feedback interferes with recognition, the correction window is extremely short, and the slippery surface reduces vehicle response → **C3**

## ASIL Determination: S + E + C → Safety Level

With S, E, and C in hand, you look up the result in the ASIL determination table in ISO 26262-3.

A few representative combinations (based on your reference data):

| S | E | C | ASIL | One-line explanation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| S1 | E4 | C3 | **ASIL B** | Minor injury, but frequent and hard to control: substantial safety measures required |
| S2 | E4 | C3 | **ASIL C** | Serious injury + frequent + hard to control: very strict development requirements |
| S3 | E4 | C3 | **ASIL D** | Fatal + frequent + hard to control: the highest safety integrity level |
| S3 | E1 | C1 | **QM** | Fatal, but extremely rare and easy to control: the quality process is enough |
| S1 | E1 | C1 | **QM** | Minor, rare, easy to control: no ASIL needed, quality management suffices |

Note: S3 + E4 + C3 = ASIL D is the "ceiling combination": the most severe injury potential, in the most common scenario, extremely hard to control. Once your HARA lands on this combination, you are in functional safety's "hell mode": every development activity must meet the strictest ASIL D requirements.

## Full Worked Case: HARA for an EPS Steering Angle Deviation

Let's walk the whole HARA once, so it sticks in your memory.

**Item:** the electric power steering system (EPS), including the torque sensor, EPS ECU, drive motor, and steering rack.

**Function:** provide assist steering torque based on the torque the driver applies to the wheel.

**Malfunctioning behavior:** the EPS unintentionally applies a continuous steering torque above 100 N, so the wheels turn left with no driver input.

How does this behavior come out of the guide words?
- Using "Unintended Activation": the EPS assist function activates itself without any driver torque input.
- Combined with "Incorrect — too high": even a self-activation is far more harmful if the torque value is abnormally large (say, torque data on CAN flipped by an alpha particle).

**Hazard:** the vehicle develops lateral deviation the driver does not expect, which can lead to leaving the lane or colliding with another vehicle or a roadside obstacle.

**Note:** this is a vehicle-level hazard definition. It does not mention "CAN communication", "torque sensor", "MCU", or any other implementation detail.

**Operational situation:** highway, 120 km/h, rain, bend (radius 500 m), lane width 3.5 m, oncoming traffic on the left.

**Parameter S assessment:**

A sudden large lateral deviation at 120 km/h on a highway: the vehicle may leave its lane and hit an oncoming vehicle head-on, or hit the roadside guardrail at speed. Even with seat belts worn and airbags deploying correctly, the crash energy and a possible rollover mean the injury can be life-threatening with uncertain survival. Under the ISO 26262 severity levels, this is **rated S3** (life-threatening injuries, survival uncertain).

**Parameter E assessment:**

Highway driving is a major part of daily driving for Chinese car owners. According to transport-industry statistics, highway driving usually accounts for more than 10% of total driving time, especially for users who commute between cities often. Rain reduces the share somewhat, but "highway + bend" is still a significant slice of time. Under the E levels, more than 10% of driving time → **E4** (high probability).

**Parameter C assessment:**

This is a very challenging controllability judgment. Consider:

1. The unexpected lateral deviation itself startles the driver, so the initial reaction time may be longer than normal.
2. The driver has to work out the relationship between "the wheel is turning itself" and "the car is drifting". The cognitive load is high.
3. Wet-road friction is low (μ ≈ 0.4–0.7 versus μ ≈ 0.9 on dry asphalt), so corrections are less effective.
4. At 120 km/h the car covers 3.3 m every 100 ms. The correction window is extremely narrow.
5. Visibility is limited in a bend, and if a car is oncoming, the correction itself (the avoidance maneuver can cause a tail slide) adds uncertainty.
6. Research on similar "sudden lateral deviation" scenarios shows that only about 50%–70% of ordinary drivers can make an effective enough correction inside the safe time window.

Reasoning from this driving-behavior data, **rate it C3** (difficult to control or uncontrollable; an ordinary driver usually cannot, or can hardly, control the harm. In engineering, the quantitative reference line is usually that fewer than 90% of drivers can avoid the harm).

**ASIL determination:** S3 + E4 + C3 → **ASIL D**.

**Safety goal:** prevent lateral vehicle deviation that the driver does not expect, caused by unintended activation of EPS steering assist.

Note that the safety goal is still at the functional level: it mentions no redundant torque sensing, no CAN protection, no motor drive monitoring. Those technical solutions are derived in the next step, the functional safety concept (the corresponding clause in Part 3).

## More HARA Scenarios (to Build Intuition for S/E/C)

**Scenario 2:** same item, but the situation becomes "city parking garage, 5 km/h, dry floor, EPS assist suddenly disappears while parking (loss of function)".
- S = S0: at low speed the driver can still turn the wheel; it takes more effort but causes no injury.
- E = E2: parking maneuvers account for < 1% of driving time.
- C = C1: at low speed the wheel just gets heavier; the vast majority of drivers can finish the maneuver with more force.
- Result: **QM**. That's why loss of steering assist at low speed is usually not treated as safety-related.

**Scenario 3:** same item, parked at a highway service area. While the driver is out checking the vehicle, the EPS mistakenly starts the steering motor, the wheel spins fast, and the driver's arm is caught.
- S = S1 or S2 (depending on the force and where the arm is caught, but generally not life-threatening the way a driving crash is).
- E = E1: this happens extremely rarely.
- C = C2 or C3: the wheel spinning is hard to predict, and the driver may not get out of the way in time.
- Result: **QM** (in the determination table, for S1 and S2 on the E1 row, all three cells C1, C2, and C3 are QM).

Note that scenarios 2 and 3 rest on different ratings: scenario 2 lands on S0, scenario 3 lands on E1. **The same functional failure of the same item must be taken through S, E, and C again in each scenario.** That's why HARA has to be done scenario by scenario, and ASIL cannot be assigned "per functional module".

## The Full Immune Mapping: HARA = An Epidemiological Investigation

Let's map HARA onto the book's immune-system metaphor.

**Item definition = host profile.** When epidemiologists investigate an outbreak, they first establish "who is the host": a child or an adult? What underlying conditions? What immune status? In HARA, you establish what the item is, where its boundary is, and what its operating modes are.

**Hazard identification = pathogen identification.** What is this pathogen? By what route does it enter the body? What symptoms can it cause? In HARA, you use guide words to work through the possible failure behavior of each function and identify what harm it could do to the vehicle and to people.

**Operational situation = transmission environment.** A virus spreads easily in a crowded indoor space and poorly outdoors with good ventilation. In HARA, the same hazard has completely different consequences in a high-speed bend and in a stationary parking garage.

**Parameter S = pathogen virulence.** Ebola's virulence (S3) far exceeds that of a common cold (S1). In HARA, the injury potential of an unexpected high-speed lateral deviation (S3) far exceeds that of losing assist at low speed (S0).

**Parameter E = exposure frequency.** You face influenza every year of your life (E4), but you may never face Marburg virus (E1). In HARA, you are on the highway almost every drive (E4), but few people drive mountain roads at night every day (E1).

**Parameter C = population immunity and access to intervention.** If you catch a virus, can your immune system control it? Is there a specific drug? If it is easily controlled (C1), the risk is low. If it can hardly be controlled (C3), the risk is high. In HARA: can the driver correct the unexpected vehicle behavior within the FTTI?

**Safety goal = the objective of the immune strategy.** The immune strategy is not which vaccine or which drug you use. That's the "immune plan" (the functional safety concept). The immune strategy is top-level: "prevent a viral infection from causing fatal disease". In HARA, the safety goal is "prevent unexpected lateral deviation caused by unintended EPS activation": a top-level objective, with the method left open.

## Common HARA Mistakes


**Mistake 1: writing a system fault as the hazard.** "Loss of CAN communication" is not a hazard. "Torque sensor output drift" is not a hazard. Those are malfunctioning behavior. A hazard must be "what dangerous behavior the vehicle develops": "loss of vehicle braking", "unintended vehicle acceleration", "lateral vehicle deviation".

**Mistake 2: downgrading S because of safety mechanisms.** "Because we added redundant safety mechanisms, S can be lowered." Wrong. S assesses severity under the assumption that the harm has already happened. Your safety mechanism cannot change the physical outcome of a crash. It can only lower the probability that the harm occurs. That lower probability shows up in the probabilistic metric for random hardware failures (PMHF), not in S.

**Mistake 3: substituting design intent for scenario analysis.** "This is driver assistance, not automated driving, so we assume the driver is always monitoring." In HARA you cannot substitute "design intent" for "the scenario after a real failure". Suppose the LKA (lane keeping assist) outputs a strong unintended lateral torque just when the driver's hands are not firmly on the wheel. Your HARA must consider that scenario, whatever the design intent.

**Mistake 4: saying "our test engineers can control it" when assessing C.** C is about the "ordinary driver", not an engineering test driver and not a professional racer. You must use typical driver behavior data, including reaction time, cognitive load, and physical limits.

**Mistake 5: confusing E with failure rate (FIT).** E is the exposure probability of a situation, not the probability of a hardware failure. Even if your MCU's FIT is 0.001 (extremely reliable), your highway exposure is still E4. The highway is your operational situation, not your failure mode.

## HARA Work Products

ISO 26262-3 requires HARA to produce a formal work product: the HARA report. It should contain at least:

- the item definition document (function, boundary, interfaces, operating modes)
- the list of operational situations (descriptions and rationale for selection)
- the malfunctioning behavior analysis for each identified function
- the S/E/C assessment for each hazardous event, with rationale
- the ASIL determination table
- the safety goal list
- references for all assessment evidence (data sources, research citations, past accident data)

This report is the foundation for every safety activity that follows. If HARA misses a hazard scenario, then the whole safety concept, the technical safety requirements, the hardware metrics, and the software architecture that follow will contain no safeguard for that scenario. That is the most serious kind of systematic failure in functional safety.

## Summary

1. The item definition sets HARA's boundary: too narrow and you miss hazards, too wide and the analysis sprawls
2. Operational situations are the basis for parameters E and C
3. Seven malfunctioning-behavior guide words help you identify hazards systematically
4. A hazard must be defined at the vehicle level
5. Parameter S (S0–S3): injury severity, not downgraded by safety mechanisms
6. Parameter E (E0–E4): how often the situation occurs, not failure probability
7. Parameter C (C0–C3): whether the driver can avoid the harm, and it must rest on behavioral data
8. S + E + C → ASIL (QM to ASIL D)
9. Full EPS case: S3 + E4 + C3 = ASIL D
