# 1.6 Thought Experiment: You Are the Safety Engineer for an ASIL D ECU

## Your Assignment

You sit in the office of a Tier-1 automotive supplier. On the monitor beside your coffee cup are an AUTOSAR project, a MISRA C checker report, and an FMEDA spreadsheet.

In front of you is a first-spin prototype steering ECU, just back from the board house: an Infineon AURIX TC3xx, TriCore lockstep dual core at 300 MHz, 2 MB of on-chip Flash, 1.4 MB of SRAM with ECC. The operating system is AUTOSAR OS (OSEK-compatible, preemptive scheduling). Development cycle: 18 months.

Your assignment: **prove that the steering assist control software on this ECU will not, because of a hardware fault or a software design defect, cause the steering wheel to lock or move wrongly while driving, leaving the car unable to steer and causing a collision.**

Your safety manager has already completed the HARA. The conclusion is ASIL D. You must deliver a safety case that a third-party certification body, such as TÜV SÜD, will sign.

The first five sections of this chapter laid five foundations: the immune system's defense in depth, Hilbert's formalist dream, Therac-25's software independence, Turing's halting problem, and the physical dice of alpha particles. Now you need to use them to **construct a safety argument.** Below is your complete reasoning chain, starting from those five foundations.

## Argument Layer 1: Physics — You Can't Withstand Cosmic Rays, but You Can Detect Them

In section 1.5 you learned that alpha particles are not science fiction. They are the inevitable result of trace isotope decay in packaging resin. On the 1.4 MB SRAM of the AURIX TC3xx in front of you, over 20 years and across the full temperature range, SEUs will certainly happen multiple times.

You do not need to prevent them. What you need is: **to know when one happens.**

Answer 1: SRAM — ECC. Your MCU's SRAM controller checks 7 ECC bits on every 32-bit read. A single-bit flip is corrected. A double-bit flip is detected as an uncorrectable error and raises an alarm. Your safety argument's conclusion at this point: SRAM is known to suffer random bit flips, but the probability that these flips are detected or corrected by SECDED ECC is ≥ 99.9% (based on FMEDA data provided by the MCU supplier).

Answer 2: Flash — ECC plus redundancy. You do not store calibration data in one Flash block; you store two copies. Each copy has its own ECC. You compare the two when reading. The probability that an alpha particle hits both copies at once is extremely low, and the common-cause failure probability has been calculated and meets the ASIL D PMHF requirement.

Answer 3: Lockstep cores — comparator. Your two lockstep TriCore cores are compared by a hardware comparator every clock cycle. If either core is struck by an alpha particle, the comparator immediately sees a mismatch and sends an alarm signal to the SMU (safety management unit). The SMU triggers the fault response within tens of microseconds and degrades to the safe state.

At the "physics layer," your safety argument is not "zero faults." It is: **faults are physically unavoidable, but every foreseeable physical failure mode has a corresponding detection or correction mechanism.**

## Argument Layer 2: Mathematics — You Can't Prove Everything, but You Can Constrain Everything

In sections 1.2 and 1.4 you learned that Hilbert's dream was shattered by Gödel, that Turing's halting problem declared general safety decisions impossible, and that Rice's theorem declared arbitrary program analysis impossible.

You will not claim your code is "perfect." You will say: **"under the given constraint set, static analysis tools can verify that the code satisfies these constraints."**

Answer 1: MISRA C constraints. Your code passes this set of MISRA C:2012 rules: no recursion (Rule 17.2), no dynamic memory allocation (Rule 21.3), no goto (Rule 15.1), and so on. These rules are not there to make your life harder. They drag your code out of the region "static analysis tools cannot cover" and into the region "that can be covered." You must state clearly in the safety case: the language subset of the code is not all of C99. It is the MISRA C subset.

Answer 2: AUTOSAR OS constraints. Your steering control task has the highest OS priority and cannot be preempted (ceiling priority protocol). Its WCET (worst-case execution time), computed as an upper bound by a static analysis tool plus a 20% safety margin, is still less than the 5 ms deadline. In a 10 ms cycle, the task has at least 5 ms available. That means even with 5 ms of bus transmission delay, the steering control command can still go out before the deadline.

Answer 3: Bounded halting, the watchdog. You do not prove the task will not run away. You kick WdgM at the end of the task. WdgM is configured with alive supervision, expecting at least one checkpoint every 10 ms. If three consecutive cycles pass without a kick (40 ms total timeout), WdgM triggers a global alarm and the ECU enters the safe state. **You do not need to prove the task will not loop forever. You only need to prove that the consequence of a loop is a reset within 40 ms.**

At the "mathematics layer," your safety argument is not "program behavior is sufficiently correct." It is: **unknown defects may exist in the program's non-safety-related behavior, but every known path that could lead to unsafe behavior has been constrained, guarded, or checked.**

## Argument Layer 3: Architecture — Software Cannot Carry What Hardware Must

In section 1.3 you learned that the Therac-25's key failure was not the software race bug. It was the removal of the hardware interlock. Software cannot carry safety by itself. There must be a mechanism independent of the software to back it up.

Answer 1: Independent watchdog. Your watchdog is not "a timer ISR on the main core." That is a software watchdog, and it suffers from the self-reference paradox: who watches the watcher? You use the MCU's built-in **independent hardware watchdog**, which has a clock source independent of the CPU (an internal RC oscillator). Even if all software on the main CPU crashes, the watchdog still works. The watchdog is also tied to the DIC (fault injection channel), and software cannot turn it off by writing a register. **This is the hardware-level answer to Gödel: use "another system" to break self-reference.**

Answer 2: MPU isolation. In your AUTOSAR OS, the memory permissions of the steering control task are enforced directly by MPU hardware. The access rights for this region are stored in the MPU configuration registers, loaded at CPU startup by the HSM (hardware security module), and cannot be modified at runtime. If the steering control task writes into an adjacent region because of a stack overflow, the MPU raises a hardware exception within the bus cycle, the SMU catches it, and the safe state is triggered. **The check is not a software `if`. The check is hardware arbitration on the bus. Physical, not logical.**

Answer 3: E2E end-to-end protection. Your steering-angle sensor data travels from the sensor chip → SPI → MCU → software processing → CAN transmission → steering actuator ECU. Every message along the entire chain carries a CRC, a counter, and a DataID. The E2E protection library at the receiver (`E2E_P04` or `E2E_P05`, which you will see in the Part 4 source walkthrough) verifies message integrity and order. If the CRC fails or the counter is out of sequence, the frame is discarded, and the system uses the last valid frame or enters the safe state. **The data and its integrity tag travel together, so the probability of forgery or corruption is pushed beyond what the hardware CRC can match.**

At the "architecture layer," your safety argument is not "the software remembers everything." It is: **every safety-critical output of the software is checked by a hardware mechanism independent of that software logic. The MPU is enforcing, the watchdog is counting down, E2E is verifying the signature.**

**One level deeper: HSI, the "no-fighting pact" between hardware and software.** At the architecture layer there is one more thing that runs through your daily work: HSI (hardware-software interface). This is not an interface in the sense of "what is the SPI baud rate." It is **the interface that defines "what safety consequence follows if hardware and software misunderstand each other's state."**

Your steering ECU has dozens of HSI elements: how the ADC's torque-sensor reading maps from a 12-bit register to uint16_t; how many cycles after writing the PWM duty register at a certain address the value actually takes effect; how many cycles after an interrupt fires the ISR starts executing; whether the CPU can fetch code from the same Flash block while it is being erased. Each one is a potential "misunderstanding point." Think of the hardware-software interaction as a conversation. Your C code says "set the PWM duty cycle to 60%" and writes a value to address 0x4000_0C00. But the hardware does not necessarily "obey." If the PWM module's clock is not yet enabled, the write has no effect. If the PWM prescaler is configured wrong, 60% corresponds not to 7200 rpm but to 12000 rpm. Your C code said one thing, and the hardware heard another. That is an HSI fault.

The HSI specification must cover more than "the bit-field definitions of the registers" (those are already in the chip manual). It must cover **"the implicit contract between hardware and software about timing, state, and permissions."** For example: your steering control task, in its 10 ms cycle, must read the torque sensor value → compute the target torque → write the PWM duty cycle. These three steps have strict timing dependencies (ADC conversion complete → DMA transfer complete → data-ready interrupt fires → task reads). But if the DMA priority is configured lower than the CPU instruction-fetch priority, the task may read stale data before the DMA has finished. The chip manual's register definitions will not tell you "the DMA priority must be higher than the CPU instruction-fetch bus priority," but your HSI analysis must point this out and provide design verification.

Another example: a block of calibration data in Flash is read by the steering control task and also by the BMU (boot management unit). The MPU configures this region as "read-only," but when the BMU performs Flash programming, it briefly switches the Flash controller for that region to an "unreadable" state. Between the BMU's programming window and the steering task's read cycle there is a brief HSI conflict. During that window, the read returns an indeterminate state of all 0xFF, and your control algorithm interprets 0xFFFF as "the steering wheel is at the extreme angle" and outputs a wrong motor torque. HSI analysis makes these "implicit contracts" explicit: write them down one by one, review them, and verify them by test. ISO 26262-4 and ISO 26262-6 both require the HSI document to be formally reviewed as part of the safety case. TÜV assessors usually pay special attention to HSI, because this is where the hardware team and the software team most easily blame each other. The hardware engineer thinks the interface is in the chip manual and software should read it. The software engineer thinks hardware should guarantee that a write takes effect, and if it doesn't, it's the hardware team's fault. The HSI document breaks this buck-passing: write every expectation on the same sheet of paper, and both sides sign it. Whoever fails to meet it fixes it.

## Argument Layer 4: Data — Compressing Nondeterminism into Statistical Trust

At the end of section 1.5 you saw PMHF: 10 FIT. In the FMEDA spreadsheet in front of you, the PMHF calculation for your steering ECU comes out at 7.3 FIT, just meeting the ASIL D target of < 10 FIT. But this number is a statistical projection, not a physical test. How do you argue that it is "credible"?

In the safety case you must do two things: **self-proof and independent proof.** Self-proof: using the MCU vendor's FMEDA data, the failure-rate data for all peripheral chips, and your analysis of safety-mechanism coverage, you derive the 7.3 FIT yourself. Independent proof: you hand this FMEDA calculation to an independent third-party assessor (usually an assessor appointed by the safety certification body), who independently verifies your assumptions, your calculations, and the vendor data you cite.

This is the essence of why PMHF is not absolute truth: it is a **statistical confidence interval**, based on known hardware failure-rate data and conservative fault-coverage estimates. You and the certification body agree on one thing: the residual failure risk is low enough to be below "a socially acceptable level of safety."

## FTTI: How Many Milliseconds from Fault to Crash

One number in the safety argument is easy to leave blank: FTTI (fault tolerant time interval). This concept answers a life-or-death question directly: **from the instant the fault occurs, how much time do you have to rescue the situation? You must bring the system into the safe state within this window, or the hazard occurs.**

FTTI is not like MCU clock speed or SRAM size. It is not a number you look up in the chip manual. It comes from vehicle-level hazard analysis and operating-scenario analysis.

Take your steering ECU. Scenario: highway, 120 km/h, the driver is making small steering corrections to stay in lane. T = 0 ms: an alpha particle hits SRAM, and the calibration value for the steering motor torque offset flips from +2.3° to +227.3°. This bad value is still latent; it has not been read yet.

But the steering wheel cannot wait long. A modern EPS (electric power steering) system has a control period of 1–2 ms. Every 1 ms, the torque sensor reads the driver's hand force, the steering control task computes the target assist, and the PWM updates the motor current. T = 1 ms: your task reads the bad calibration value on its next scheduling, computes a wrong assist torque of 40 N·m, and the motor starts applying extra torque. The driver feels the wheel "suddenly turn by itself." This is called **unintended steering torque**.

How quickly does that push the car out of its lane? It depends on vehicle speed and the steering angle offset. At 120 km/h, if a sustained torque equivalent to about 15° of steering angle is applied to the wheel, the car will drift one third of a lane width in about 400–500 ms. Beyond that offset, even if the driver reacts immediately to correct it, the remaining reaction and correction time is not enough to bring the car back into the lane before a collision. The hazard occurs.

So your FTTI is about 400 ms. That is: from the instant the alpha particle hits SRAM to the instant the driver crashes because of the abnormal steering deflection, there is a window of about 400 ms. **All your safety mechanisms — ECC detection, lockstep comparison, SMU alarm, and PWM output shutdown — must complete the entire process of fault detection, decision, and entry into the safe state within those 400 ms.**

Now use this FTTI to check the response-time chain of your safety mechanisms: ECC checks on every SRAM read cycle, with about 0 µs latency (synchronous detection). The lockstep comparator compares every clock cycle (at a 300 MHz clock, once every 3.3 ns), with about 3.3 ns latency. After detecting a mismatch → the hardware sends an alarm signal to the SMU over a hardwire, about 0.5 µs. The SMU receives the alarm, decides that the safe state must be triggered, and executes the fault response logic, about 10 µs. The SMU cuts the PWM output and releases the enable signal of the motor pre-driver chip, about 50 µs. Total response time: about 60 µs.

You complete the entire response in the first 0.015% of the FTTI's 400 ms window. You have nearly four orders of magnitude of safety time margin: the safety mechanism responds in microseconds, while the FTTI is in milliseconds. This margin is not a luxury. It is the source of confidence in the argument. It means that even if some link in the response chain suffers an unexpected delay (say the SMU processor takes an extra 20 µs because of task multiplexing), your total response time is still far below the FTTI. Your safety argument does not need to demand "zero latency." It only needs to demand "total response time less than the FTTI, and a conservative upper bound on the maximum delay of every link."

This is the place of FTTI analysis in the safety argument: **it is not just a checkmark on paper saying "response < FTTI." It turns your whole safety-mechanism response chain from "fast" into "fast enough under a given time constraint, and fast with margin at every stage."**

## Argument Layer 5: Process — Not One Signature, but Proof of a Safety Culture

You now have four layers of argument — physics, mathematics, architecture, data — plus the FTTI time constraint. But when the TÜV assessor opens your safety case, they look not only at the content of these arguments. **They also look at who made them, how they were made, who independently checked them, and what the check records show.** This is the fifth layer of the safety argument: process.

The process layer starts with ISO 26262-2, "functional safety management." At first glance this part may seem boring: "safety plan," "confirmation review," "verification review," "independence requirements." It looks like meeting minutes written for project managers and has nothing directly to do with "will the steering wheel lock?" But peel back that shell, and the process layer answers a fundamental question: **why should I believe your argument is not something you made up alone in a room?**

The answer lies in three institutional designs:

**First institution: joint signing of the safety plan.** The safety plan is not something "one person writes and that's it." It must be signed by the project safety manager, the functional safety manager, the development lead, and the test lead. Each signature does not mean "I have read it." It means "I bear management responsibility for the implementation of the part I own." If the development lead signs a commitment to "MC/DC coverage ≥ 98%" while the project manager signs an agreed SOP schedule, then when late in development MC/DC can only reach 93%, the problem is not "why didn't the test team do better." It is a conflict between two signing roles: schedule and coverage cannot both be met, and the issue must be escalated. The essence of joint signing is to make potentially contradictory management commitments explicit, so the conflict is exposed at the signing table instead of exploding a week before SOP.

**Second institution: confirmation review.** The safety plan, HARA report, safety concept, and safety case — these four key deliverables must undergo independent confirmation review. The reviewer cannot be anyone who participated in the work. For ASIL D, the reviewer must reach **the highest independence level, I3**: independent of the department responsible for the work product in management, resources, and release authority. In organizational terms: the confirmation reviewer must come from an independent reporting line, such as an independent assessment team reporting directly to the company-level safety director, not constrained by the project department's resources and release authority. This design lets the reviewer challenge any project-level decision without worrying that it will affect their performance review.

**Third institution: the progression of independence levels (I0/I1/I2/I3).** ISO 26262-2 defines four independence levels: I0 = the confirmation measure should be performed, and if performed, the reviewer should be different from the author of the work product; I1 = the confirmation measure shall be performed, and the reviewer shall be different from the author of the work product (may be in the same team); I2 = the reviewer shall be independent of the team responsible for the work product, meaning they do not report to the same direct manager; I3 = the reviewer shall be independent of the department responsible for the work product in management, resources, and release authority. The ASIL D HARA confirmation review requires I3, which means every rating of severity, exposure, and controllability must be reexamined by a department completely independent of yours. They can say: "You think the exposure of this scenario is E3. We think it should be E4, because 20% of this model's customer base regularly drives in bad weather. The exposure of this scenario is higher than you assumed, and the corresponding ASIL should go up one level."

Together these three institutions form a **closed loop of safety culture: multi-party confirmation, independent verification, and separation of interests.** They are not there to make trouble for engineers. They answer an underlying truth: a functional safety argument is not a mathematical proof. It cannot be derived by one person, in one office, with paper and pen. A single person will always overestimate their own judgment; cognitive science has confirmed this again and again. A safety argument needs **the support of organizational behavior: multiple roles, different perspectives, independent interests, all examining the same set of arguments together, to bring one person's judgment bias down to an engineering-acceptable level.**

When the TÜV assessor signs their name on the last page of your safety case, they are not signing "this ECU is 100% safe." They are signing: **"under the constraints of this development process, all reasonable safety arguments have been made, recorded, and independently confirmed. To the best of my knowledge, nothing has been omitted."** The last link in your safety argument is not technical. It is the system that guarantees the completeness and honesty of the technical argument.

## The Safety Case: Is Your Argument Hard Enough?

The final step: deliver the safety case. When the TÜV assessor opens it, what they want to see is not whether your code runs correctly, but **whether your chain of argument is complete.**

1. HARA: your ASIL D determination has reasonable severity, exposure, and controllability ratings.
2. Safety goal: your safety goal is not "the steering wheel must turn correctly" but "the steering wheel must not lock with more than 100 N of steering resistance while driving."
3. Technical safety requirements: every safety goal is decomposed into verifiable technical requirements. Every output of the lockstep comparator, every region permission setting of the MPU, every timeout window configuration of the watchdog can be traced to at least one safety goal.
4. Test reports: unit tests, integration tests, safety requirement verification. Every ASIL D safety requirement is verified by at least one test case. MC/DC coverage requires that every independent influence of every condition's every truth value in all safety code be covered by at least one test case.
5. FMEDA report: hardware single-point faults, residual faults, and latent multiple-point faults all have analyzed coverage.

If any link in your case is broken — for example, a safety requirement has no corresponding test case in the test report — the TÜV assessor will mark it "to be supplemented," and your SOP date slips.

The safety case is not "proof that the software is perfect." It is **a complete, self-consistent, traceable chain of argument: from HARA's "this scenario could kill someone" to the test report's "this scenario has been covered."** There is no magic in the chain. Every item is something engineering can do, test, and audit.

## Closing

This chapter started from 1.5 billion years of immune-system evolution, went through Hilbert's formalist dream, the three lives lost to Therac-25, the self-reference paradox of Turing's halting problem, and the physical track of an alpha particle crossing an SRAM cell. These seemingly unrelated pieces of knowledge are woven into one complete chain inside the safety argument for a single steering-control ECU.

The philosophical foundation of functional safety is: perfect is unattainable, good enough is attainable. This is not a sigh of resignation. It is clear-headed engineering wisdom. Hilbert and Gödel tell you "some things can never be proved." But your lockstep comparator does not deal in proof. It deals in cycle-level hardware comparison. Your watchdog does not deal in proof. It deals in timeout reset. **Engineering's answer is not mathematical completeness. It is a physical backup.**

## Summary

1. Argument layer 1 (physics): alpha particles and bit flips are unavoidable. ECC, lockstep, and redundancy detect them. They do not prevent the event, but they detect and respond when it happens.
2. Argument layer 2 (mathematics): perfect analysis is impossible. MISRA C constrains the space, AUTOSAR OS constrains time, and the watchdog compresses undecidable questions into bounded decisions.
3. Argument layer 3 (architecture): software does not carry what hardware must. The independent watchdog breaks the self-reference paradox, the MPU intercepts at the bus level, and E2E verifies integrity at the message level.
4. Argument layer 4 (data): PMHF is not an absolute guarantee. It is statistical confidence derived from FMEDA and reliability data.
5. The essence of the safety case: not proof of perfection, but a traceable argument chain from HARA to the test report.
