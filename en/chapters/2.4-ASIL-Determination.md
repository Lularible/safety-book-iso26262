# 2.4 ASIL Determination: From Three Parameters to Four Letters

The meeting room is hazy. Not with real smoke: with the hot air blowing from the projector's exhaust. The projector throws rows of an Excel sheet onto the white wall. You sit at the far left of the long table, staring at row 17. That row reads: highway scenario, unintended steering torque causes the vehicle to leave its lane. Your functional safety team has been arguing for fifteen minutes.

The argument is about one parameter: **controllability, C2 or C3?**

"The driver still has 3 seconds to react. Call it C2; most people can hold it." The systems engineer across from you says.

"Are you sure?" The safety manager puts down his coffee cup. "That's 130 km/h. S3 + E4 + C2 is ASIL C. S3 + E4 + C3 is ASIL D. Do you know how much the development cost differs between those two? About an order of magnitude."

The room goes quiet.

Your mind races. C2 means "normally controllable; most drivers can avoid the harm". C3 means "an ordinary driver usually cannot, or can hardly, control the harm". On a highway, when steering suddenly goes out of control, not every driver can react correctly within 3 seconds. And the instinctive reaction may be to slam on the brakes, which can make the loss of control worse.

You finally say: "It should be C3."

The safety manager nods. "ASIL D. We're done."


That's the truth about ASIL determination. It isn't a math problem. It's a **philosophical question answered with engineering judgment**. Behind every rating of every parameter sits a deep understanding of the word "safe". And the final letter, A, B, C, D, or QM, decides the fate of the whole development team.

## The Core Immune Metaphor: How Threat Levels Are Judged

Before we dig into the ASIL table, go back to the immune system. Every day your body faces millions of foreign agents: bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites. But the immune system does not treat every threat alike. It has an elegant **threat-level classification mechanism**:

- Common commensal bacteria on the skin surface? **No response needed.** That's a QM-level threat.
- A mild local infection? **Local inflammation**: macrophages and neutrophils are called in. That's ASIL A.
- A moderate infection with a risk of spreading? **Fever plus systemic inflammation**: the complement system is activated and cytokines are released. That's ASIL B.
- A severe infection that threatens organ function? **Full-body immune mobilization**: large-scale activation of T cells and B cells. That's ASIL C.
- A lethal pathogen breaks in, such as sepsis? **The highest level of immune storm**: resources committed at any cost, the entire immune system fighting on every front. That's ASIL D.

The immune system will not mount a systemic fever for an ordinary mosquito bite, and it will not send a few macrophages to deal with sepsis. It judges precisely: **how severe is the threat (S), how likely am I to be exposed (E), and can I control it before damage occurs (C)**.

Isn't that exactly S + E + C?

### Why Not S × E × C?

You may ask: how does the immune system combine S, E, and C? By multiplication? If that's your instinct, you are already trapped in the old "S × E × C = some number" frame. In ISO 26262, ASIL determination is **not multiplication**. It's a **table lookup**.

This is not arbitrary. The trouble with multiplication: some combinations inflate the risk rating (S1 × E4 × C3 and S3 × E1 × C3 come out to the same number, but their real risks are completely different), while others get underestimated. A lookup table forces a human expert to make an independent engineering judgment for **each concrete S + E + C combination**, instead of taking the lazy way out with a formula.

Your immune system also works by lookup, not multiplication. When a Toll-like receptor (TLR) recognizes a pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP), the signal it sends is not a single "S × E × C" intensity value. It activates **a specific immune pathway** according to **the specific pattern of that pathogen**. Endotoxin (LPS) from Gram-negative bacteria activates the TLR4 pathway, viral double-stranded RNA activates TLR3, bacterial flagellin activates TLR5. Each pathway's activation threshold, response strength, and regulation evolved independently. That's the immune system's lookup table.

The ISO 26262 ASIL determination table is, in essence, the functional safety field's pattern-recognition receptor.

## The ASIL Determination Table: From Three Parameters to Four Letters

The clause on hazard analysis and risk assessment in ISO 26262-3 defines the HARA process. Its most important step maps the three ratings S (severity), E (exposure), and C (controllability) to an ASIL.

Below is the complete ASIL determination table from Part 3. You should memorize it, or at least tape it somewhere very visible at your desk.

For the full table, see the ASIL determination table in ISO 26262-3:2018. Its core structure is:

```
S1 + E1/E2/E3  + C1,C2 = QM
S1 + E3        + C3    = ASIL A
S1 + E4        + C2    = ASIL A
S1 + E4        + C3    = ASIL B

S2 + E1/E2     + C1,C2 = QM
S2 + E3        + C1    = QM
S2 + E3        + C2    = ASIL A
S2 + E3        + C3    = ASIL B
S2 + E4        + C1    = ASIL A
S2 + E4        + C2    = ASIL B
S2 + E4        + C3    = ASIL C

S3 + E1        + C1,C2 = QM
S3 + E1        + C3    = ASIL A (arguably QM)
S3 + E2        + C1    = QM
S3 + E2        + C2    = ASIL A
S3 + E2        + C3    = ASIL B
S3 + E3        + C1    = ASIL A
S3 + E3        + C2    = ASIL B
S3 + E3        + C3    = ASIL C
S3 + E4        + C1    = ASIL B
S3 + E4        + C2    = ASIL C
S3 + E4        + C3    = ASIL D
```

### What Is This Table Telling You?

Notice the obvious trend: **only S3 + E4 + C3 reaches ASIL D**. The highest safety level is reserved for "most severe injury + most common scenario + hardest to control".

**QM (quality management) covers a large number of combinations.** ISO 26262 does not require every function to go through the functional safety process. If the risk is low enough, quality management is enough.

**The conservatism rule:** when you hesitate over a parameter, the standard requires the more conservative rating. That isn't overdesign. It's respect for uncertainty.

Let's take each ASIL level in turn, and what it means for your development work.

### QM (Quality Management)

QM is not an ASIL level. It means **no additional safety activities defined by ISO 26262 are required**; the existing quality management system (such as IATF 16949) is sufficient.

That does not mean "no concern for safety". QM only says the failure of this function does not create an unreasonable risk, or the risk is already low enough that no dedicated safety measure is needed. For example:
- the radio volume control
- the interior ambient lighting control

That doesn't mean these functions may fail freely: the radio jumping to maximum volume can startle you, but it won't cause a crash. So they are QM.

Are there QM-level threats in the immune system? Of course. The trillions of commensal bacteria in the gut touch the intestinal wall every day, and the immune system stays "tolerant": it does not attack. That's the immune system's quality management: no response by default, unless there is evidence that they have crossed a line.

### ASIL A: The Lowest Safety Rigor

ASIL A is the lowest functional safety level. It requires the least safety activity, but it **does** require that activity.

A typical case: S1 (minor injury) + E3 (medium exposure) + C3 (hard to control) reaches ASIL A. Imagine a seat adjustment that, in an extreme case, moves unexpectedly, puts the driver in an uncomfortable position, and may distract them. The injury severity is low (at most a minor knock to the leg), but the situation is common, and the driver cannot correct it immediately. ASIL A requires at least a safety analysis and verification of the safety requirements.

For ASIL A development:
- A safety plan is required, but the documentation requirements are relatively loose.
- Hardware metrics: ASIL A does not require quantitative SPFM and LFM assessment. Target values are set only for ASIL B, C, and D.
- Software: some safety mechanisms are recommended, but far less strict than for higher ASILs.
- Safety analysis: an inductive analysis (such as FMEA) is enough.

### ASIL B: Clear Safety Rigor

ASIL B is the middle safety level. Note from the table that both S1 + E4 + C3 and S2 + E4 + C2 reach ASIL B.

A typical ASIL B case: the anti-pinch function of a power window fails. If the window does not detect an obstacle while closing (a child's finger, say), it can cause moderate injury (a fracture, for example). The situation is common (windows are opened and closed every day), and the driver can intervene to some degree (release the button), but may not be in time.

ASIL B development requirements:
- Hardware architectural metrics: SPFM ≥ 90%, LFM ≥ 60% recommended.
- Software: follow a coding standard, use a language subset, and run static analysis.
- Safety analysis: FMEA in more detail, and FTA can start to be considered.
- Testing: requirements-based testing and structural coverage testing.

### ASIL C: High Safety Rigor

ASIL C is the second-highest level. S2 + E4 + C3 and S3 + E4 + C2 land in ASIL C. That means serious injury, a high-exposure scenario, and poor controllability.

A typical case: unintended acceleration in adaptive cruise control (ACC). On the highway (high exposure), ACC wrongly sets the target speed to maximum (severity S3, potentially a fatal crash), and the driver notices something is wrong but may not have time to react (C2 or C3). Systems like this are usually ASIL C.

ASIL C development requirements:
- Hardware metrics: SPFM ≥ 97%, LFM ≥ 80%.
- Software: highly recommended coding guidelines, defensive programming, and stricter structural coverage (MC/DC recommended).
- Safety analysis: FTA (fault tree analysis) highly recommended.
- Safety mechanisms: an independent safety monitoring path is required.
- Verification: stricter integration testing and hardware-in-the-loop testing.

### ASIL D: The Strictest Safety Rigor

ASIL D is the summit of functional safety. Only S3 + E4 + C3 reaches it.

What kind of scenario is that? **The most severe injury (death) + the most common exposure + the hardest-to-control situation.** Unintended torque from the steering system on the highway, complete loss of braking, an airbag firing when there is no crash. These are typical ASIL D candidates.

ASIL D development requirements:
- Hardware metrics: SPFM ≥ 99%, LFM ≥ 90%, PMHF < 10 FIT (fewer than 10 failures per billion hours).
- Software: the strictest coding standard, MC/DC structural coverage as a highly recommended metric, and the most comprehensive test strategy.
- Safety analysis: FTA and FMEA in parallel, complementing each other.
- Safety mechanisms: an independent, physically isolated safety monitoring path is mandatory.
- Development process: stricter configuration management, change management, and safety audits.

ASIL D is like the immune system's "systemic immune storm": commit every available resource, at any cost. But there is a subtle point: **the immune storm itself is dangerous** (a cytokine storm can kill). In the same way, overdesign at ASIL D creates problems: more system complexity (complexity is itself a safety risk), a longer development cycle, and soaring cost. Judge precisely. ASIL D should be used only where it is really needed.

## The Conservative Classification Rule: How to Decide When You Are Unsure

In HARA, every parameter rating should rest on evidence and engineering judgment. But often the data is not enough for a precise call. That's where the **conservative classification rule** comes in.

ISO 26262 requires: if you cannot determine which level a parameter belongs to, choose the one less favorable to you, meaning the "more severe" one. If a car's exposure on rural roads falls between E2 (below 1% of average operating time) and E3 (1% to 10% of average operating time), and you cannot produce exact data, choose E3.

This "choose the worse case" principle is stated explicitly in the hazard analysis and risk assessment clause of Part 3. It expresses a core functional safety creed: **better to overprotect than to risk underestimating**.

But the principle carries a risk. Choose the most conservative rating every time, and eventually every function becomes ASIL D. That defeats ISO 26262's economic rationality. What you need is **reasonable conservatism**: choose the precise rating when data is sufficient, choose the safer side when data is lacking, and do more analysis to remove the uncertainty when you are fundamentally unsure.

## The Economic Driver: The Cost Truth Behind ASIL

Face reality: ASIL is not a purely technical label. It has enormous economic meaning.

Each step up in ASIL raises development cost by roughly 1.5 to 3 times. Going from QM to ASIL A may add 50%; going from ASIL C to ASIL D may double it. This is not just the cost of more tests and documents. More fundamentally, it is **architecture cost**:
- ASIL B may need dual-channel sensors; ASIL C may need three channels.
- ASIL C can use a safety-related single-core MCU; ASIL D often needs a lockstep dual core plus independent monitoring.
- Software partitioning for ASIL B/D, the so-called FFI (freedom from interference): software at a higher ASIL must be strictly isolated from software at a lower ASIL.

In the automotive industry, for a typical EPS (electric power steering) system, the ASIL D portion can account for more than 70% of the project's safety-related development effort while making up only 20% of the code. That's the engineering reality of putting the good steel on the blade.

## Practical Cases: From HARA to ASIL

Four concrete cases to consolidate your understanding:

### Case 1: Brake Light Failure

**Scenario:** you press the brake pedal, but both brake lights fail to illuminate because of a circuit fault. The vehicle behind cannot tell you are slowing. Highway, 120 km/h.

- **S (severity):** S3. A rear-end collision can kill the occupants. It is "fatal".
- **E (exposure):** E4. You use the brake lights every time you drive. This is a "high probability" exposure that is present throughout every drive.
- **C (controllability):** C1. The driver behind can judge that you are slowing from the closing distance and your deceleration trend, so the collision is still "generally avoidable". But note: at night or in bad weather, C may rise to C2 or even C3. Here we rate C1.

**Result:** S3 + E4 + C1 = ASIL B.

Does that match your intuition? A brake light failure sounds frightening, but because the driver behind still has ways to observe, controllability brings the overall risk down to ASIL B. Functional safety is not emotion. It is engineering analysis.

### Case 2: EPS Steering Lock

**Scenario:** a motor control module fault locks the steering column in the electric power steering, and the wheel cannot be turned. Highway, 100 km/h.

- **S:** S3. Steering failure leaves the vehicle unable to avoid an obstacle; it enters the oncoming lane or runs off the road, with a very high probability of being fatal.
- **E:** E4. The steering function is in use at all times. Not "frequent": "continuous". It fully meets the high-probability exposure condition.
- **C:** C3. Once the steering is locked, the only thing the driver can do is brake, but braking cannot replace steering for avoidance. An ordinary driver finds it "difficult or even impossible" to avoid the harm.

**Result:** S3 + E4 + C3 = ASIL D.

### Case 3: Parking Sensor Failure

**Scenario:** while reversing, the ultrasonic sensor fails and the system keeps showing "no obstacle behind", but there is actually a crouching child behind the car. Low-speed reversing in a parking lot.

- **S:** S2. A reversing collision can cause "severe and life-threatening" injuries such as fractures to a pedestrian or a crouching child, but the low speed and the child's small stature may raise the severity. We are between S2 and S3, so we take S3 conservatively.
- **E:** E3. Reversing takes 1%–10% of vehicle operating time and is very common in urban parking. Rated E3.
- **C:** C2. The driver is ultimately responsible for checking behind (looking back, using the mirrors). Radar is supporting information, not the only source. Most drivers can avoid the harm to some degree. If a driver relies entirely on radar and never looks back, controllability drops. Here we assume a standard driver and rate C2.

**Result:** S3 + E3 + C2 = ASIL B.

### Case 4: High-Voltage Battery Thermal Runaway

**Scenario:** after an internal short in the battery, the battery management system (BMS) of an electric vehicle fails to cut the high-voltage circuit and raise an alarm. Heat builds up rapidly inside the battery, the temperature crosses the thermal runaway threshold, and the battery burns or even explodes. Driving on the highway, the occupants cannot get out immediately.

- **S:** S3. A fire caused by battery thermal runaway is fatal, especially when the occupants cannot stop and leave the vehicle immediately. Maximum severity.
- **E:** E4. The battery is always in operation; it charges and discharges on every driving cycle. The exposure is continuous.
- **C:** C3. As the driver, you cannot tell within seconds whether the battery is about to run away thermally, and no action of yours can stop an internal short from spreading into thermal runaway. Controllability is "uncontrollable".

**Result:** S3 + E4 + C3 = ASIL D.

## Summary

1. ASIL is determined by a lookup table, not by multiplying S × E × C
2. Only S3 + E4 + C3 reaches ASIL D
3. QM is not an ASIL level; it means the identified risk can be managed under quality management without conforming to ISO 26262
4. Conservatism says "when unsure, choose the more severe", but it should not be abused
5. Each step up in ASIL means a significant cost increase
6. ASIL rates the consequence of injury, not the fault itself
