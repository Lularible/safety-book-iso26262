# 1.3 Therac-25: Software That Killed Three Times

### Georgia, 1985

Marietta. A 61-year-old breast cancer patient lies on the treatment table of a Therac-25. The machine, built by Atomic Energy of Canada Limited (AECL), can aim a high-energy electron beam or an X-ray beam precisely at a tumor. Her treatment plan is postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy: electron-beam mode, about 200 rad, delivered to the left chest wall and axillary region to clear any microscopic disease left after surgery.

The operator types in the command. The screen flashes "MALFUNCTION 54," fault 54. This is a known error code on the Therac-25, one that has appeared frequently over the past few months. It usually means the actual delivered dose does not match the prescribed value. The operator presses the clear key. Re-enters the command. MALFUNCTION 54 again. Clears again. Re-enters again.

The third time, the machine fires a beam of extremely high energy in **full-power X-ray mode**. But the screen still says "electron-beam mode." The mode shown to the operator and the configuration the machine is actually executing are no longer the same. At the moment of irradiation the patient feels "a strong electric shock, like being burned."

The dose she actually receives is about **25,000 rad**, 125 times the prescribed dose. Months later, radiation burns cause tissue necrosis. She loses permanent function in her left arm and shoulder, and her breast is removed. She lives the rest of her life in pain, but **she survives**.

The same accident happened on the Therac-25 **at least six times**. The FDA investigation found that this was not a hardware failure. It was a **race condition** in concurrent software. Under a particular timing, a one-byte flag was set abnormally, causing the machine to skip the physical insertion of the electron-beam scattering target. Without the target, the electron beam became a lethal X-ray beam.

The Therac-25 accidents drove the creation of a regulatory system for medical software safety.

### The Hardware Was Not Broken — Software Design Rejected the Hardware's Safety

The Therac-25 differs from its predecessor, the Therac-20, in one key respect: **it removed the hardware safety interlock.**

On the Therac-20, if the software tried to fire a high-energy electron beam without inserting the X-ray target, an independent hardware circuit would physically cut power to the beam source. Whatever the software said, the physical switch would not close and the beam could not fire. On the Therac-25, AECL's engineers decided that "software reliability was high enough." They removed the hardware interlock and handed most safety decisions to software.

That is the root of how a race condition can kill. **Software has no physical "no."**

The Therac-25's operating system was concurrent: a main control task, a keyboard-scan task, and a dose-monitoring task. The three tasks shared a set of global variables. In a particular timing window, only microseconds wide, the keyboard-scan task modified one byte of data while the dose-monitoring task read the same byte. The high bit of the byte flipped at the wrong instant, so the confirmation "target inserted" was erroneously skipped. The machine began to fire.

But this was not a "one-off hardware failure." The reason the accident was so hard to pin down in the FDA investigation is that it was **reproducible but unpredictable**. Every time the operator cleared MALFUNCTION 54 and re-entered the command, they were in effect rolling the timing dice again: the relative timing between the keyboard-scan task and the dose-monitoring task differed each time, and one roll happened to land in the race window.

What the Therac-25 taught the world is this: when safety depends on software, you must assume the software will fail, and back it up with independent, non-software mechanisms. ISO 26262's requirements for "independent safety monitoring," "hardware interlocks," and "redundancy" point in the same direction as the Therac-25's lesson.

### From Therac-25 to ISO 26262-6: The Sin and Redemption of Software

The FDA investigation report, published in the 1980s and still one of the most important engineering documents in software safety, lists the Therac-25's software defects. Many items on that list have counterparts in later medical and automotive safety standards. When you read the list, you will find it is almost a "textbook of counterexamples" for ISO 26262 from the concept phase through software and hardware development.

**Defect 1: A global flag variable was shared by three concurrent tasks with no mutual-exclusion protection.** The keyboard-scan task (running every 200 ms, handling the keyboard input buffer) and the dose-monitoring task (running every 40 ms, reading the dose counter and comparing it with the preset value) shared the same 8-bit flag byte. Bit 0 of the flag meant "X-ray target in place"; bit 1 meant "collimator in place." In the race window, the keyboard-scan task was writing one bit of the byte while the dose-monitoring task was reading the same byte, and the value read was a "half-updated" intermediate state. This was not a theoretical probability problem: the Therac-25's operating frequency meant the two tasks had an overlapping concurrency window roughly every 10 seconds, and the race had a chance of triggering within the roughly 1 microsecond of overlap. Actual operating logs showed MALFUNCTION 54 occurring several times a day on average.

**ISO 26262-6's response:** software architecture design must provide freedom from interference (FFI) for safety-related data. Specific techniques include MPU partitioning, which isolates the memory region holding safety-critical data from all non-safety tasks at the hardware level, and protection of shared data: when any two tasks of different ASILs share a variable, whether you choose a mutex or an integrity check, the mechanism itself must undergo safety analysis.

**Defect 2: The error code MALFUNCTION 54 was cleared at will by the operator, with no fault retention.** In the Therac-25's software design documents, MALFUNCTION 54 was defined as "the deviation between the measured dose rate and the expected value exceeds 5%." But the handling was: show the code on screen, pause irradiation, wait for the operator to press the clear key. After clearing, **the machine retained no fault history, ran no diagnostics, and restricted no subsequent operation.** The operator could immediately re-enter the treatment parameters and restart. The FDA investigation found that the operating log on the day of the accident recorded nothing between the six MALFUNCTION 54 events, meaning the operator took no troubleshooting action after any of the clears.

**ISO 26262-6's response:** safety-related fault detection must trigger a fault response protocol: record the fault type, the time it occurred, and the operating context before it occurred. The fault response protocol must define a graded strategy: a minor fault can be logged and operation continued, a moderate fault limits the function and degrades it, a severe fault goes directly to the safe state. The fault response must not be bypassable by the user (operator, driver): "clearing a fault" must be a controlled operation, usually requiring specific diagnostic equipment privileges.

**Defect 3: The on-screen status was out of sync with the actual execution state.** When the race triggered, the machine was actually in X-ray mode: full power, no physical scattering target, the beam shining directly into the patient, the dose rate 125 times that of normal treatment. But the operator's screen still showed "electron-beam mode, target inserted, dose rate normal." The reason: the status display data came from the main control task, and the main control task was reading the very flag byte that the race had corrupted. **The synchronization chain between screen information and the real machine state was broken.**

**The response in the concept and system parts of ISO 26262:** the displayed information about the "safe state" must come from a "trusted source" — not a periodic sample from the main control task, but the direct output of a safety mechanism. For example, in an ASIL D EPB system, the drive signal for the "parking status indicator" does not come from a CAN message for the instrument cluster. It comes from a hardwire safety-state signal from the brake ECU. If the CAN message is disturbed or delayed, the hardwire signal can still light the red parking warning lamp on the dashboard. **Safety-related information display must not depend on a "non-safety" data channel.**

**Defect 4: The software had no independent fault shutdown loop.** The Therac-25 did have a second, independent dose-monitoring microprocessor (DAC) that read the actual radiation dose from an independent ionization chamber and compared it with the preset value. But that microprocessor's output **was not connected to the emergency power-cut loop**. It could only "record" an overdose. It was a post-hoc audit tool, not a real-time safety mechanism. When the race triggered and the DAC detected an overdose, it wrote the anomaly to a log. But the log was in EEPROM, and the machine's main control logic knew nothing about it. It kept firing.

**The response in the system part of ISO 26262:** **the fault response chain must be end to end, from detection to actuation.** The output of an independent fault-detection unit, such as a safety monitor MCU, must connect directly to the actuator's power-cut loop, not pass through the main MCU's "judgment." Because the main MCU may itself be the source of the fault: letting it decide "whether to cut power because the safety monitor reported a problem" is letting the defendant be the judge.

After the Therac-25, medical device regulation changed fundamentally around the world. The US FDA issued a series of regulatory documents on medical device software through the 1990s. IEC 62304, the medical device software lifecycle standard, was published in 2006. And ISO 26262's strict requirements for software safety — software safety architecture, freedom from interference, fault response protocols, safe states — carry the Therac-25's lessons in their genes.

### From Therac to Cars: The Same Bug, Moving from 0 to 120 km/h

You may say: the Therac-25 was a medical device. What does it have to do with the ECU I write?

The connection is this: **medical devices and automotive safety systems share the same software safety model.** The differences are only two: speed of motion and the operator.

The operator of a medical device is a trained technician, working in a controlled hospital environment, with redundant dosimeters and physical constraints (lead walls, a treatment room). The operator of an automotive safety system is **a driver you don't know** — untrained, possibly tired, possibly in a rainstorm, on a congested highway, with family sitting beside them.

Replay the Therac-25 race bug in a car at 120 km/h and the consequences are immediate. It does not take months. A few hundred milliseconds after the fault, the steering wheel is pulled 3 degrees off, and the car leaves its lane. The software architecture of the LKA system must guarantee that in any race window, an erroneous steering-angle command is never executed. **That is a higher bar than the Therac-25's: you need not only after-the-fact prevention, but before-the-fact prevention and immediate correction.**

That is why ISO 26262 was first published in 2011. It is an adaptation of IEC 61508 for electrical/electronic systems in road vehicles. The definition of "safety" differs for cars because **the speed of harm differs**.

### Toyota Unintended Acceleration: A Global Wake-Up Call for ECU Software Safety

August 2009, San Diego, California. A Lexus ES350 speeds toward an intersection on the highway. The driver shouts into the 911 call, "We can't stop!" The car eventually goes out of control and catches fire. All four people inside die. This is the most famous of Toyota's "unintended acceleration" accidents — the Saylor family of four lost their lives. The NHTSA investigation report attributed the direct cause to a floor mat trapping the accelerator pedal.

From 1999 to 2011, more than 3,000 complaints of unintended acceleration in Toyota vehicles were filed with NHTSA in the US market. Sticking floor mats and slow-returning accelerator pedals explained some of them, but many complaints still could not be clearly attributed.

In 2011, NASA was commissioned by the US Department of Transportation to conduct a 10-month thorough review of Toyota's electronic throttle control system (ETCS). NASA's final report concluded: "The NESC team did not find any electronic or software defect that could explain unintended acceleration." That is a conclusion about insufficient evidence, not an absolute proof that "the electronics had no defects."

But the story did not end with NASA. In 2013, during Toyota's civil litigation, the plaintiffs' expert witness Michael Barr (Barr Group) performed an independent analysis of Toyota's ETCS source code and reached a conclusion very different from NASA's. Barr testified in court that Toyota's ETCS had multiple software design problems:

**First, the lack of an independent fault-termination path.** The ETCS main control program and the fault-monitoring program ran on the same MCU, sharing the same RTOS. If the main control program skipped the throttle-closure safety check because of an exception such as a stack overflow, and the monitoring program happened to be disturbed in the same scheduling cycle, both would fail at the same time. This is architecturally isomorphic to the Therac-25 race defect: **the safety mechanism and the protected function shared the same failure domain.**

**Second, insufficient fault logging and diagnostic coverage.** The ETCS had multiple redundant sensors — two independent accelerator pedal position sensors, a throttle position sensor, a brake pedal switch — but no code performed cross-sensor consistency checks. The software checked each sensor's reading against a plausible range separately, but a combination such as "pedal A reads 40% open, pedal B reads 0%, brake is pressed" is obviously impossible under normal operation.

These two criticisms are strong from a software engineering standpoint, but they come from expert analysis in litigation, not from an official safety conclusion. The complexity of this case is itself a lesson: when the consequences of a system's failure are severe enough — multiple deaths, thousands of complaints, congressional hearings — "the cause of the fault" is no longer just an engineering question. It becomes a legal question, a political question, and a public relations question at the same time.

Therac-25 and Toyota unintended acceleration are isomorphic in their architectural defect — **safety functions and non-safety functions sharing the same failure domain** — which is exactly the core problem ISO 26262's FFI (freedom from interference) requirements try to eliminate. But the Toyota case also teaches a subtler lesson: even when the safety architecture has latent weaknesses, the fault may still be intermittent, hard to reproduce, and hard to attribute, and "unable to prove a defect" does not equal "no defect."

The Therac-25 accidents drove the creation of a regulatory system for medical software safety. Toyota unintended acceleration is one of the real-world backdrops against which the second edition of ISO 26262 tightened its requirements for software safety architecture and test coverage. After the MCAS software defects on the Boeing 737 MAX, the aviation world reexamined its requirements for independent safety argumentation and review independence in software design.

Toyota has always denied any causal link between these software problems and unintended acceleration, and has maintained that position in multiple lawsuits. The Barr Group analysis described in this section comes from public court records and does not represent the author's conclusion about the cause of the accidents.

Every safety rule you follow today may stand on a real accident, a real investigation report, and real people who were killed or injured. You cannot use accidents to validate safety. You need standards to prevent them.

### Summary

1. The Therac-25's race condition caused at least six radiation overdose accidents, three of them fatal. The root cause was a software concurrency bug plus the removal of hardware interlocks. It defined software safety's "principle of distrust": you cannot assume the software is correct, and you must have an independent hardware backup.
2. The Therac-25's list of software defects maps onto ISO 26262's safety requirements from concept through system to software and hardware.
3. Cars and medical devices share the same safety model. The difference is that harm from a car is immediate, so the reaction-time requirements are stricter.
4. The core dispute in both the Therac-25 and Toyota unintended acceleration points to the same architectural problem: the safety function and the protected function share the same failure domain. ISO 26262's FFI (freedom from interference) requirement is a direct response to that lesson.
