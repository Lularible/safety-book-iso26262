# 2.14 ASIL Decomposition: Lower the Level Without Weakening the Goal

## The immune metaphor: distributed immunity — don't put all your eggs in one basket

The human body's immune defense is never a single mechanism. The skin is the first physical barrier. If it is breached, macrophages and complement proteins in the innate immune system respond at once. If the pathogen still gets through that line, the adaptive immune system activates: dendritic cells extract antigen and present it to T cells, and T cells activate B cells to produce specific antibodies. These three layers operate independently and share one "safety goal" (defend against foreign pathogens), yet the failure of any one layer does not collapse the whole defense.

That is the core philosophy of ASIL decomposition. For an ASIL D safety goal, ISO 26262 requires a single-point fault metric (SPFM) of ≥99%, a latent fault metric (LFM) of ≥90%, and a probabilistic metric for random hardware failures (PMHF) below 10 FIT. To hit those numbers you pour redundancy and diagnostics into the hardware: dual-core lockstep processors, ECC memory, supply monitoring, watchdogs, full BIST... High cost, complex design.

But have you considered another approach? Don't cram ASIL D into one chip. Let two independent chips each carry ASIL B and together reach the safety level of ASIL D. That is ASIL decomposition: split one "hard" safety problem into two "easy" ones. Easy, but not fragile. Like the immune system's layered defense: if one layer is breached, the others are still on guard.

## Scenario: a dual-MCU architecture for a steering ECU

Three in the afternoon. You draw two boxes on the whiteboard in the design review room.

Left box: "Main control MCU (Infineon TC3xx)." Right box: "Safety monitor MCU (TI Hercules TMS570)." Between them you draw a thick isolation line. Your goal: ASIL D steering assistance safety.

The problem in front of you is this. With a single MCU, reaching ASIL D requires dual-core lockstep, ECC, MBIST, LBIST, supply monitoring, an external watchdog, redundant sensor channels... Material cost, PCB area, and software complexity all shoot up. But if you split the function — the main MCU handles normal steering assistance control logic, the safety monitor MCU independently checks whether the main control is working correctly — each chip only needs to reach ASIL B. Two ASIL B chips, combined through a sufficiently independent architecture, reach ASIL D safety integrity.

You write on the whiteboard: **ASIL D = ASIL B(D) + ASIL B(D)**.

The "(D)" in parentheses is the key. It says this element carries the original ASIL D safety goal, but through decomposition its own development requirements drop to ASIL B. The parentheses denote "lineage." This element was decomposed from ASIL D; it is not naturally B.

Your colleague looks up from across the table: "Why make it so complicated? Isn't one ASIL D chip fine?"

You say: "One ASIL D chip is fine, of course. But ASIL D MCUs are far fewer and far more expensive than ASIL B parts. Two ASIL B MCUs give you a wider choice, a safer supply chain, and the architecture provides physical redundancy for free. If one MCU is taken out by EMI, the other can still detect it and trigger the safe state."

"Then why not build two software partitions inside one chip? That's cheaper."

You pick up the red marker and cross it out on the board: "That's the first trap we're covering today."

## Decomposition schemes: three legal downgrade paths

ISO 26262-9 defines the formal grammar of ASIL decomposition. For ASIL D you can downgrade to:

**Scheme one: C(D) + A(D)**
The primary element drops to ASIL C and the auxiliary element drops to ASIL A. This is the "strong-weak pairing": the main force carries most of the safety responsibility, and the auxiliary performs critical checks. It suits scenarios where the main MCU is capable and the monitor MCU only needs simple diagnostics.

**Scheme two: B(D) + B(D)**
Both elements drop to ASIL B. This is the "twin scheme": two equally capable chips supervise each other. It is the most common architecture and the typical choice for a steering ECU.

**Scheme three: D(D) + QM(D)**
One element stays ASIL D and the other drops to QM (quality managed, no safety requirements). This sounds contradictory. If the auxiliary element is QM, then when it fails, the main ASIL D element must be able to maintain safety on its own. This scheme suits scenarios where "the auxiliary function is optional and does not affect safety." For example, the main switch in the safety shutdown path is ASIL D and the auxiliary soft-start circuit is QM: if the soft-start circuit breaks, the worst case is no smooth start, but the main switch can still shut down reliably.

For ASIL C decomposition:
- **B(C) + A(C)**: one drops to B, one drops to A
- **C(C) + QM(C)**: one stays C, one drops to QM

For ASIL B decomposition:
- **A(B) + A(B)**: both drop to A
- **B(B) + QM(B)**: one stays B, one drops to QM

For ASIL A decomposition:
- **A(A) + QM(A)**: the standard gives only this one for A, and requires that it be used "only when needed"

You draw this downgrade tree on the whiteboard, then circle "hardware metrics unchanged" hard.

## Golden rule one: hardware metrics do not drop with decomposition

This is the rule most often broken in ASIL decomposition. You decompose ASIL D into B(D)+B(D). The development-process requirements (the activities in ISO 26262-2, -3, -4, -5, -6, -8, and -9 that deal with systematic failures) may be executed at the downgraded ASIL: the main MCU does software development and testing to ASIL B, and the monitor MCU does software development and testing to ASIL B. **But** the hardware architectural metrics (SPFM, LFM) and the assessment of the probabilistic metric for random hardware failures (PMHF) **must still meet the original ASIL D targets**.

What does that mean? Even though your two MCUs are each developed to ASIL B, the overall hardware must still achieve SPFM ≥99% and PMHF <10 FIT. You cannot cut corners on hardware safety mechanisms just because you decomposed. You can use a simpler software process, but you cannot accept worse hardware diagnostic coverage.

This is a deep insight in ISO 26262: **random hardware failures are physical phenomena. They do not shrink because you decomposed.** Two ASIL B chips contain more transistors than one ASIL D chip, and therefore more potential failure modes. You must control those failures with enough hardware safety mechanisms: ECC, lockstep, BIST, supply monitoring — none of them optional. What ASIL decomposition lowers is "the probability of development-induced systematic errors," not "the probability of random hardware failures."

## Golden rule two: sufficient independence — the lifeline of decomposition

You split ASIL D into B(D)+B(D). Now you have to answer a lethal question: **how do you guarantee the two B(D) elements do not fail at the same time?**

If both MCUs share the same power rail, and an overvoltage transient on that rail destroys both chips at once, your decomposition collapses completely: a single common-cause failure (CCF) punches straight through all your redundancy. That is why ISO 26262-9 imposes severe independence requirements on ASIL decomposition:

1. **A dependent failure analysis (DFA) must show that no credible dependent failure exists.** The DFA is a precondition for decomposition, not an optional step. You must perform a DFA at the architecture design stage and identify every coupling factor: shared power, shared clock, shared PCB substrate, identical chip types, identical development toolchains. Then analyze, one by one, whether they can cause common-cause or cascading failures.

2. **If the DFA cannot rule out some coupling factor, you must treat the common-cause failure as a single-point fault at the original ASIL.** For example, if both MCUs share one power rail and a rail failure affects both, then you must invest in redundancy and safety mechanisms on the power design that match ASIL D (two independent supplies, supply monitoring, undervoltage protection, and so on).

3. **Homogeneous redundancy cannot simply justify an ASIL downgrade.** This is the key statement in the standard's section on homogeneous redundancy: "The use of homogeneous redundant elements is, without further measure, not sufficient to reduce the ASIL because the same systematic fault could cause a common cause failure of the redundant elements."

In plain terms: two MCUs of the same type running the same code. That is not ASIL decomposition. That is "the same bug running twice." If the main MCU's firmware has a systematic defect (say it divides by zero under a particular timing pattern), the monitor MCU runs the same code and triggers the same bug under the same conditions. Both are gone, and ASIL D becomes "two Bs that failed together."

**Heterogeneity is the best practice for ASIL decomposition.** The main control uses an Infineon TC3xx, the monitor uses a TI Hercules TMS570. Different instruction sets (TriCore vs ARM Cortex-R), different compilers (Tasking vs TI CCS), different development teams. The probability that a systematic defect appears in both at the same time approaches zero. Even if one side has a bug, the other can still independently detect the anomaly and trigger the safe state.

## Golden rule three: integration activities run at the pre-decomposition ASIL

The standard requires: **integration activities, and the verification and confirmation measures that follow them, must be executed at the ASIL that applied before decomposition.** That is, although the main MCU and the monitor MCU are each developed as B(D), when they are integrated into the complete steering ECU system, integration testing, integration verification, and vehicle integration must go back to ASIL D.

This is the most easily overlooked rule in ASIL decomposition. You put two ASIL B things together: their interfaces, timing, and coordination logic. Those things themselves form a new level of complexity. The two chips talk over SPI: SPI electrical characteristics, timing jitter, protocol anomalies, arbitration failures. These are scenarios that ASIL B development does not cover. You must verify the behavior of the whole integrated body with ASIL D rigor.

In your steering ECU architecture this shows up as:
- The SPI communication between the main MCU and the monitor MCU must have end-to-end protection (E2E Profile 1, CRC-8, DataID, and a 4-bit counter)
- The monitor MCU's "heartbeat check" of the main MCU must complete within the ASIL D timing requirement (that is, within the fault tolerant time interval, FTTI)
- The "handshake failure" handling logic between the two MCUs must be verified to ASIL D: no deadlock, no livelock, no silent failure
- Vehicle-level safety verification (fault injection testing, long-term testing, user testing) must cover this integrated body; you cannot test each MCU alone

## Scenario echo: the red circle on the whiteboard

Back to the review room that afternoon. You finish the last stroke on the whiteboard. Your colleague stares at the four circles, is quiet for a moment, and then says: "So ASIL decomposition isn't a free lunch."

Right. ASIL decomposition is not a free lunch. It's a trade: you replace one ASIL D development process with two ASIL B development processes, but the price you pay is:
1. Hardware metrics cannot drop: the safety mechanisms you need are still needed
2. You must prove sufficient independence: the DFA is a hard gate
3. Integration activities return to the original ASIL: putting the pieces together must be stricter
4. You cannot fake it with homogeneous redundancy: heterogeneity is the right path

"Is it worth it?"

"When ASIL D MCUs come from only two or three suppliers, with a 52-week lead time and a 200-dollar unit price, two ASIL B MCUs — wide choice, fast delivery, and only 80 dollars combined — are still worth it even after the extra cost of the DFA and heterogeneous design. And the architecture gets physical redundancy for free. That is an advantage a single-chip solution simply does not have."

You photograph the whiteboard and file it in the project's architecture decision record (ADR). Every reason for this decision — technical, cost, supply chain, safety — is on record. Safety design is not a purely technical problem. It is the search for an optimum under constraints. ASIL decomposition is a sharp tool in that optimization toolbox: sharp, but it must be used with care.

## Summary

1. Legal schemes: D→C(D)+A(D), B(D)+B(D), D(D)+QM(D); C→B(C)+A(C), C(C)+QM(C); B→A(B)+A(B), B(B)+QM(B); A→A(A)+QM(A), only when needed
2. Hardware metrics do not drop with decomposition
3. Sufficient independence is the lifeline of decomposition: the DFA must prove no common cause, and homogeneous redundancy cannot justify a downgrade
4. Integration activities run at the pre-decomposition ASIL
