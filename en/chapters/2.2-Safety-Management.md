# 2.2 Safety Management: Who Signs, and What the Signature Means

Two in the afternoon. Your phone buzzes. A message from the VP: "Zhang, I know the project is badly delayed, but can we skip the functional safety confirmation review this quarter? Let's ship the sample first. The customer at RSI is pushing hard. We'll catch up on the review report next version." You stare at the screen, your finger hovering over "OK".

You are the safety manager on this steering ECU project.

You think back three years to an EPS project at another Tier 1. Under schedule pressure, the safety manager signed off on a software release that had never been independently reviewed. Six months later, a prototype vehicle lost steering control during a high-speed test and the test engineer nearly died. The investigation found that in a function of fewer than 50 lines, the parameter range check had been removed "to optimize performance". In court, the safety manager said one sentence: "I knew I shouldn't sign, but I had no choice."

You put the phone down and type: "No. We'll talk at the company tomorrow."

Signing is the heaviest responsibility you carry as a safety manager. This chapter is about functional safety management: who signs, what the signature means, and why you must learn to say no.

## The Two-Layer Architecture of Part 2

ISO 26262-2, "Functional safety management", is the standard's organizational design manual. It has two layers:

**Layer one: organization-level safety management.** This applies to the whole company. It doesn't ask what project you are on. It asks whether this company is capable of doing functional safety at all. It covers:

- Establishing and maintaining a safety culture
- Appointing and authorizing the safety manager
- Overall policy for the safety lifecycle
- Competence management
- Connecting the quality management system to functional safety

**Layer two: project-level safety management.** This applies to a specific development project. It asks: how is safety management carried out in this project? It covers:

- Planning and coordinating safety activities
- Planning the safety case and the confirmation measures
- Tailoring the safety lifecycle
- Change management and impact analysis
- Functional safety assessment

Think of it this way: the first layer asks whether the hospital is licensed to practice; the second asks whether this particular operation follows the protocol. A top-tier hospital (organizationally qualified) does not guarantee that every operation succeeds (each project still needs item-by-item confirmation), but an operation performed at an unlicensed clinic (organizationally unqualified) is illegal from the start.

## Safety Culture: Not a Slogan on the Wall

You walk into meeting rooms at many companies and see "Safety First" and "Zero Accidents" on the wall. But if you hear engineers grumbling over lunch, "That safety review is just a formality; the project manager signs off anyway", then the company has no real safety culture.

ISO 26262 makes concrete demands on safety culture. It is not just a slogan. It names several key attributes:

**A commitment to continuous improvement.** Safety is not a one-time effort. Every issue found in a review, every bug exposed by a test, every field repair in series production: all of it must feed back into the safety system. The immune system doesn't stop patrolling because "there was no infection last time". It responds more efficiently because it has seen a similar pathogen.

**Ownership of safety.** Whoever writes the code owns its safety. It is not the safety manager's job: the safety manager owns the process, the confirmation, and the gatekeeping, but every engineer is the first person accountable for the safety of their own work product. In the body, the immune system is not the only thing defending you: skin cells secrete antimicrobial peptides themselves. Functional safety is everyone's responsibility.

**Accountability.** It is a hard word to translate. It means "responsible" plus "traceable". Every design decision, every review comment, every test result should be recorded, traceable, and signed by someone. If a defect is found five years later, you can follow the document chain back to who decided what, in what context. This is not about assigning blame: it is about finding the root cause and improving the system.

**Striving for excellence, not just compliance.** The highest level of safety culture is when engineers do safety not to pass a review, but because they genuinely believe safety is part of the product. You say a doctor has "good medical ethics". He doesn't diagnose carefully because he fears a malpractice review. He diagnoses carefully because he believes careful diagnosis is simply what being a doctor means.

## The Safety Manager: Central Regulator of the Immune System

In a functional safety system, the safety manager's role is critical. ISO 26262-2 sets clear requirements:

**The safety manager must be formally appointed.** Not "so-and-so also handles safety on the side", but a formal appointment document. That document defines the safety manager's scope of responsibility and authority.

**The safety manager must have enough authority.** This is critical: the safety manager must be able to stop a design decision that violates safety requirements, demand additional safety mechanisms, and hold the safety line when schedule and safety conflict. If the safety manager lacks authority, the signature is just a rubber stamp.

**The independence requirement falls mainly on the confirmation measures; the safety manager can be the project manager.** Those are two separate things ISO 26262 states. Why stress independence? Because the project manager's KPIs are schedule, cost, and delivery. If confirmation reviews, functional safety audits, and functional safety assessments are not independent, there is an incentive to sacrifice safety for delivery. These confirmation measures must be performed by people independent of the developers, project management, and authors of the work product. The higher the ASIL, the higher the required independence level.

Think of the immune system. Its "KPI" is protecting you from infection, even if that means fever, fatigue, and bed rest. If the immune system answered to your desire to go out and play, it would lose the ability to protect you.

**The safety manager must have an escalation path.** When the safety manager and the project manager clash, the safety risk must be reportable all the way up to someone with the authority to allocate resources. ISO 26262 sets up anomaly escalation and resource assurance for this: a safety anomaly that has not been managed to closure should be escalated to the person responsible for functional safety; the organization should also provide the resources needed to achieve functional safety.

It's like the head of infection control at a hospital needing a direct line to the hospital director. If a department head rejects the isolation plan, the infection control head can't just drop it. She can go around the department head and straight to the director.

## Tailoring the Safety Lifecycle: Not Every Item Needs the Full Process

The ISO 26262 safety lifecycle is a complete framework, but not every project has to run every activity. Part 2 lets you tailor it.

Tailoring rests on two dimensions:

1. **Item type:** are you developing a brand-new item, or modifying an existing one? How large is the impact?
2. **ASIL level:** QM/A/B/C/D. The higher the risk, the stricter the activities.

For example:
- A new ASIL D item: run the full safety lifecycle.
- A small change to an existing ASIL B item: you may need only an impact analysis, part of the development activities, and appropriate verification.
- A QM item (not safety-related): the ISO 26262 development requirements don't apply, but the project-level impact analysis and its confirmation review still have to be done. The judgment "QM" itself comes out of HARA. You can't just declare "this is QM, so no HARA".

**But you must record the rationale for tailoring.** You can't skip something because "we felt it wasn't needed". The safety plan must state plainly which activities were omitted, why, on what basis, and whether anyone independently reviewed the soundness of that decision.

In immune terms: not every infection needs a full-body mobilization. For a mild sore throat you need only a local inflammatory response; you don't need to mobilize the bone marrow to produce a flood of new white blood cells. But if you treat sepsis as an ordinary cold (wrong tailoring), the result can be fatal. And telling "a cold" from "sepsis" comes from a correct diagnosis (HARA).

## The Safety Plan: A Battle Map for Safety Activities

The safety plan is a core work product required by Part 2. It is not an administrative document. It is the top-level plan for your safety management.

The safety plan must define:

**What to do:** which safety activities, which objectives, which work products.

**Who does it:** the owner of each activity. Not "the team". A named person.

**When:** the planned dates and milestones for each activity.

**Dependencies:** which activities must finish before which others? Which can run in parallel?

**Resource needs:** which tools (software tools need qualification), which people, which equipment.

**Who performs independent review:** for confirmation review, safety audit, and safety assessment, what is the independence level of the reviewer in each case.

The safety plan should be written at project start and updated as the project moves. It is not a document you finish and archive. It is a live project-management tool. A battle map isn't hung on the command-post wall to look nice: every time the situation changes, the map is updated.

## The Confirmation Measures: Three Lines of Defense

ISO 26262-2 defines three confirmation measures, forming functional safety's three lines of defense:

### First Line: Confirmation Review

An independent review of a specific work product for sufficiency and correctness. The objects include key work products such as the HARA report, the safety plan, and the safety case.

A confirmation review is not an ordinary peer review. It demands more independence and a more systematic examination.

### Second Line: Functional Safety Audit

This assesses whether the safety activities were carried out according to the safety plan. It does not check whether the technical solution is right; it checks whether the things that should have been done were done.

- The safety plan said to do an independent review. Was it done?
- The safety plan said to do a code walkthrough. Where's the evidence?
- The safety plan said changes need an impact analysis. Where's the record?

An audit is a compliance check of the process.

### Third Line: Functional Safety Assessment

This is the most important confirmation measure: it assesses whether the whole item meets the functional safety requirements. It answers: "Based on all the processes that were followed and all the evidence that was produced, do we have reason to believe this system is safe?"

A functional safety assessment is a conclusive, integrated judgment. It does not repeat the technical reviews. It stands one level higher and examines whether the whole safety argument is complete, consistent, and sufficient.

## Independence Levels: Stepping Up from I0 to I3

ISO 26262 splits the independence requirement for confirmation measures into four levels. The higher the ASIL, the higher the required independence. The point is to keep the reviewer's judgment free of the developer's own interests and biases.

| Independence level | Requirement | Who can review | Immune-system analogy |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I0** | The confirmation measure should be performed; if performed, someone else must do it | It must be performed by someone other than the person responsible for creating the work product; authors cannot review their own work | Cellular self-repair: a cell detects and repairs its own DNA damage |
| **I1** | Different people | Not the same person who developed the work product, but may be on the same project team | Neighboring cells in the same tissue send danger signals |
| **I2** | Different teams | Reviewers come from a different project team and report to a different direct manager | Dedicated immune cells in a different tissue (e.g. a lymph node) |
| **I3** | Different department or organization | Reviewers come from a different department, a different company, or an external independent assessment body | An external "expert consultation panel", unaffected by departmental interests |

**ISO 26262-2 explicitly requires** that for an ASIL D item, the functional safety assessment reach independence level I3. If your product is ASIL D, you must find another department or an external body to do the safety assessment. The people who developed the product cannot assess it themselves.

It's like the pre-operative conference for high-risk surgery, where outside experts must take part. Hospital doctors may carry confirmation bias: "this is our department's patient", "this is our chief's plan". Outside experts carry none of that baggage.

## Impact Analysis: An Item Someone Else Built, and Your Safety Responsibility

In real projects you almost never start from zero. You might:

- use an existing MCU platform
- reuse the brake control algorithm from the previous product
- bring in a third-party AUTOSAR basic software stack
- add a new feature to an existing EPS system (such as automated parking)

Here Part 2 requires an **impact analysis**. You assess whether the existing item is still safe in its new context.

The impact analysis must answer:

**How was the existing item developed and verified before?** Did it go through a functional safety development process? If so, what ASIL was assumed at the time? Is the item boundary the same as it is now?

**What changes does the new context introduce?** Inputs changed? Outputs changed? Operating environment changed (it used to be a B-segment car, now it's an A-segment car)? Interactions with other systems changed?

**Do those changes affect safety?** If the current safety requirements are stricter than before (the old assumption was ASIL B, now you need ASIL D), is the existing design and verification evidence still enough?

**What additional activities are needed?** What can be reused? What must be redone?

The conclusion of an impact analysis is not "the existing system passed ISO 26262, so we can use it as is". What you must argue is "based on the combination of existing evidence and additional activities, the overall safety in the new context is achievable".

In immune terms: having chickenpox as a child does not mean you are perfectly immune now. If the varicella virus reappears in a new form, your immune memory (the old system's verification evidence) is still valid, but you need to confirm the antibody titer is sufficient (impact analysis). If it isn't, you get a booster (additional verification).

## Work Products: The Evidence Chain of Safety Management

Part 2 requires a set of work products to prove that safety management is effective. The most important ones:

**Safety plan:** defines the safety management activities for the whole project.

**Confirmation review report:** the result of each confirmation review, the issues found, and their closure status.

**Functional safety audit report:** the audit scope, findings, and recommendations.

**Functional safety assessment report:** the final assessment conclusion: "do we have reason to believe this item achieves functional safety".

**Safety case summary:** a continuously updated document that argues, at a summary level, how functional safety was achieved. It does not replace the detailed technical documents, but it gives management and external assessors an overview.

These work products form a complete evidence chain. If someone challenges whether your system is safe, you don't hand over a single report. You hand over a chain that runs from plan to review to audit to assessment.

## An Impact Analysis Walkthrough: An Old EPS in a New Model

Let's work through a concrete exercise. This is a real scenario you will very likely meet as a safety manager.

**Scenario:** three years ago your company developed an EPS system for a B-segment sedan. You did a full HARA and settled on ASIL C. All development activities (system design, hardware design, software design, testing) were completed to ASIL C. The safety case is complete, and the functional safety assessment passed (I2 independent review).

Now marketing decides to use this EPS system in a brand-new A0-segment small SUV. You need to do an impact analysis.

**Step 1: Analyze the changes**

Compare the old and new models:
- Old model: sedan, wheelbase 2700 mm, curb weight 1650 kg, front track 1580 mm, top speed 210 km/h
- New model: small SUV, wheelbase 2500 mm, curb weight 1350 kg, front track 1500 mm, top speed 180 km/h

Differences:
1. Shorter wheelbase and narrower track: the vehicle responds differently to lateral disturbance.
2. Lower weight: for the same torque input, steering response is faster and more sensitive.
3. Different suspension geometry: the steering system's load characteristics differ.
4. Different users: the sedan skews business, the SUV skews younger private owners (possibly including more novice drivers).

**Step 2: Decide whether HARA must be redone**

Are the original HARA assumptions reusable? Let's go parameter by parameter:

- **Parameter S:** the injury severity of a crash caused by unintended steering has little to do with the platform change. Crash safety is mainly set by seat belts, airbags, and body structure, and those belong to the new model. If the new model's passive safety is equal or better, S can be carried over. But it must be confirmed with the new model's crash-test data.
- **Parameter E:** the exposure of the operational situations may change. A small SUV owner may do fewer long highway trips (lower highway exposure), but may spend more time on narrow city streets and in parking garages (more low-speed exposure). Because E ≥ E4 is the most conservative case covered, the original assessment still holds.
- **Parameter C:** this is the parameter most sensitive to a platform change. A shorter wheelbase plus lower weight means the same "unintended lateral torque" produces a faster lateral deviation. A scenario rated C2 before may have to become C3 on the new model. You must also assess whether the new suspension introduces extra response delay or nonlinearity.

Conclusion: **HARA cannot simply be reused.** At minimum, parameter C must be reassessed for the new platform.

**Step 3: Define the additional activities**

Based on the impact analysis, you need to:
1. reassess parameter C (possibly with driving-simulator tests or vehicle dynamics simulation)
2. verify that the existing safety mechanisms are still sufficient under the new model's FTTI (the FTTI may shrink because the platform responds differently)
3. verify the hardware's behavior in the new model's power supply and EMC environment
4. if the ASIL rises because of the platform change (C → D), add the activities specific to ASIL D (such as I3 independent assessment and stricter software tool qualification)
5. run vehicle integration testing on the new model to verify that the safety goals are met in the real environment

**The core lesson of this exercise:** when you port an item that already has a safety case to a new platform, the safety responsibility does not transfer automatically. You must rebuild the safety argument in the new context. ISO 26262 does not accept "this was safe in the last project, therefore it is safe in every project forever". The impact analysis exists to break that assumption.

## Practical Advice for Building Safety Culture

Safety culture is not built with a document. As a safety manager, you can take these practical steps in daily work:

**Make bad news travel fast.** Encourage engineers to report a problem the moment they find it, instead of quietly fixing it and leaving no trace. A potential safety issue reported in time is a gift. It gives you time to assess and respond. A hidden problem can become a trap at any moment.

**Make safety review a conversation, not an approval.** Don't let it become the safety manager stamping a form. A review should be a technical conversation: the reviewer challenges, the author defends the design decision. If no one raises an objection in a review, either the reviewer didn't look carefully or the culture doesn't allow objections. Both are dangerous.

**Record decisions, not just conclusions.** The document should say not only "what we decided to do", but also "what alternatives we considered" and "why we ruled them out". Five years later, when a new engineer takes over the code, she shouldn't have to ask "why is it designed this way?" The answer should be in the document.

**Run post-mortems on real failure data.** Every field failure and every bug found in internal testing should be reviewed with the team, asking three questions: why wasn't this failure caught earlier? Did our HARA foresee this scenario? Why didn't our safety mechanism stop it? Real failure data teaches more than any training course.


## Safety Audits: Five Common Findings You Might Miss

Drawing on a lot of project audit experience, these are the most common findings. Use the list to check your own project:

1. **The safety plan was never updated as the project progressed.** The plan was created at kickoff and never touched again. But the project changed: requirements changed, the team changed, the timeline changed. If the safety plan is still last year's version, it is not a management tool. It's an archived artifact.

2. **Confirmation review independence is below par.** The review record shows the confirmation reviewer's name in the author list of the document under review. Or the reviewer is a direct subordinate. That makes independence a dead letter. Confirmation reviews must meet the I0–I3 independence requirements.

3. **The safety case becomes decoration after version two.** The first version was detailed and careful. The second version changed only the date and version number on the cover. A safety case must stay alive. It should be updated continuously to reflect the current state of the safety argument.

4. **Impact-analysis triggers are applied too casually.** A BOM change on a chip is judged "no safety impact" with no rationale recorded. An external software library goes from 2.0 to 2.1, is labeled a "minor change", and skips the impact analysis. Every change decision needs a written rationale, even when the conclusion is "no impact".

5. **Safety communication is a ritual.** The safety manager sends a weekly "safety status update" to the project manager, and the project manager never replies or objects. That means either the update carries no real information, or the project manager doesn't understand what safety status means. Safety communication should not be one-way reporting. It should be a two-way technical conversation.

## Back to the Immune System: Safety Management Is Immune Memory

**Safety culture = immune memory.** An organization's safety culture is its collective memory of past safety incidents. It is the experience, lessons, and behavior patterns a company has distilled from meeting safety challenges. Your immune system learns and builds memory through every infection. It doesn't even need a real infection: a vaccine (case studies of other companies' accidents) is enough to get it ready.

**Safety manager = central immune regulator.** The safety manager doesn't write safety code, but is responsible for making the parts work together, respond as one, and skip no critical step. If immune cells are engineers, the safety manager is a regulatory T cell. It doesn't kill pathogens itself, but it organizes and coordinates the whole immune response.

**Confirmation review = a second opinion.** The doctor says you need major surgery. You don't sign immediately. Chances are you find an expert at another hospital to confirm. Not because you distrust the first doctor, but because the person making the decision and the person reviewing it cannot be the same, especially when a wrong call costs a life. ISO 26262 institutionalizes the second opinion through independence levels I0 to I3.

**Impact analysis = immune cross-reactivity assessment.** You were vaccinated against one flu strain, and now a new strain appears. Will the vaccine still protect you? You analyze it (impact analysis) instead of assuming "if I can resist the old flu, I can resist the new one". ISO 26262 requires any existing item, before it enters a new system, to pass an impact analysis showing it is still safe enough in the new context of use.


## Summary

1. Safety culture is not a slogan on the wall: the test is what the team does when nobody is watching
2. The safety manager needs authority and an escalation path; the independence requirement falls on the confirmation measures
3. Confirmation measures are three lines of defense: confirmation review → safety audit → safety assessment
4. Independence levels I0 to I3: the ASIL D functional safety assessment must be I3 independent
5. Impact analysis is where your safety responsibility for "an item someone else built" begins
