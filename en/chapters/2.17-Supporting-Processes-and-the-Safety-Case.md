# 2.17 Supporting Processes and the Safety Case: If It Isn't Written Down, It Didn't Happen


## Scenario: the assessor puts down his pen

June 2025, Munich. A senior functional safety assessor from TÜV SÜD walks into your review room. He has done ISO 26262 assessments here for 16 years and has seen the safety cases of 125 projects, 31 of which were rejected at the first review. You sent him the electronic package of your safety case 48 hours in advance. Today he will put your ASIL D steering system under the microscope.

He does not open your code repository. He does not look at your Simulink models. He does not run your hardware test bench.

He opens an Excel file. Your **Safety Case matrix**.

Then he points at row 174: "HARA #17: loss of steering assistance when the driver requests steering torque (ASIL D). Show me the audit chain from this hazardous event to the verification report for TSR-042."

Your safety engineer flips through pages for 5 minutes. The FSR-031 → TSR-042 traceability is in the traceability matrix on the left side of the V model. The software unit test report for TSR-042... is on some Confluence page, but the link is dead. The hardware SPFM calculation report is in another shared folder, but the file name doesn't contain the TSR number, so it can only be inferred from the creation date.

The assessor puts down his pen. "Gentlemen," he says, "I see a lot of evidence. But I don't see one complete chain. If traceability cannot be verified at review time, I cannot confirm that it was actually performed during development."

The review is suspended. This is not a technical failure. It is a failure of process transparency.

**In functional safety, no record means it never happened.**

ISO 26262-8 (supporting processes) and the safety-case requirements in ISO 26262-2 together define this iron rule. It does not sit at any layer of the V model. It sits behind the V model, running through every layer, making sure that what one layer "said it would do" is what the next layer "actually did," and that the fact of having done it can be independently verified by any third-party auditor at any time.


## Configuration management: the first anchor chain for every safety artifact

If your design team has 12 software engineers, 8 hardware engineers, and 6 systems engineers, all working at the same time on HARA, the functional safety concept, the technical safety concept, the software architecture, and the hardware architecture, how do you make sure everyone is using the same version of the assumptions?

How do you make sure the software team, when developing the MC/DC test cases for TSR-042, is working from the ASIL screening rules updated three days ago and not last week's version?

How do you make sure the hardware team, when calculating SPFM, cites the JEDEC JESD89E failure rate database and not the expired JESD89C?

The answer is **configuration management (CM)**. ISO 26262-8 makes configuration management a standalone supporting-process clause, and its requirements are systematic.

Configuration management rests on three concepts: **baseline, change control, and traceability**.

A **baseline** is "a set of work products frozen at a specific point in time." You must establish baselines at the key milestones of the project: after the safety plan review, after HARA approval, after safety concept approval, after technical safety concept approval, after hardware detailed design, after software unit testing, and after vehicle verification. Each baseline records which work products (documents, models, code, test cases, test reports) at which versions together formed one "slice" of the safety argument at that moment.

Why establish baselines? Imagine no baseline. You submit the final HARA in November 2024, then make a small adjustment to the ASIL of one hazardous event in December — while the functional safety concept (FSC) team finished all its work at the end of November based on the "old HARA." If that difference goes unnoticed, a safety goal at ASIL C may be implemented to ASIL A requirements: the entire safety concept has slipped two levels. A baseline is your "synchronization point." It records versions, but more than that, it imposes a process rule: "before baseline B is released, every downstream activity that depends on it must be performed against B, and no non-baseline version before or after B may be used."

**Change control** is the mechanism that keeps baselines consistent. ISO 26262-8 requires: for any change to a safety-related element that has been baselined, an **impact analysis** must be performed. Impact analysis is not simply "one line of code changed, so the corresponding unit test is affected." It traces upward along the dependency chain:

* If the torque sensor sampling rate changes from 1 kHz to 800 Hz, the impact analysis must assess:
  * Effect on the TSR: the FTTI is 200 ms, a 1 kHz sampling rate gives a maximum detection delay of 1 ms, and 800 Hz gives 1.25 ms — is that still within the FTTI? Check your timing budget table.
  * Effect on the software architecture: the ADC DMA interrupt frequency changes from 1 kHz to 800 Hz. What does that do to the schedule table? Could a task miss its deadline because the interrupt rate dropped?
  * Effect on the hardware design: does the ADC sampling capacitor value need to change because of the new sampling frequency?
  * Effect on the HARA: does the lower sampling rate change the calculation of hazard exposure time, and therefore the ASIL determination?

That is the breadth of change impact analysis. It crosses every layer of the V model. And the result of the impact analysis must itself be recorded and reviewed. Do not underestimate the act of "recording": an impact analysis that is not recorded was not performed.

**Traceability** is the final output of configuration management. It answers one question: "Can I walk from a hazardous event to the corresponding test case, and from the test case back to the hazardous event?" ISO 26262-8 sets out traceability requirements between safety requirements: from safety goal to functional safety requirement, from functional safety requirement to technical safety requirement, from technical safety requirement to hardware/software safety requirement, and from hardware/software safety requirement to verification test cases. This is a **bidirectional traceability chain**: traceability must go forward (from requirement to implementation) and backward (from test back to requirement).

In large projects, bidirectional traceability is usually managed with a traceability matrix. For an ASIL D system, that matrix may contain thousands of rows: each HARA hazardous event maps to several safety goals, each safety goal to several FSRs, each FSR to several TSRs, each TSR to several software requirements (SWRs)/hardware requirements (HWRs), and each requirement to several test cases. Every row in the matrix must carry a "verified" flag, meaning the corresponding test has been executed and passed.


## Document management: the bones and flesh of the safety argument

If the safety argument is your "speech" — proving to the assessor, the OEM, and the regulator that the system is safe enough — then the work products that support it are the "evidence." A speech without evidence is empty. Evidence without organization is useless.

ISO 26262-8 makes document management a standalone supporting-process clause and requires a **document management system** that clearly defines:

1. **Work product templates**: every type of safety work product — HARA report, FSC, TSC, hardware safety analysis report, software safety analysis report, verification report, safety case summary — must have a standardized template. A template is not just a formatting tool; it is a **completeness checklist**. Every section heading in the template reminds you "this item cannot be omitted."

2. **Review and approval workflow**: safety work products must go through confirmation review. ISO 26262-2 defines four independence levels: I0 (should be performed; if performed, it must be done by someone other than the creator), I1 (must be done by someone other than the creator), I2 (must be done by someone independent of the creation team, meaning they do not report to the same direct manager), and I3 (must be done by a department independent of the creating department in management, resources, and release authority). For an ASIL D safety case, confirmation review must reach I3. The reviewer's signature, review comments, and conclusion (pass / pass with conditions / fail) must be archived.

3. **Version control and release management**: every safety work product must have an explicit version number, revision history, author, approver, and effective date. When someone cites "FSR-031 v2.1," everyone can know exactly what that version contains.

4. **Document structure classification**: ISO 26262-8 groups work products into several classes: safety planning (project safety management plan, DIA, and so on), analysis (HARA, FMEA, FTA, DFA), design (safety concept, architecture design), verification (test plan, test report), and summary (safety case). Each class has its own storage location, access rights, and retention period.

On retention periods, ISO 26262 does not state a figure directly, but OEM purchasing terms usually require the safety case to be retained for at least 15 years after end of production (EOP). That means your HARA document from today must still be readable, accessible, and understandable in 2039. So: don't keep safety documents in personal folders. Don't use a server without backups. Don't use document formats that are no longer supported. If you track your traceability matrix with Office 2019 macros today, make sure someone can still open that format in 2039.


## Software tool qualification: who vouches for your toolchain?

Has the Simulink model compiler you use to generate code been certified to ISO 26262? Can your static analysis tool (Polyspace) guarantee it won't miss a division-by-zero in your code? Can your HIL test platform inject fault signals with millisecond timing accuracy?

**Software tool qualification** is the domain of ISO 26262-8. The basic logic: if a tool's output is used directly as evidence in the safety argument — for example, the binary the compiler produces is the code that actually runs — then the tool's reliability directly affects the credibility of the safety argument. If the tool makes a mistake, your evidence is contaminated.

ISO 26262-8 assigns tools to three confidence levels (**tool confidence level, TCL**):

* **TCL1**: the tool cannot introduce safety-related errors, or its output is verified by another independent tool. A script used only for format checking, for example. At worst it tells you "the indentation is wrong"; it cannot affect a safety function.

* **TCL2**: the tool can introduce safety-related errors, but its output can be detected. A model-to-code generator (Embedded Coder), for example. If it wrongly generates a subtraction where an addition was intended, you will almost certainly catch it in software unit testing through MC/DC coverage, because the expected output will not match the actual output. Qualification for a TCL2 tool includes: documenting known problems and usage restrictions in the tool user manual, defining the tool's safe use cases, and recording the tool configuration and use cases in the project.

* **TCL3**: the tool can introduce safety-related errors, and such errors **cannot** or can only with **extreme difficulty** be detected in later testing. This is the highest risk level. A classic example is a fault injection tool: the tool claims "the torque sensor signal has been injected as an open-circuit fault," but what it actually injected is a short to ground. You cannot distinguish the two faults in routine HIL testing, because the system response may be the same (output torque goes to 0). A TCL3 tool must undergo the strictest qualification: the qualification certificate provided by the tool supplier (the tool qualification requirements of ISO 26262:2018), an assessment of the tool safety manual, historical data from projects that used the tool, and possibly independent verification testing.

The decision flow for tool classification is a key process you need to go through:

1. List every software tool used in the project: compiler, linker, code generator, static analysis tool, unit test framework, HIL script engine, CAN communication test tool...
2. Perform TI and TD analysis for each tool, then look up the table to get the TCL:
   * TI (tool impact): TI1 applies when there is an argument that "the tool failing and introducing or failing to detect an error in the item or element under development" is not possible; TI2 applies to everything else.
   * TD (tool error detection): TD1 applies when there is high confidence that "the failure and its erroneous output will be prevented or detected"; TD2 for medium confidence; TD3 for everything else.
   * Lookup rule: the TI1 row is TCL1 in all three cells; in the TI2 row, TD1 maps to TCL1, TD2 to TCL2, and TD3 to TCL3.
3. For TCL2 and TCL3 tools, choose the appropriate qualification method and perform the qualification activities.

In real projects, tool qualification is often underestimated. You may think "I use mainstream industry tools, there won't be a problem." But the assessor wants evidence, not a feeling. If you cannot say which TCL level your compiler falls under, or whether it has been qualified, he will write a weakness in the assessment report, or even a non-conformity.


## Software component qualification and distributed development

ISO 26262-8 also covers two important topics: **qualification of software components** and **interfaces within distributed developments**.

**Software component qualification** answers one question: can I take existing software — a CAN protocol stack used in an earlier project, an AES crypto library taken from an open-source community, a motor control algorithm supplied by a vendor — and use it directly in my ASIL D system?

The answer is yes, with conditions. ISO 26262-8 defines the requirements for "reuse of a software component with safety evidence":

* You must perform a **gap analysis** on the reused component: it met ASIL X (say ASIL B) in its previous application, but now you want to use it in an ASIL D environment. Where is the gap? Is additional MC/DC coverage required? Are there extra timing constraints? Is a deeper code review required?
* You must assess the component's suitability in its **new operating environment**: the AES library was previously used to encrypt sensor data (ASIL A), and now you want to use it to encrypt critical safety messages (ASIL D). The key management, side-channel attack protection, and self-test coverage required are completely different.
* If you plan to strengthen the case with **service history** — how many vehicles has this component run in? For how many hours? How many relevant failures occurred? — that is the separate proven-in-use path. Software component qualification is about evidence of suitability for the reused component.

**Interfaces within distributed development** deal with the boundary of responsibility between OEM and supplier. In a typical automotive program, the OEM defines the functional safety concept and the vehicle-level safety goals, the Tier-1 is responsible for the technical safety concept and system design, and the Tier-2 supplies chips and basic software. Every link in that chain carries its own safety responsibility. But without clear interface agreements, safety leaks through the gaps.

ISO 26262 requires the OEM and each Tier-1 supplier to sign a **development interface agreement (DIA)**. The DIA must clearly define:
* Each party's safety responsibilities: who is responsible for which part of the safety analysis, which part of verification, and which part of safety case maintenance;
* Distribution of safety requirements: the OEM hands FSRs to the Tier-1, and the Tier-1 derives TSRs from them. If the Tier-1 believes an FSR is technically impossible (for example, "the EPS must enter the safe state within 50 ms of an antenna failure," but the communication cycle is 100 ms, so it is physically impossible), the DIA must describe the escalation/negotiation mechanism;
* The exchange format, exchange frequency, and acceptance criteria for work products: when the Tier-1 delivers safety case fragments to the OEM, how does the OEM verify that those fragments comply with ISO 26262?
* Independence requirements for safety assessment: can the Tier-1's internal safety review be accepted by the OEM, or must a third-party assessor independently review the Tier-1's work?

A DIA is not just a signed piece of paper. It is the "constitution" governing safety responsibility between OEM and supplier. When something goes wrong, the DIA tells each party "who was supposed to do what," and "who did not do it."


## The safety case: where all evidence finally lands

Now let's pull all the threads back. Configuration management keeps every work product version-consistent. Change management ensures every modification has an impact analysis. Document management ensures all work products exist in a uniform format, adequately reviewed, and properly archived. Tool qualification ensures that the tools generating and verifying those work products are themselves trustworthy. Software component qualification ensures that reused "foreign objects" don't break the integrity of the safety argument. Distributed development interfaces ensure that safety responsibility across the supply chain connects without gaps.

All of this converges into one thing: the **safety case**.

ISO 26262-2 defines the safety case as a "continuous, structured, argumentative communication medium" containing the **safety argument** and the **work products** that support it.

The structure of the safety argument is logical: a tree of "claim — sub-claim — evidence." The top-level claim is: "This system achieves an acceptable level of risk with respect to the safety goals throughout the vehicle lifecycle." It is expanded layer by layer through these sub-claims:

1. **Hazard analysis is correct and sufficiently complete**: evidence: HARA report, FMEA report, DFA report, hazardous event list and its review records;
2. **The safety concept achieves sufficient risk reduction**: evidence: FSC, TSC, safety mechanism definitions, redundancy design documents;
3. **The hardware architecture meets the quantitative targets for random hardware failures**: evidence: SPFM/LFM calculation sheets, PMHF calculation sheet, hardware detailed design documents, failure rate database references;
4. **The software architecture achieves independence from single faults**: evidence: FFI analysis report, software architecture diagrams, ASIL decomposition and coexistence argument;
5. **Hardware/software verification covers every TSR**: evidence: test plan, test cases, test results (pass/fail), open defect list, regression test records;
6. **System integration and vehicle verification confirm the safety goals**: evidence: integration test report, HIL test results, vehicle road test data;
7. **Safety in production, operation, and decommissioning is also managed**: evidence: EOL test specification, field monitoring process, safety chapters of the service manual.

When the assessor opens the safety case, he can see the complete structure of this "argument tree." He can click any sub-claim and drill down to its evidence layer: see the version number, author, reviewer, approval date, and the exact data of the test results. He can trace backward: see which TSR a test case covers, which FSR that traces to, and ultimately which hazardous event in the HARA.

**That is the real meaning of "if it isn't written down, it didn't happen."** It is not a slogan. It is the only objective way ISO 26262 has to judge the integrity of your engineering logic system. You may have the world's most advanced fault detection algorithm and the most elegant triple-redundant architecture — but if an independent third party cannot fully understand and verify them at any time using only your safety case, then in the world of assessment they do not exist.


## The immune metaphor: organizational memory and vaccination records

If the earlier parts of this book described the immune system's "effector mechanisms" — how T cells recognize antigen, how antibodies neutralize viruses — then Part 8 describes the **immune system's organizational memory**.

* **Configuration management** ≈ the immune system's gene rearrangement records. B cells and T cells undergo V(D)J recombination as they mature, generating an astronomical diversity of antigen receptors. Every rearrangement is precisely "recorded" in the cell's DNA: it cannot be lost or confused. Configuration management ensures that the state of every safety-related work product at every point in time is recorded. You can "replay" the safety argument at any moment of the project.

* **Change management** ≈ the immune system's tolerance mechanism. A mature immune system distinguishes "self" from "non-self": it maintains immune tolerance to the body's own tissue and does not attack. Change impact analysis is that tolerance mechanism applied to functional safety: for every change you introduce, the system must assess whether it will cause an "autoimmune attack" (some part of the safety argument being damaged unintentionally). If so, the assessment mechanism triggers "immune rejection": reject the change, or require the affected safety activities to be completed before the change.

* **Document management** ≈ the vaccination record booklet. Before traveling to Africa you need a yellow fever vaccination, and the doctor stamps your little yellow book with the vaccine batch number, vaccination date, and expiry date. ISO 26262 document management is that "little yellow book." It does not directly protect you from the virus, but it proves that you really were vaccinated (did the safety analysis), and that the vaccine is still within its validity period (the work product has been reviewed and approved).

* **Tool qualification** ≈ the registration approval of a diagnostic test kit. A PCR test kit must go through clinical validation (what sensitivity and specificity percentages it achieves) before it can be approved for disease diagnosis. A code generator must go through tool qualification (be shown not to introduce undetectable errors under defined conditions of use) before it can be used to produce safety-related code.

* **The safety case** ≈ proof of the immune system's status. Before competing internationally, an athlete must submit a medical report, vaccination certificates, and doping test results. The safety case is functional safety's "medical report." It proves to the OEM, the regulator, and society: "this system has received adequate vaccination and health checks and can operate safely on public roads."


## Summary

1. Configuration management is the time anchor of the safety argument
2. Document management is the carrier of the safety argument
3. Tool qualification prevents the "detection tool" itself from failing
4. Software component qualification ensures that reused "foreign objects" are compatible with the new environment
5. The DIA is the constitution of safety responsibility across the supply chain
6. The safety case is where all activities finally converge
