# 2.16 Production, Operation, and Decommissioning: Safety Is Not Turnkey


## Scenario: a steering failure in car number 10,001

March 2025. As functional safety manager, you have just signed the last SOP (Start of Production) release document. The electric power steering system for a battery-electric SUV: from HARA to safety verification, two years and four months, every review passed. The End-of-Line test benches on the production line are running at full speed, rolling a car off every 76 seconds. The first batch of 10,000 units has shipped to 128 dealers nationwide. You sip your coffee, look at the ASIL D certificate issued by TÜV, and feel you can finally relax.

On day 43, an urgent email from the after-sales quality department breaks the calm.

A car with 15,230 km on the odometer. The owner was taking a highway ramp to the right at 62 km/h when the EPS suddenly lost assistance for 3.2 s. Steering wheel torque jumped from 4.8 Nm to 21.3 Nm in an instant — as if the steering had switched from electric assistance to pure mechanical mode. Fortunately the driver wrestled the wheel back and there was no collision. But the car reproduced the fault twice more within the next 24 hours, and the fault code pointed to a torque sensor SPI communication timeout.

Your safety team moves in at once. Reading the ECU's NVM (non-volatile memory) fault log, you find the root cause: the solder joint on the torque sensor chip had been growing a microcrack under the combined action of temperature cycling (-40 °C to 85 °C, 4.7 full cycles per day) and continuous vibration (3.2 Grms, covering the engine-bay spectrum). At around 15,000 km — several hundred hours of accumulated operation at typical city speeds — the crack caused an intermittent open circuit.

But that isn't the problem. The problem is: **your EOL testing and design verification never covered this failure mode at all.**

In the design verification (DV) phase you ran 1,200 hours of temperature cycling and 300 hours of random vibration — but you ran them separately. You never superimposed vibration and temperature cycling, while the real vehicle environment has both at once. The EOL test only ran the 15-second power-on self-test: nowhere near enough to detect an intermittent open circuit that needs accumulated thermal cycling to appear.

A safety argument is not a turnkey project. SOP is where continuous responsibility begins. Part 7 of ISO 26262 exists to solve this problem: from production to scrapping, safety must be continuously managed and maintained across the whole lifecycle.


## Production control: safety comes from manufacturing as much as design

ISO 26262-7 requires: for every product or process with safety-related characteristics, **product-specific production requirements** must be defined. The subtext is: a good design can be ruined by bad production.

Take the torque sensor from the previous example. For an ASIL D torque sensor, the soldering process parameters (reflow temperature profile, solder paste stencil thickness, X-ray sampling ratio) must be written explicitly into the production control plan. If the IPC Class 2 soldering standard cannot meet the safety requirement — and usually it cannot; for BGA or QFN sensor chips, ASIL D systems demand IPC Class 3 or even stricter joint quality — then you must specify the higher standard in the control plan and prove with process capability indices (Cpk ≥ 1.67) that the line can hold it consistently.

Safety-related characteristics in the production process must be identified and controlled. These include, but are not limited to:

* **Electrical characteristics**: sensor supply voltage ripple, ADC reference voltage accuracy, communication interface eye-diagram margin;
* **Mechanical characteristics**: PCB solder joint strength, connector insertion and locking force, compression of the waterproof seal;
* **Software characteristics**: Flash programming checksum, read-back verification after calibration parameters are written, signature verification of safety firmware.

Each characteristic needs a matching process control method: SPC (statistical process control) charts, poke-yoke fixtures, automated optical inspection (AOI), in-line functional testing — and all of it must be recorded and traceable at the work-station level.

More critical is **EOL (End-of-Line) testing**. The purpose of EOL testing is to decide quickly at the end of production whether this controller has safety integrity. But it is not a substitute for design verification. EOL coverage and time budget are constrained by takt time (76 seconds per unit, for example), so you must choose carefully:

1. **Integrity testing of critical signal paths**: exercise the torque sensor over its full range (say ±10 Nm, checking linearity and symmetry at five points), read the digital output through the ECU, and compare with the reference value;
2. **Safety shutdown path testing**: inject a fault signal deliberately and verify that the EPS can enter the safe state within the FTTI (Fault Tolerant Time Interval, 200 ms in this case);
3. **Memory integrity testing**: Flash/EEPROM factory checksum, read/write testing of the NVM region;
4. **Safety mechanism activation testing**: let the watchdog (window watchdog) actually time out once and confirm that the MCU resets correctly.

Pay special attention: EOL testing cannot test only the "good case." It must deliberately inject faults to verify the response of the safety mechanisms. An EOL test that verifies normal functions but not fault responses is shipping cars that are already sick.

One more real data point: a Tier-1 supplier once reported that after adding EOL fault injection testing, its brake system ECU line caught about 0.37% production escapes. Those controllers all passed normal functional testing, but the safety-mechanism activation tests exposed problems (for example, a watchdog configuration register occasionally lost its contents during fast power cycling). 0.37% means one in every 270 units "passed while sick." Without safety-oriented EOL testing, those defects go straight to the end user.

Design says "the system is safe in theory." Production says "the system is safe in practice." Miss either one, and you cannot withstand the risks of the real world.


## Operation: the main battlefield of safety responsibility

From SOP to scrapping, a car runs for 15 years and 200,000 km on average. Over those 15 years, safety is not proven in a lab. It is proven every morning when parents drive their children to school, every late night when a truck driver is on the road, every moment on a rainy day when ESP intervenes. The operation, maintenance, and decommissioning part of ISO 26262-7 turns these real-world scenarios into normative requirements.

**Safety information in the owner's manual** is a badly underestimated safety mechanism. ISO 26262-7 is explicit: the owner's manual must contain safety-related operating information. Have you seen the EPS fault lamp on the dashboard? The yellow steering wheel symbol. Its illumination logic, display timing, and accompanying text message ("steering assistance fault, stop the vehicle immediately") all need to be defined as safety mechanisms in the HARA, and the owner's manual must explain the meaning and the correct response to the end user.

The owner's manual must also contain:
* Identification and operating instructions for every safety warning indicator;
* Required maintenance intervals and safety-related items (such as the calibration check of the torque sensor);
* Restrictions on vehicle modification (for example, fitting a non-original steering tie rod can change the torque transfer characteristics and therefore affect the EPS fault detection threshold);
* Correct use of safety functions (for example, the activation conditions and driver-monitoring requirements of LKA lane keeping).

These are not legal disclaimers. They are the ISO 26262 safety concept landing at the "human-vehicle interaction" level.

**Field monitoring** is the most important safety activity in the operation phase. ISO 26262-7 requires the organization to establish a systematic field monitoring process that collects and analyzes safety-related events occurring in vehicles in service. This is not just "watching the complaint rate." It is building an active surveillance network like the immune system's memory T cells.

A complete field monitoring system includes:

1. **Data collection layer**: on-board diagnostic trouble codes (DTCs), safety event snapshots (freeze-frame data: vehicle state for the 5 seconds before the fault), and on-board diagnostic data (the calibration verification code in OBD-II mode 9) are sent back through the telematics unit (T-box);
2. **Screening and classification layer**: a safety event classification engine automatically identifies which DTCs are relevant to functional safety. For example, the EPS torque sensor communication timeout DTC maps directly to an ASIL D safety goal and gets the highest priority;
3. **Trend analysis layer**: statistics on the occurrence rate of the same DTC in the same model, the same batch, and the same mileage band. If the torque sensor communication timeout rate for a batch rises significantly around 15,000 km (p<0.01), a quality alert fires immediately;
4. **Root cause analysis layer**: recall or sample the failed parts and perform physical failure analysis (scanning electron microscopy of the joint cross-section, X-ray tomography), combined with vehicle logs to reconstruct the failure sequence;
5. **Closed-loop feedback layer**: feed the conclusions back to the design team (does the FTA/DFMEA need updating, does the safety concept need revision), the production team (does the EOL test need new items), and the customer/OEM (is a service action or recall needed).

**Incident reporting** is the output of field monitoring. ISO 26262-7 requires: any safety-related event found in the field must be escalated through defined interfaces, from OEM to supplier or from dealer to OEM. The report must contain at least: a description of the event, the vehicle identification information involved, the fault codes, the environmental conditions, the severity of the consequences, and a preliminary cause analysis. The timeline is critical: the window from the OEM learning of the event to notifying the supplier is usually no more than 72 hours.

**Repair and service** also has specific safety requirements. When a safety-related part is replaced (for example, the entire steering gear), regression verification must be performed: confirm that the torque sensor output is still within the calibrated range after replacement (tolerance ±0.1 Nm) and that the steering angle sensor zero position has been learned correctly. The service manual must mark safety-related repair steps and verification methods in bold, highlighted, or warning-symbol form. And the post-repair EOL test must be equivalent to the factory EOL test: a dealer cannot substitute a simplified test for the full production-line test.


## Decommissioning and scrapping: the last mile of safety

It sounds remote, but ISO 26262-7 does cover it: **decommissioning**. A battery-electric car carrying a high-voltage battery, airbag systems, and brake-by-wire still holds enough energy to kill when it is dismantled for scrapping.

The safety requirements include:
* The discharge procedure for the high-voltage system must be stated explicitly in the service manual, including waiting times and residual voltage measurement steps;
* Dismantling rules for the airbag deployment system: an unintended trigger can seriously injure the technician;
* Handling of ECUs that contain safety-related data (such as the event data recorder, EDR, and EPS calibration data in NVM). This data may serve as evidence in accident analysis and must be retained or exported as required by regulation;
* Recycling of the lithium battery: avoid thermal runaway during dismantling.

Decommissioning is not the end of safety responsibility. It is the last delivery point of that responsibility: remove the physical hazard from the product safely, and dispose of or preserve the information assets as required.


## The immune metaphor: maintaining adaptive immunity

Back to the immune metaphor that runs through this chapter.

If the HARA, FMEA, and safety concept in the design phase are the immune system's **adaptive immunity** — vaccination that lets the system "remember" every known pathogen (hazard/failure mode) and build specific antibodies (safety mechanisms) — then production, operation, and decommissioning correspond to **immune surveillance and immune maintenance**.

* **Production** ≈ the differentiation and selection of immune cells. T cells (ECUs) produced in the bone marrow (the production line) must pass strict selection in the thymus (EOL testing), ensuring they recognize foreign antigen (can detect faults) without attacking the body's own tissue (no false safety actions or false alarms). Cells that fail the standard (non-conforming controllers) are eliminated by programmed cell death (scrapped or reworked).

* **Operation** ≈ long-range patrol by memory T cells. Every car sold is like a memory T cell distributed throughout the body, continuously monitoring the local microenvironment (integrity of sensor signals, bit error rate of communication links, plausibility of torque output). As soon as "antigen recurrence" is detected (a fault signal matching a known failure mode), it triggers an "immune response" (enter the safe state: degrade assistance / warn / cut power) and reports to the "immune system center" (the OEM/Tier-1 field monitoring center).

* **Repair and recall** ≈ booster vaccination. When field data reveals a failure mode that FMEA did not cover — as if a new viral variant appeared — the OEM must quickly update "immune memory" (revise the safety concept, update software calibration parameters) and inject the updated "vaccine" into every affected vehicle through a recall.

* **Decommissioning/scrapping** ≈ the orderly dissolution of the immune system. Controlled airbag deployment, safe discharge of the high-voltage system, harmless processing of the lithium battery. This resembles the apoptosis program of aging cells in the body: remove the danger without triggering a cytokine storm (without causing secondary harm).

* **Field monitoring and incident reporting** ≈ the epidemiological surveillance network. The WHO's global outbreak monitoring system (GOARN) collects case data from around the world, identifies unusual outbreak patterns, and triggers rapid response. The OEM's field monitoring system has the same structure and function. Without that network, public health flies blind; without field monitoring, the functional safety argument is a one-time promise that loses its validity the moment SOP is signed.

You may ask: why does ISO 26262 devote an entire Part 7 to production and operation? Why not assume "design is done, production just follows the drawings"?

Because: **the decay of safety integrity is certain, not accidental.**

Every physical system degrades in the real environment. Solder joints develop low-cycle fatigue cracks under temperature cycling. The equivalent series resistance (ESR) of capacitors rises year by year at high temperature. The charge retention of memory cells weakens under irradiation and temperature. These degradation mechanisms can be predicted in design-phase FMEA (you listed "solder joint fatigue" as a failure cause), but the degradation rate, threshold, and triggering conditions are probabilistic. Only continuous field monitoring lets you pin those parameters down.

More importantly: **some factors in the operating environment simply cannot be fully foreseen at the design stage.** You won't know that owners in one region like to fit thick plush covers on the steering wheel, keeping the torque sensor's operating temperature 3 °C higher all year. You won't know that one batch of PCB material has a glass transition temperature Tg 5 °C below nominal, so the stress concentration point in the solder joint appears earlier. You won't know that an owner's aftermarket daytime running lights route their power cable so that the electromagnetic interference lands exactly in the sensitive band of the torque sensor's SPI communication.

These "unknown unknowns" can only be found in the field. And the point of ISO 26262-7 is that it makes the closed loop of "find → report → analyze → correct → regression verify" a mandatory requirement rather than a suggestion.

## Summary

1. The production control plan must cover every safety-related characteristic, with Cpk ≥ 1.67
2. EOL testing must include fault injection verification
3. The owner's manual is an extension of the safety concept
4. Field monitoring keeps the safety argument valid over time
5. Incident reporting runs a 72-hour chain from the vehicle to the OEM to the supplier
6. Decommissioning safety requirements are just as serious: high-voltage discharge, airbag handling, data retention
