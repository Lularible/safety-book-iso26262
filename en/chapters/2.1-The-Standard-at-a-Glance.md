# 2.1 The Standard at a Glance, and Its Vocabulary

Chapter 1 went from Gödel to alpha particles and gave you a philosophical framework for thinking about functional safety. But what an engineer ultimately faces is not a philosophical proposition. It's clauses in a standard.

You finally made up your mind and downloaded all 12 parts of ISO 26262:2018 from the ISO website. Twelve PDFs sit neatly on your desktop, like twelve closed doors. You take a deep breath and double-click Part 1.

Five minutes later you are lost in a maze of terms: "item", "element", "fault", "error", "failure", "hazard", "hazardous event". Every definition cites another term, nested like Russian dolls. You open Part 3 to see how HARA is done, and find that it cites the vocabulary in Part 1, depends on the management process in Part 2, and pulls in ASIL tailoring from Part 9. What you are looking at is not a standalone document. It's one node on an enormous spider web.

That's the shock ISO 26262 delivers to every newcomer: it isn't a textbook you read from cover to cover. It's a city you can only enter once you understand its overall architecture.

## One City: Twelve Districts

Let's imagine ISO 26262:2018 as an immune city. The twelve districts are not laid out in a line. They form a three-dimensional V: the design side on the left, the verification side on the right, and shared support at the bottom.

**Part 1: Dictionary (vocabulary).** This is the city's language center. Every term used in the other districts is defined here. Without it you can't take a step anywhere else. It defines what an item is, what an element is, what a hazard is. It's the immune system's "cell identification manual": a T cell must first know what is "self" and what is "non-self" before it can do its job.

**Part 2: City Hall (functional safety management).** The city's control center. It tells you who is responsible for safety, who has signing authority, how to build a safety culture, and how to write a safety plan. It's the immune system's "central regulation": without management, even a huge population of immune cells is just a rabble.

**Part 3: Scout Battalion (concept phase).** This is where safety activity starts. HARA (hazard analysis and risk assessment) is done here, safety goals are defined here, and the functional safety concept is conceived here. It corresponds to the immune system's "pathogen recognition" stage. First you have to work out who the enemy is, how dangerous it is, and where it comes from.

**Part 4: City-Wall Defense (system-level product development).** System architecture design, the technical safety concept, and safety mechanism integration happen here. It's the physical defense line formed by the wall, the moat, and the watchtower. System integration testing also happens in this part, which is the acceptance inspection of the fortifications.

**Part 5: Cellular Defense (hardware-level product development).** Hardware safety requirements, hardware design, hardware architectural metrics (SPFM/LFM), and the probabilistic metric for random hardware failures (PMHF) all live here. This is the most microscopic layer of the immune system: how a single cell resists a pathogen. An alpha particle flips an SRAM bit? That's an "infection" at the cellular level, and it needs a hardware mechanism to detect and repair it.

**Part 6: Signaling Pathways (software-level product development).** Software safety requirements, software architecture design, software unit design and implementation, software testing. Software is the logic layer above the hardware, like immune signaling pathways: cytokine signaling and signaling cascades. A software bug is a wrong signal: a macrophage should be engulfing a pathogen, but it receives the wrong instruction: "stand down, this one is a friend."

**Part 7: Factory Inspection (production, operation, service, and decommissioning).** Safety-related production processes, monitoring during operation, safety assurance after repair, and safety requirements for decommissioning. It's what happens after a newborn leaves the womb: the immune system has to face the real world. A production deviation can turn a "qualified immune system" into "a defective product that looks normal."

**Part 8: Logistics Support (supporting processes).** Supporting activities that run across the whole safety lifecycle: interface management, configuration management, change management, document management, and qualification of software tools. It's the immune system's "logistics": bone marrow keeps producing blood cells, lymph nodes maintain immune memory.

**Part 9: Grading System (ASIL-oriented and safety-oriented analysis).** ASIL decomposition, tailoring rules for safety analysis, dependent failure analysis. It's "adjusting the strength of the immune response": some threats need a full-body mobilization (ASIL D), others need only local defense (ASIL B).

**Part 10: Travel Guide (guidelines).** These are not normative requirements. They explain and illustrate Parts 2–9. It's the "immune city tourist handbook": how other people do it, and what the common mistakes are.

**Part 11: Semiconductor Special (semiconductor guidelines).** Special considerations for semiconductors: digital components, analog components, memory, sensors. In immune terms, these are "defense strategies for specific cell types": neurons and liver cells face different threats and defend themselves differently.

**Part 12: Motorcycle Special.** ISO 26262:2018 applies to safety-related E/E systems on production road vehicles; mopeds are not covered. Part 12 gives the adaptation requirements for motorcycles. The immune principle is the same, but the "host" is different, so the defense strategy has to be adjusted.

## The V-Model: Two Phases of the Immune Response

The ISO 26262 V-model is a core structure that runs through the whole book. The left arm of the V represents "what you required" (specification/design). The right arm represents "what you proved" (verification/validation).

Imagine the immune system facing a new virus:

**Left arm (downward): recognition and planning**

1. First, the immune system must identify the intruder. This is the "concept phase", which is HARA. You have to answer: who is the enemy (hazard identification)? How big is the threat (severity)? How widely does it spread (exposure)? Can we control it (controllability)?
2. Then you set a defense strategy. This is the "functional safety concept". At the macro level: we need neutralizing antibodies and activated T cells. In a car: we need "enter a safe state when a steering anomaly is detected".
3. Next, break the strategy down to the organ level: "system-level design". The skin provides a physical barrier; the lymphatic system transports immune cells. In a car: the braking system's architecture, redundancy concepts, and allocation of safety mechanisms.
4. Further down, allocate to the cellular and molecular level: "hardware-level design" and "software-level design". In a car: the concrete ECU hardware circuits and embedded software.

**Right arm (upward): verification and validation**

1. Lowest-level verification: "software unit testing" and "hardware module testing". Like an in-vitro experiment: does this antibody really bind the viral protein?
2. Integration verification: "hardware/software integration testing" and "system integration testing". Like animal trials: does the whole immune system work in a living body?
3. Vehicle verification. "Vehicle integration and testing". Like clinical trials: in real driving scenarios, do all the safety mechanisms work together as intended?
4. Final confirmation: "safety validation" and "functional safety assessment". Like FDA approval: based on all the evidence, we are confident the system is safe.

The beauty of the V-model is traceability. Every test on the right arm must trace back to a requirement on the left arm. You can't say "I tested it" and call it done. You must prove that what you tested is what you originally required. Immunology works the same way: you can't just say "I gave the vaccine". You must prove that "the vaccinated person produced effective antibodies against that specific virus."

## The Safety Lifecycle: From Birth to Retirement

The ISO 26262 safety lifecycle covers the entire life of an automotive electronic system and divides it into six phases:

1. **Concept phase:** you do HARA, define safety goals, and conceive the functional safety concept. This is like embryonic development, where the immune system's basic architecture is laid down.
2. **Product development phase:** system/hardware/software design, implementation, integration, and testing. This is like the immune system maturing after birth: it meets various antigens and builds a memory bank.
3. **Production phase:** control of safety-related production processes. This is like making sure every "newborn's" immune system matches the design: a production deviation can leave some people with an innate immune defect.
4. **Operation phase:** continuous safety monitoring after the vehicle goes on the road. This is like the immune system running day after day: meeting new pathogens and repairing everyday damage.
5. **Service phase:** safety requirements during repair and maintenance. This is like debridement and suturing after an injury: the intervention itself can disturb a defense structure that was already in place.
6. **Decommissioning phase:** safety requirements when the vehicle is scrapped. This is like the death of an individual: but "herd immunity" (the model's generalized safety data) lives on.

These six phases are not a one-way trip. They form a loop. Real development brings changes: a requirement changes, a design changes, a new failure mode is found. Every change triggers a fresh pass through the safety lifecycle. Your immune system isn't static either: every infection and every vaccination updates your "safety case".

## The Vocabulary: Basic Grammar of the Immune City

Now that you understand the city layout, let's learn its basic grammar. The terms below come up again and again throughout ISO 26262. I have deliberately framed them from the immune system's point of view to help you build intuition.

### Item: The Organ

**Definition:** A system, or a group of systems, that implements a vehicle-level function.

Your heart is an item. Your braking system is an item. Your EPS (electric power steering) is an item. An item can implement one vehicle-level function on its own, such as "provide steering assist torque".

When you run HARA, you first define the item's boundary: what belongs to this item? What doesn't? What are its inputs? Its outputs? How does it interact with the outside world (other ECUs, the driver, the road)?

It's like studying the heart. You have to define it: the heart includes the left ventricle, the right ventricle, and the valves (they belong to the item), but not the lungs (even though they are tightly coupled). Blood is the input/output. Nerve signals are external control inputs.

### Element: The Cell

**Definition:** A constituent unit of an item. A sensor, a microcontroller, a software module, a communication bus: any of these can be an element.

The heart is made of cardiomyocytes, conduction cells, and endothelial cells. An EPS system is made of a torque sensor, an MCU, a MOSFET power bridge, a motor, and a CAN transceiver.

An element is a unit of work that can be developed and tested on its own. Safety requirements eventually have to be decomposed down to the element level.

### Fault: Infection

**Definition:** An abnormal condition that can cause an item or element to fail.

An alpha particle striking an SRAM cell is a fault. A solder joint cracking from age is a fault. Excessive temperature causing parameter drift is a fault.

A fault is an abnormality at the physical source. It does not necessarily produce an observable consequence right away. It's like breathing in an influenza virus: the virus has entered the mucosal cells of your respiratory tract. That's a fault. But at this point you may have no symptoms at all, and the immune system may even clear the threat at the fault level.

### Error: Symptom

**Definition:** A deviation between a computed or observed value and the true value.

The torque value stored in SRAM should have been "3.5 Nm". An alpha particle flips one bit, and it becomes "35.5 Nm". That's an error. Noise on the CAN bus causes a CRC mismatch. That's also an error.

An error is how a fault shows itself, but it is still an internal state abnormality. It's like a virus starting to replicate inside a cell: your viral load is rising. That's an error. But you may still have no subjective symptoms.

**The key distinction:** a fault is the cause; an error is the manifestation of a fault. One fault can produce several errors, one error can come from several faults, and an error can be produced directly by external disturbance without any fault at all.

### Failure: Loss of Organ Function

**Definition:** The termination of an item's or element's intended behavior because a fault has become manifest.

When the torque sensor output stays wrong, the EPS system can no longer provide correct steering assist. That's a failure. When CAN communication with the brake ECU is lost for longer than a set time and the vehicle loses brake assist, that's a failure.

A failure is a qualitative change that comes when errors accumulate past a threshold. It is an externally observable loss of function. It's like a virus replicating enough that the immune system mounts a systemic response: you have a fever, you feel weak, you can't work normally. That's a failure.

### Hazard: A Threat to the Host

**Definition:** A potential source of harm caused by malfunctioning behaviour.

**Here is a trap that is easy to fall into, and you must remember it**: a hazard must be defined at the vehicle level, not at the system level. What you say is:

- ✅ "At highway speed, unexpected front-wheel steering makes the vehicle leave its lane" (hazard at the vehicle level)
- ❌ "The EPS torque sensor outputs a wrong value" (this is not a hazard; it is malfunctioning behaviour / a failure)

Again: in HARA, a hazard must be defined as "potential harm to the vehicle occupants or other road users", not as "a functional deviation of the ECU". This distinction is the core mental shift in HARA.

If the body is the vehicle, the hazard is "the virus travels from the nasal cavity into the lower respiratory tract and spreads to the lungs". That's a threat to the body. "The virus binds the ACE2 receptor on nasal epithelial cells" is a fault/error-level problem.

### Hazardous Event: A Threat in a Concrete Scenario

**Definition:** A combination of a hazard and an operational situation.

The same hazard means wildly different things in different scenarios:

- Unexpected steering + highway at 120 km/h in rain → fatal threat
- Unexpected steering + parking garage at 5 km/h, straight ahead → scrape risk
- Unexpected steering + vehicle stationary during maintenance → crushes the mechanic

That's why hazardous events exist. You cannot talk about harm without a scenario. The same virus is a completely different threat to an immunocompromised elderly person and to a healthy young adult.

### Safety Goal: The Immune System's Core Mission

**Definition:** The top-level safety requirement at the vehicle level, derived from HARA.

A safety goal is not a technical solution. It is a functional objective, no matter how you implement it.

For example: **"Prevent lateral vehicle deviation that the driver does not expect, caused by unintended steering."**

Notice that this safety goal does not mention "redundant torque sensors", "CAN signal timeout monitoring", or "motor angle plausibility checks". Those are technical safety requirements (TSRs). You derive them in Part 4; they are not the safety goal itself. The safety goal stays at the level of "what harm must be prevented".

### FTTI: The Window from Infection to Death

**Definition:** In a system without safety mechanisms, the minimum time span from the occurrence of a fault to the possible occurrence of a hazardous event.

In immune terms: without treatment (a safety mechanism), what is the minimum time from a virus entering a respiratory cell to viral pneumonia causing respiratory failure? That's the FTTI.

FTTI is an inherent property of the system. Your design does not set it. It is the inherent time of the hazardous event's physical process. For example: the steering rack takes at most 200 ms to go from its normal position to the chassis deflection limit, so your FTTI is on the order of 200 ms. Brake fluid leaking out of the reservoir until braking fails may take tens of seconds, so the FTTI is in the seconds-to-tens-of-seconds range.

FTTI is the hard deadline for your safety mechanism design: all fault detection and fault response must finish inside the FTTI.

### FHTI = FDTI + FRTI

- **FDTI (fault detection time interval):** the time from the occurrence of a fault to its detection. The slot available to the fault detection mechanism.
- **FRTI (fault reaction time interval):** the time from fault detection to the system entering the safe state or starting emergency operation. The time needed to make the transition.

You must guarantee **FDTI + FRTI < FTTI**. Otherwise the hazardous event has already happened by the time your safety mechanism reacts.

In immune terms: FDTI is the time the immune system takes to recognize the pathogen (from the virus entering a cell to a dendritic cell presenting antigen). FRTI is the time it takes to launch the attack (from T-cell activation to enough neutralizing antibody). FTTI is the minimum time "from infection to multi-organ failure". If FDTI + FRTI > FTTI, the patient is already in the ICU and your immune response is just arriving. Too late.

## The Fault → Error → Failure → Hazard Chain: A Real Case

Let's make the abstract chain concrete. Consider a real physical mechanism:

1. **Fault:** A high-energy alpha particle (from a trace radioactive impurity in the chip packaging material) strikes the storage node of an SRAM cell, the charge drains away, and the stored bit flips from "1" to "0".

2. **Error:** That flipped bit happens to be the high bit of the torque value in the CAN frame's data field. What was "0x000C" (12, a request for 12 Nm of steering torque) flips to "0x800C" (32780, absurdly large). Your system now believes the driver is violently cranking the wheel to the right.

3. **Error propagates:** Software processes the wrong torque value, packs it into a CAN frame, adds a CRC that looks correct (computed over the wrong data), and sends it onto the CAN bus. The EPS controller receives it, concludes the driver needs strong steering assist… and drives a large current into the motor.

4. **Failure:** Based on the wrong data, the EPS applies far more torque to the steering column than the driver expects. The steering rack starts to move. The wheel turns by itself with no driver input. The EPS has terminated its intended behavior: "provide assist torque according to the driver's intent".

5. **Hazard:** The vehicle develops "lateral deviation the driver does not expect". On a highway, this can push the vehicle out of its lane.

6. **Hazardous Event:** Suppose this happens on a highway, in rain, at 120 km/h, with a large truck in the next lane. That is S3 (life-threatening, survival uncertain) + E4 (highway driving is frequent) + C3 (very few drivers can control unexpected steering) → an ASIL D hazardous event.

The chain starts with one alpha particle and ends in a potentially fatal crash. Your safety mechanisms must break it somewhere inside the FTTI: ECC memory protection (breaks at fault → error), end-to-end CAN communication protection (breaks at error → failure), redundant torque sensor cross-checking (breaks at failure → hazard).

## The Full Immune-System Mapping

Now let's map every term onto the complete immune-system picture:

| ISO 26262 term | Immune-system mapping | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Item | Organ (e.g. the lung) | The system boundary that delivers a specific function |
| Element | Cell (e.g. alveolar epithelial cell) | The smallest building block of the system |
| Fault | Infection (virus enters a cell) | An abnormal condition at the physical or logical level |
| Error | Symptom (viral replication inside a cell) | An observable deviation of state |
| Failure | Loss of organ function (pneumonia) | Termination of the intended function |
| Hazard | Threat to the host (low blood oxygen) | Potential harm to life |
| Hazardous Event | Threat in a specific environment (high altitude + low blood oxygen + poor lung function) | Hazard + operational situation |
| Safety Goal | Immune objective (prevent a fatal infection) | Top-level functional safety requirement |
| FTTI | Shortest time from infection to death | Inherent time of the physical process |
| FHTI | Shortest time from recognition to clearance | Reaction time of the safety mechanism |
| Safety Mechanism | Immune response (T cells, antibodies) | Measures that detect and control faults |
| ASIL | Strength of the immune response | Risk level determines defensive investment |

## The Item-Element Hierarchy

ISO 26262 defines a clear hierarchy for decomposing a vehicle function down to the smallest developable unit. Each level down, your safety requirements must be refined accordingly.

```
Vehicle → Item → System → Component → Hardware Part → HW Subpart → HW Elementary Subpart

Example:
Vehicle
 └── Steering function (Item)
       ├── EPS controller (System)
       │    ├── MCU (Component)
       │    │    ├── Cortex-R5F Core (Hardware Part)
       │    │    │    └── FPU (HW Subpart)
       │    │    │          └── Adder (HW Elementary Subpart)
       │    │    └── SRAM (Hardware Part)
       │    │          └── Memory cell (HW Elementary Subpart)
       │    └── CAN transceiver (Component)
       └── Steering motor (System)
            ├── Position sensor (Component)
            └── Motor winding (Component)
```

The point of this hierarchy: when you work on hardware architectural metrics (Part 5), you need to allocate safety mechanisms to the right level. A fault at the HW Elementary Subpart level (an alpha particle flipping an SRAM bit) can be protected by ECC at the Hardware Part level. If the ECC fails, end-to-end protection at the System level catches it. That's the "layered defense" principle, and it maps exactly onto the immune system's three lines: skin barrier → innate immunity → adaptive immunity.

## At the Vehicle Level: Don't Get Trapped in the ECU Box

ISO 26262 keeps emphasizing two key concepts: **vehicle function** and **vehicle operating state**. These two concepts bridge "the systems engineer's mindset" and "the functional safety engineer's mindset".

**A vehicle function is not the function of one ECU.** EPS providing steering assist is a "system function". "Vehicle steering" is the vehicle function. "Vehicle braking" is a vehicle function; "ESC hydraulic modulation" is a system function. "Vehicle acceleration" is a vehicle function; "engine torque control" is a system function.

When you do HARA, you must shift your thinking from "what is my ECU doing" to "what is this vehicle doing". The driver perceives vehicle behavior, not ECU behavior. A driver does not say "my EPS PWM duty cycle is abnormal". He says "the wheel turned by itself" or "the car won't do what I tell it".

**Vehicle operating states** include: stationary, low-speed driving, high-speed driving, reversing, turning, braking, accelerating, coasting, hill parking, service mode, crash state. Each state has a different risk profile: the same function failure is a completely different problem in "high-speed cornering" and in "stationary maintenance".

## Practical Points for Defining an Item

Beyond boundaries and interfaces, an item definition must answer several key questions:

**What operating modes does the item have?** For EPS: normal assist mode, degraded (limp-home) mode, park assist mode, service/diagnostic mode. Each mode has a different functional scope and different malfunctioning behaviour. You must do HARA for each mode separately.

**Which external signals does the item depend on?** EPS depends on vehicle speed (which sets the assist curve), engine speed, and ESC status. If one of these external signals is wrong (not because of an EPS fault, but because another ECU providing it failed), EPS must detect it and respond safely. But note: in HARA you assess "the harm caused by this item's malfunctioning behaviour". The effect of an external signal fault on your item is something you handle in the functional safety concept. The HARA for the external item's fault is that item's own responsibility.

**Does this item share any hardware resources with another item?** Do EPS and ESC share the same CAN network? The same power domain? If resources are shared, you must assess the common-cause effect of a shared-resource failure on both items in the dependent failure analysis (Part 9, DFA).

## The Safety Case: An Immunization Record

ISO 26262 requires you to eventually build a "safety case": a complete argument that your system is safe enough for every identified hazard.

A safety case is like a bound volume of immunization records, medical checkup reports, and gene sequencing results. You can't just say "we gave the vaccine". You must provide antibody titer results, T-cell response tests, and long-term follow-up data. Same for a car: you can't just say "we followed ISO 26262". You must prove with a chain of evidence that every safety goal is met, every failure mode is covered, and every safety mechanism has passed verification.

The safety case includes, but is not limited to: the HARA report, the safety goal list, the functional safety concept, the technical safety concept, the hardware architectural metrics report, the software architectural metrics, test reports, the safety validation report, and the functional safety assessment report. Together these work products form a complete argument.
