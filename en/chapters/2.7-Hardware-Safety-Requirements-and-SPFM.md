# 2.7 Hardware Safety Requirements and SPFM: How Strong Is Your First Line of Defense?


Two thirty in the afternoon. You open the FMEDA (failure mode, effects, and diagnostic analysis) spreadsheet. Forty-seven rows fill the screen: every hardware component in the steering ECU (electronic control unit) you are responsible for. Next to each component is a number from the supplier's reliability handbook: failure probability, in FIT (failure in time, 1 FIT = 10⁻⁹/h). Your task looks brutal: find every isolated failure point among these 47 components that could "go rogue", then prove that the coverage of the safety mechanisms you deployed, plus the components' own safety properties, eliminates at least 99% of the single-point failure risk. Right: because you are targeting ASIL D (the highest safety level).

You rub your eyes and type a percentage into the "diagnostic coverage" column. "Is 60% enough? No, that's only low coverage." You delete it and type 90%. You think, delete it, and type 99%.


## 1. From FSR to HSR: When Functional Safety Requirements Become Hardware Propositions

Before we start calculating SPFM, we must settle one question: **where do hardware safety requirements (HSRs) come from?**

In section 2.6 you derived a set of **functional safety requirements (FSRs)** from the functional safety concept. An FSR describes how the system should be safe, but it is still relatively abstract: for example, "the system shall enter the safe state within 200 ms after detecting a steering torque sensor fault". Every word in that sentence has to be translated again at the hardware level:

- **"detected"** → what kind of hardware detection circuit is needed? (A comparator? A redundant sampling channel?)
- **"steering torque sensor fault"** → which failure modes of the sensor must be covered? (Open circuit? Short circuit? Drift?)
- **"within 200 ms"** → what does this timing constraint mean for the hardware? (Watchdog timeout period? The FTTI requirement?)
- **"enter the safe state"** → how is the safe state implemented at the hardware level? (Cut the motor drive? Pull the enable signal low?)

ISO 26262-5:2018 sets a clear requirement for this step: **hardware safety requirements shall be derived from the FSRs and shall include a detailed description of the safety mechanisms and their attributes.** Specifically, every HSR should contain at least:

1. **Controlled object:** which hardware component or subcircuit is responsible for meeting this requirement.
2. **Failure detection mechanism:** how failure is detected (voltage monitoring, current monitoring, timing checks, redundant comparison, and so on).
3. **Fault response time:** the maximum permitted time from fault detection to entering the safe state.
4. **Safe state definition:** the expected hardware behavior after a fault (high impedance, output grounded, reset, and so on).
5. **Diagnostic coverage requirement:** the diagnostic coverage level the safety mechanism must reach (low/medium/high).

## 2. Hardware Fault Classification: Four Classes Set Your SPFM Denominator

The informative annex on fault classification in ISO 26262-5:2018 gives the complete taxonomy of random hardware faults. You must sort every potential failure mode of these 47 components into one of the four classes below. The definitions are precise, the classes are mutually exclusive under one safety goal, and they directly set the numerator and denominator of the SPFM formula.

### 2.1 Single-Point Fault (SPF)

> **Definition (vocabulary): a fault in a hardware element that is not covered by any safety mechanism. The fault by itself directly causes a violation of the safety goal.**

You are an immune system. A single-point fault is an infection erupting in a corner of your body where no immune cell patrols: no antibody recognizes it, no phagocyte responds, and the pathogen goes from appearing to killing in one step.

Typical SPF scenarios at the hardware level:
- an MCU GPIO pin shorts to ground, so the motor enable signal stays high and the safe state cannot be entered;
- no detection circuit monitors the level of that GPIO pin;
- fault occurrence = safety goal violation, with no buffer in between.

SPF is the most dangerous fault type in the whole functional safety design. For an ASIL D system, the "zero tolerance" principle for SPF is not a hard zero requirement, but SPFM ≥ 99% means at most 1% of the failure probability can slip through as SPF or RF.

### 2.2 Residual Fault (RF)

> **Definition (vocabulary): the part of a random hardware fault that is not controlled by a safety mechanism. When diagnostic coverage is 100%, RF is zero; when coverage is below 100%, RF is the uncovered tail.**

The analogy to the human immune system is exact: you get vaccinated (a safety mechanism), and your body produces antibodies against a virus. But antibody coverage is not 100%: a tiny number of virus particles may escape antibody recognition. That escaping part is RF.

In formula form:
$$\lambda_{RF} \leq \lambda \times \left(1 - \frac{K_{DC,RF}}{100\%}\right)$$

Here $K_{DC,RF}$ is the diagnostic coverage for residual faults. Note the "≤". You may conservatively assume coverage is worse than hoped, but you must never optimistically round it up.

SPF and RF are handled together in the SPFM formula for a simple reason: **from the safety goal's point of view, SPF and RF have the same effect. Both escape every safety mechanism and hit the safety goal directly.** The only difference is that SPF has no safety mechanism coverage at all, while RF has a safety mechanism that does not cover it completely.

### 2.3 Multiple-Point Fault (MPF)

> **Definition (vocabulary): a fault that causes a violation of the safety goal only in combination with one or more independent hardware faults.**

MPF is the most subtle class in functional safety analysis. On its own it is not fatal: like a person infected with latent tuberculosis, no symptoms, normal life. But once the immune system is suppressed for another reason (a second independent fault appears), the latent infection breaks out.

ISO 26262-5 further subdivides MPF:
- **Detected MPF (MPF_D):** after the fault occurs, a safety mechanism finds it within the multiple-point fault detection interval (MPFDI). The danger is very low, because you can deal with it before it "finds an accomplice".
- **Perceived MPF (MPF_P):** during normal vehicle operation, the driver can directly perceive the fault (the instrument cluster is already showing a warning, for example).
- **Latent MPF (MPF_L):** neither detected by a safety mechanism nor perceived by the driver; it sits quietly in the system, waiting to conspire with another independent fault.

Latent multiple-point faults are exactly what the LFM (latent fault metric) focuses on. That's the subject of section 2.8.

### 2.4 Safe Fault (S)

> **Definition (vocabulary): a fault that does not significantly increase the probability of a safety goal violation.**

In the immune system: every day a large number of epidermal cells die and flake off your skin. That's normal and no threat to survival. Same at the hardware level: a burnt-out LED indicator does not affect braking, so it is a safe fault.


## 3. The Complete Picture of Failure Probability

ISO 26262-5:2018 explicitly requires that the total failure rate λ of a hardware element be decomposable into the sum of the four classes (the fault classification equation in the normative annex on hardware architectural metrics):

$$\lambda = \lambda_{SPF} + \lambda_{RF} + \lambda_{MPF} + \lambda_S$$

The subscript on each Greek letter is not a casual academic label. It is a FIT number you must fill into your FMEDA spreadsheet precisely. Each of the 47 components can be drawn as a pie chart of these classes: some have a high SPF share (danger! strengthen safety mechanism coverage), some have a high S share (relax: most failure modes are harmless), and components with a large MPF share need extra attention to the latent-fault detection interval.


## 4. The SPFM Formula: Calculating Your Immune Defense Coverage

Now you open the FMEDA spreadsheet and get ready to calculate SPFM. **The single-point fault metric (SPFM) measures what share of all random hardware failures are neither single-point faults nor residual faults: that is, either detected and controlled by a safety mechanism, or safe in themselves.**

The hardware architectural metrics equation in the normative annex of ISO 26262-5:2018 (normative, not informative, so the formula is mandatory):

$$SPFM = 1 - \frac{\sum_{SR,HW}(\lambda_{SPF} + \lambda_{RF})}{\sum_{SR,HW}(\lambda)} = \frac{\sum_{SR,HW}(\lambda_{MPF} + \lambda_S)}{\sum_{SR,HW}(\lambda)}$$

Reading the formula step by step:

**Denominator Σ_{SR,HW}(λ):** the sum of the total failure rates over all safety-related hardware elements (SR,HW = safety-related hardware): the total failure rate of all safety-related failure modes of all 47 hardware components on your ECU. Note that only hardware elements related to the safety goal are counted. The LED indicator, if unrelated to the safety goal, may stay out of the denominator. But once it is in, it is all in.

**The subtrahend in the numerator, Σ(λ_SPF + λ_RF):** all the failure share that directly threatens the safety goal: the full single-point failure rate plus the full residual failure rate. This is the part of the threat your safety mechanisms **miss**.

**1 − [threat / total]:** what remains is the share the safety mechanisms have "immunized", which is your SPFM.

**Equivalent form Σ(λ_MPF + λ_S) / Σ(λ):** SPFM from the other side. The share your defense line "successfully intercepts": multiple-point faults (detected or latent, both "cannot kill alone") plus safe faults (not fatal at all).

### 4.1 Worked Example: One SPFM Calculation

Suppose the hardware failure analysis for your steering ECU sums up as follows (FIT):

| Fault class | Symbol | Value (FIT) |
|---------|------|-----------|
| Single-point fault | λ_SPF | 2.3 |
| Residual fault | λ_RF | 1.7 |
| Multiple-point fault (including latent) | λ_MPF | 35.0 |
| Safe fault | λ_S | 61.0 |
| **Total failure rate** | **λ_total** | **100.0** |

Substitute into the SPFM formula:

$$SPFM = 1 - \frac{2.3 + 1.7}{100.0} = 1 - \frac{4.0}{100.0} = 1 - 0.04 = 0.96 = 96\%$$

96%. Against the SPFM target values in ISO 26262-5:2018, that is not good:

| ASIL | Minimum SPFM |
|---------|-------------|
| ASIL B | ≥ 90% |
| ASIL C | ≥ 97% |
| ASIL D | ≥ 99% |

96% clears the ASIL B requirement (90%) easily, but it is 1 percentage point short of ASIL C's 97% and a full 3 points short of ASIL D's 99%. If you are targeting ASIL D (as steering systems usually are), you need to raise the diagnostic coverage of some components in the FMEDA from medium to high. That's SPFM's **reverse drive** on hardware design: fail the calculation, go back.

### 4.2 How SPFM Forces You to Add Safety Mechanisms

Back to the 47 components. Suppose a careful look at the FMEDA shows that one MOSFET driver (λ = 8.0 FIT, current K_DC,RF = 90%) contributes 0.8 FIT of RF:

Switch to a safety mechanism with higher diagnostic coverage (say from "90% medium" to "99% high"), and RF falls from 0.8 FIT to 0.08 FIT, saving 0.72 FIT. Every 0.1 FIT of RF you squeeze out moves SPFM one step closer to 99%.

That's exactly SPFM's value as a **design-iteration driver**: it is not a report card issued after the fact. It keeps telling you during design: "this spot still needs a safety mechanism, that spot is done".

## 5. The Relationship Between SPFM and Diagnostic Coverage

The SPFM formula does not explicitly contain a diagnostic coverage (DC) parameter. But in practice you cannot discuss SPFM without DC, because:

$$\lambda_{RF} = \lambda \times \left(1 - \frac{K_{DC,RF}}{100\%}\right)$$

Here K_DC,RF is the diagnostic coverage for residual faults. The diagnostic coverage annex in ISO 26262-5:2018 divides diagnostic coverage into three levels:

| Level | Coverage range | Typical implementation mechanism |
|------|----------|------------|
| Low | ≈ 60% | Detects only pin open/short |
| Medium | ≈ 90% | Adds range checks and plausibility checks |
| High | ≈ 99% | Redundant comparison, lockstep core, ECC memory |

For an ASIL D system, if your safety mechanism reaches only medium coverage (~90%), each hardware element still leaves an RF tail of about 10%. In the example above, with total λ = 100 FIT, RF alone would take several FIT, and SPFM would struggle to reach 99%. That's why ASIL D usually requires high diagnostic coverage on components in the critical safety path.

One point is easily misunderstood: **λ_RF in the SPFM calculation is the sum over all hardware elements, while diagnostic coverage is assessed element by element.** You cannot say "I used a 99%-coverage safety mechanism, so SPFM is 99%". SPFM is a weighted average over the whole system, set by each component's failure-mode distribution and the coverage you apply to each mode.


## 6. The Immune Mapping: SPFM as Functional Safety's Immune Baseline

If you think of the whole hardware system as an organism, the immunological correspondence of the four fault classes and SPFM becomes very clear:

| Functional safety concept | Immunological counterpart |
|------------|----------|
| **Single-point fault (SPF)** | **Immunodeficiency:** a tissue has no immune surveillance at all locally; once a pathogen appears, disease is certain |
| **Residual fault (RF)** | **Immune escape:** vaccine-induced antibodies do not fully cover a viral variant; a few virus particles evade neutralization |
| **Multiple-point fault (MPF)** | **Latent opportunistic infection:** tuberculosis bacteria lie dormant in a granuloma; the person does not fall ill while immunity is normal, and only breaks down when immunity drops |
| **Safe fault (S)** | **Harmless commensalism / apoptosis:** skin keratinocytes flake off, gut flora metabolize normally; no threat |
| **SPFM** | **Baseline immune coverage:** when the body faces all known pathogens, what share can the immune system clear effectively or render harmless |
| **Safety mechanism** | **Antibodies and immune cells:** effector molecules and cells that specifically recognize and clear a given threat |
| **Diagnostic coverage** | **Antibody affinity / breadth of recognition:** how efficiently antibodies bind a given epitope, and how far they cross-protect against variants |

**SPFM = 99% can be read as:** your immune system can recognize and clear 99% of known threats. The remaining 1% is either an immunodeficient region (SPF) or the residual risk of an escape mutation (RF). That 1% is the residual risk you must accept in the design and make up for at other layers (software, process, driver warning).

The metaphor extends further: **ASIL D's SPFM ≥ 99% is like requiring a "herd immunity threshold".** An organism must have near-perfect immediate immunity against the vast majority of common pathogens, because its environment (a vehicle on the highway) does not allow the gradual process of "get infected, then make antibodies". Faults must be **detected preventively**, not **repaired reactively**.

## Summary

1. Hardware safety requirements (HSRs) are derived from FSRs; each HSR covers the controlled object, detection mechanism, response time, safe state, and diagnostic coverage
2. Random hardware faults fall into four classes: SPF, RF, MPF, S, and they sum to λ
3. SPFM = 1 − Σ(λ_SPF + λ_RF) / Σ(λ)
4. SPFM targets for ASIL B/C/D: ≥ 90% / ≥ 97% / ≥ 99%
5. SPFM drives safety mechanism design: miss the target → raise diagnostic coverage or add redundancy
