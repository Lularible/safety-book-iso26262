# 2.18 Part Review: The Complete Argument from HARA to the Safety Case


## Scenario: a safety journey that starts with one HARA table

January 2023. You sit in a meeting room, the whiteboard in front of you covered in sticky notes. This is the kickoff of the electric power steering (EPS) functional safety project. Project code: EPS-R5. Platform: a battery-electric sedan. Your first task: define the HARA (hazard analysis and risk assessment) for the EPS system.

Operating scenario number OSE-042: the vehicle takes a highway ramp to the right at 62 km/h, curve radius about 120 m, road friction coefficient μ≈0.7. The driver is applying about 4.8 Nm of steering torque through the wheel.

Suppose a fault occurs: **the steering assistance function is lost unexpectedly while the driver is requesting steering torque.**

Hazard analysis for this scenario:
- Hazard: the vehicle loses the expected steering assistance and the steering effort demand spikes — the driver must apply about 4.4 times the torque (from 4.8 Nm to about 21.3 Nm) to complete the same steering maneuver.
- Exposure (E): E4 (high probability). Turning onto a highway ramp is a daily driving scenario, occurring hundreds of thousands of times per day per thousand vehicles.
- Controllability (C): C3 (fewer than 90% of drivers can avoid harm under typical conditions). At 62 km/h on a ramp with a 120 m radius, sudden loss of steering assistance means more than 90% of ordinary drivers cannot control the car safely within the time available to avoid harm (usually 2-3 s).
- Severity (S): S3 (fatal injury possible). Loss of steering control sends the vehicle off the ramp or into the guardrail, and the outcome can be fatal.
- ASIL: E4 + C3 + S3 → **ASIL D**.

Thus **safety goal SG-001** is born:

> **"The steering assistance system shall not fail to provide steering torque assistance when commanded by the driver under normal driving conditions."**
> **ASIL D.** FTTI = 200 ms. Safe state = provide at least 50% of nominal assistance torque + warning lamp on.

This is not a harmless sentence written on paper. This sentence will drive the work of every engineering team for the next two years and four months.

Now let's walk the whole path: starting from SG-001, how one complete argument chain penetrates every layer of the V model and finally becomes an irrefutable bidirectional audit trail in the safety case.


## HARA → safety goal → functional safety concept (FSC)

SG-001 defines the top-level safety goal: any fault that prevents steering assistance must be detected within 200 ms and the system must enter the safe state within the FTTI. But SG-001 does not say which faults would prevent steering assistance. The answer to that comes from FMEA (failure mode and effects analysis) and FTA (fault tree analysis).

In your system FMEA, the team identified these key failure modes:
1. **Loss of the torque sensor main signal** (open/short/SPI timeout): directly prevents the EPS from sensing the driver's intent;
2. **Motor phase current sensor failure**: prevents the motor from delivering the target torque correctly;
3. **MCU clock instability**: disrupts the timing of the control algorithm;
4. **Power management IC failure**: cuts power to a safety-related supply rail (such as the 3.3 V analog domain).

FSRs (functional safety requirements) are derived from SG-001:

* **FSR-031**: the system shall detect the integrity of the torque sensor signal and enter the safe state within ≤200 ms whenever signal loss, out-of-range, or communication timeout occurs.
* **FSR-032**: the system shall detect the integrity of the motor current sensor... (similar structure).
* **FSR-033**: the system shall detect loss of MCU clock validity...
* **FSR-034**: the system shall detect undervoltage on the safety-related supply rail...

Each FSR inherits the ASIL D and FTTI of SG-001 and adds concrete technical boundaries. This inheritance is recorded in the **first layer of the traceability matrix**: SG-001 → FSR-031, FSR-032, FSR-033, FSR-034.

The functional safety concept (FSC) gathers all FSRs and begins architecture-level thinking: how are these FSRs allocated to different elements of the system? Which elements carry safety-related functions, and which are QM (only required to meet quality management standards)?


## Technical safety concept (TSC): from "what" to "how"

FSRs tell you "what must be detected." TSRs (technical safety requirements) tell you "by what technical means."

Take FSR-031 (torque sensor signal integrity detection). Your system architecture team decides on these safety mechanisms:

* **TSR-042**: the ADC module of the EPS controller shall continuously sample the differential output of the torque sensor at ≥1 kHz. At any time, if the ADC sample exceeds the valid range of 4.5 V to 0.5 V (corresponding to the sensor's actual output range of 0.5 V to 4.5 V), or if the SPI communication CRC check fails for 3 consecutive frames, the EPS shall enter the safe state within the FTTI (200 ms) by switching the motor control mode to "safe degraded mode": maintain basic assistance at 50% of nominal assistance and illuminate the EPS fault lamp on the dashboard.

* **TSR-043**: the ECU shall be equipped with an independent hardware watchdog (window watchdog, service window 50 ms-80 ms). If the MCU fails to service the watchdog inside the window, the watchdog must trigger an MCU reset within 10 ms. After reset the ECU shall run the ASIL D safe start sequence (including full RAM/Flash integrity checks), and the initial assistance mode shall be "safe degraded mode."

* **TSR-044**: the safety-critical 3.3 V power domain shall be equipped with an independent undervoltage monitoring circuit (threshold 2.97 V ±1%). On trigger it must reset all safety functions supplied by that domain within 50 µs and enter the safe state within ≤5 ms.

Each TSR is an engineering specification that can be assigned to a hardware engineer (the undervoltage circuit design for TSR-044), a software engineer (the ADC sampling and threshold logic for TSR-042), or a systems and test engineer (the watchdog timing definition and verification for TSR-043).

**The second layer of the traceability matrix** is established: FSR-031 → TSR-042, TSR-043, TSR-044...


## Hardware architecture: SPFM, LFM, PMHF

TSR-042, TSR-043, and TSR-044 are decomposed into hardware and software requirements. The hardware team begins the hardware safety analysis.

**Hardware architectural metrics (the hardware part of ISO 26262)**:

ASIL D hardware architecture must satisfy:
- **SPFM (single-point fault metric) ≥ 99%**: the share of single-point and residual fault failure rates in the total failure rate of safety-related hardware must be low enough that the remainder reaches 99% or more.
- **LFM (latent fault metric) ≥ 90%**: among the failure rate remaining after removing single-point and residual faults, the share of latent multiple-point faults must be low enough that the remainder reaches 90% or more.
- **PMHF (probabilistic metric for random hardware failures) < 10 FIT** (10⁻⁸/hour): the total probability of violating the safety goal due to any random hardware failure must be below once per 10⁸ hours.

The hardware team performs FMEDA (failure mode, effects, and diagnostic analysis) on every safety-related hardware component of the EPS controller. Take the torque sensor interface circuit as an example:

* MOSFET switch failure: single-point fault → safety mechanism (ADC channel diagnostics; three consecutive out-of-range readings mark a fault) → coverage 99.5%;
* ADC reference voltage drift: latent fault → safety mechanism (periodic reference cross-check against an independent bandgap reference every 100 ms) → latent fault coverage 98%;
* PCB trace open: single-point fault → safety mechanism (signal out-of-range detection) → coverage 99.8%.

After aggregating all safety-related hardware components, the hardware team obtains:
- SPFM = 99.4% (target 99%, pass)
- LFM = 92.1% (target 90%, pass)
- PMHF = 7.3 FIT (target <10 FIT, pass, design safety margin about 27%)

These values are recorded in the **hardware safety analysis report**, whose "references" column points to specific TSR numbers (such as TSR-042), which in turn trace up to FSR-031 and ultimately belong to SG-001.

At this point, **the third layer of the traceability matrix** is established: TSR-042 → the "torque sensor interface circuit safety mechanism coverage report" in the hardware safety analysis report.


## Software architecture: FFI and freedom analysis

Meanwhile, the software team is doing something equally important: **FFI (freedom from interference) analysis**.

The core question of FFI: between the ASIL D safety functions and the QM non-safety functions running on the same MCU, is there resource interference? Queuing effects, deadlocks, hidden coupling of shared resources. All of these fall within the scope of FFI.

In the EPS-R5 project, the software architecture design covers these key FFI points:

* **Memory protection**: the ASIL D torque calculation function (cal_TorqueCompute) and the QM Bluetooth log transfer module share the same SRAM. An MPU (memory protection unit) must ensure that QM code cannot write into the ASIL D data region. The MPU configuration itself must be verified during the safe start sequence (CRC-32).
* **Timing protection**: the ASIL D 200 ms safety loop may miss its deadline because QM tasks occupy the CPU. The solution: time partitioning. ASIL D tasks run in dedicated slots, and QM tasks cannot preempt the CPU within the ASIL D time budget.
* **Information exchange protection**: status data passed from ASIL D tasks to QM tasks (such as motor temperature for diagnostic logging) must use an end-to-end (E2E) protection protocol: add CRC-16 and a sequence counter, so that data received by QM cannot be mistaken for safety information because of a communication link error.

The FFI analysis results go into the **software safety analysis report**. Each protection measure maps to a TSR (for example, "time partitioning" maps to the "respond within 200 ms" requirement of TSR-042) and traces up to FSR-031 and SG-001.


## Software unit design and MC/DC testing

TSR-042 requires "a torque signal outside the 4.5 V-0.5 V range is judged a fault." The software team refines this TSR into concrete software unit requirements:

* **SWR-042-01**: the function `Sensor_ValidateTorque()` shall read the ADC raw value (adcVal). If adcVal > ADC_MAX_VALID or adcVal < ADC_MIN_VALID, it shall confirm the out-of-range condition 3 consecutive times (1 ms apart) and then return SENSOR_STATUS_FAULT.

This function contains a conditional decision: `if ((adcVal > ADC_MAX) || (adcVal < ADC_MIN))` — two conditions (adcVal > ADC_MAX and adcVal < ADC_MIN) combined with OR logic. **MC/DC (modified condition/decision coverage)** requires:

ASIL D software must satisfy MC/DC coverage. For the decision above, the following test cases are needed:

| Case | adcVal > ADC_MAX | adcVal < ADC_MIN | Decision result |
|------|------------------|------------------|---------|
| TC-01 | True | False | True |
| TC-02 | False | True | True |
| TC-03 | False | False | False |

These 3 test cases together prove that each condition independently affects the decision result (TC-01 proves condition 1 alone can make the decision True, TC-02 proves condition 2 alone can make the decision True, and TC-03 serves as the control).

This test case set (TC-01/02/03) is recorded in the **software unit test report** as covering SWR-042-01. SWR-042-01 points to TSR-042 in the traceability matrix, TSR-042 points to FSR-031, and FSR-031 points to SG-001.

You also verify that the function's **branch coverage** and **statement coverage** both reach 100%. At ASIL D, branch coverage is "++" and statement coverage is "+", and you need both.

At this point, **the fourth layer of the traceability matrix** is established: SWR-042-01 → unit tests TC-01/02/03 (MC/DC coverage evidence).


## Integration testing: after hardware and software come together

Software unit testing verified only the logical correctness of a single function. When that function is embedded in the complete EPS controller software scheduling framework — interacting with task scheduling, interrupt handling, DMA transfers, watchdog servicing, and other runtime elements — is it still correct?

**Software integration testing (ISO 26262-6)** verifies this. For TSR-042, you design a complete integration test scenario:

* Run the full EPS controller software on the HIL bench (including the FreeRTOS scheduler, CAN communication stack, diagnostic stack, NVM manager... every runtime element).
* Use the fault injection unit (FIU) to inject a **simulated open-circuit fault** on the torque sensor output: the signal drops instantly from 2.5 V to 0 V.
* Monitor the EPS CAN output frames. Verify:
  * Within ≤200 ms after fault injection, the diagnostic frame sent by the EPS controller indicates "torque sensor fault" (bit 2 of byte 4 of CAN ID 0x1A2 set to 1).
  * Within ≤200 ms, the EPS controller enters "safe degraded mode": motor torque output drops to 50% of nominal (in the torque sensor's CAN output, the target torque value drops from 4.8 Nm to 2.4 Nm).
  * Within ≤200 ms, the dashboard EPS fault lamp illuminates (bit 6 of byte 1 of CAN ID 0x4B0 set to 1).
  * Repeat the test 3 consecutive times at 0.5 s intervals; timing meets the 200 ms FTTI requirement.

These results are recorded in the **software integration test report**, citing TSR-042. **The fifth layer of the traceability matrix** is established.


## System integration and vehicle verification: proof on the test track

Software integration testing passed on the HIL bench. The SPFM/LFM/PMHF numbers from the hardware FMEDA also met their targets. Now only one question remains: **the vehicle.**

**System integration testing (ISO 26262-4)** and **vehicle verification (ISO 26262-4)** are the final stage of the safety argument, and the closest to reality.

You install the complete EPS controller (with all production calibration parameters) in a test mule and run two rounds of verification:

* **System HIL testing**: on a dSPACE simulation platform, connect the complete EPS mechanical system (steering gear, motor, torque sensor, and angle sensor are all real hardware) and connect a vehicle dynamics model over CAN (Carsim simulating vehicle dynamics in real time: body roll, load transfer, tire stiffness changes). In this integrated environment, inject a "torque sensor communication timeout" fault. Verify that the EPS enters the safe state within the FTTI, and verify that the vehicle dynamics model remains controllable in safe degraded mode (50% assistance): the driver can correct the steering within 2 s, and the lateral deviation of the body stays under 0.5 m (a lane is typically 3.5 m wide).

* **Vehicle road testing**: on the high-speed circular track of an actual proving ground (radius 150 m, design speed 80 km/h), reproduce scenario OSE-042 at 62 km/h. Temporarily wire a programmable switch into the sensor branch. Without the driver's knowledge (but with a safety observer in the passenger seat and a roll cage fitted), use a remote command to disconnect the torque sensor signal at 62 km/h on the 120 m radius curve. Recorded data:
  * EPS from fault occurrence to safe degraded mode: 148 ms (<200 ms FTTI, pass)
  * Driver feels the steering torque spike and successfully corrects the trajectory: about 2.1 s (at 50% assistance)
  * Maximum lateral deviation of the vehicle: 0.37 m (within the lane, pass)
  * No collision, no loss of control, no driver-reported panic afterward (post-test questionnaire score 1.2/5, where 1 is "fully controllable").

The vehicle verification results go into the **vehicle verification report**, citing TSR-042 and the related FSRs. **The sixth layer of the traceability matrix** is established: vehicle road test — scenario OSE-042 — fault injection → TSR-042 → FSR-031 → SG-001.


## The safety case: gathering all the chains

After two years and four months, you have:
* HARA report (where SG-001 was born)
* FSC (FSR-031 and the others derived from SG-001)
* TSC (TSR-042 and the others derived from the FSRs)
* Hardware safety analysis report (SPFM 99.4%, LFM 92.1%, PMHF 7.3 FIT — cites TSR-042)
* Software safety analysis report (FFI — time partitioning protection, MPU memory protection, E2E protection: cites TSR-042)
* Software unit test report (MC/DC covering SWR-042-01: cites TSR-042)
* Software integration test report (HIL bench fault injection verification: cites TSR-042)
* System integration test report and vehicle verification report (fault injection on the real vehicle: cites TSR-042)
* EOL test specification (production-line verification of the torque sensor path: cites TSR-042)
* Field monitoring process definition (72-hour reporting mechanism for torque-sensor-related DTCs)

All these work products are linked through the **traceability matrix**, forming one complete audit chain:

> **HARA #17 (OSE-042, ASIL D) → SG-001 → FSR-031 → TSR-042 → SWR-042-01 / hardware safety mechanism → unit test evidence MC/DC / hardware SPFM calculation → integration test report (HIL) → vehicle verification report (road test) → EOL test specification → field monitoring definition → safety case summary "EPS Steering Assistance Safety Argument Report"**

When the TÜV assessor opens your safety case again in June 2025, he can now:
1. Start from HARA #17 and click forward along the traceability chain: through SG-001, FSR-031, TSR-042, SWR-042-01, all the way to line 42 of the MC/DC test report — verifying that every downstream work product is complete and correct;
2. Walk backward from the MC/DC test report: through SWR-042-01, TSR-042, FSR-031, SG-001, all the way to HARA #17 — verifying that no traceability link is missing;
3. Stop at any intermediate node and check the review signature, version number, and release date of that work product — confirming that the whole process is compliant.

He saw it. He signed.


## The immune metaphor: the full pathway from pathogen recognition to immune memory

Let's summarize this complete argument chain with the immune metaphor:

* **HARA** ≈ the pathogen catalog. Your immune system needs to know which pathogens (hazards/failure modes) can cause disease (an accident). HARA is that catalog: classified by severity (ASIL), recording each pathogen's characteristics (exposure scenario, controllability, severity).

* **Safety goal (SG)** ≈ the immune response objective. "For this pathogen, neutralizing antibodies must be produced" corresponds to "for this hazardous event, ASIL D safety integrity must be achieved."

* **FSR** ≈ the macroscopic definition of the immune response strategy. "IgA antibodies must be produced on the surface of the respiratory mucosa" corresponds to "signal integrity detection must be implemented at the torque sensor interface."

* **TSR** ≈ the molecular mechanism of the immune response. "V(D)J recombination in B cells produces a specific antibody variable region; the complement system tags the pathogen with C3b" corresponds to "the ADC samples the signal at 1 kHz, three out-of-range readings confirm a fault, and hardware and software together enter the safe state within the FTTI."

* **Hardware architecture SPFM/LFM/PMHF** ≈ the effectiveness metrics of the innate immune system. The speed at which neutrophils reach the infection site, the phagocytosis rate of macrophages, the efficiency of membrane attack complex formation in the complement system. These are the biological mapping of "random hardware failure coverage." SPFM 99% means: of 100 single-point faults, 99 are "recognized and destroyed" by safety mechanisms.

* **Software MC/DC testing** ≈ in vitro validation of a monoclonal antibody. "The lab confirms that the antibody still neutralizes SARS-CoV-2 at a 1:1000 dilution" corresponds to "MC/DC testing verifies that every condition in SWR-042-01 independently affects the decision result."

* **Software integration testing** ≈ validation of the immune response at the organ level. "Antibody binding to virus is confirmed in lung tissue sections from a mouse model" corresponds to "after fault injection on the HIL bench, verify that the EPS sends the correct CAN diagnostic frame within the FTTI."

* **Vehicle verification** ≈ human clinical trials. "Thirty thousand subjects are vaccinated in a phase III trial to verify efficacy and safety" corresponds to "fault injection in the 62 km/h ramp condition on the test vehicle verifies the EPS safe degradation response."

* **The safety case** ≈ the international travel health certificate (with quarantine updates for each entry). For every flight you take, the destination country may require: basic vaccination records, recent infectious disease screening, entry and exit quarantine stamps — evidence that is not issued once and valid forever, but keeps updating with your travel history. The safety case is also a living document that keeps evolving: the initial version is completed before SOP, but every OTA update, every batch of after-sales failure analysis, and every BOM change leaves a new record in the case. It is not an approval document that is "finished and handed in." It is a health passport that keeps updating from SOP to vehicle scrapping.

### The boundaries of this book

That is the full scope of ISO 26262:2018. But the safety challenges of automotive electronics do not stop there.

This chapter does not discuss **safety of the intended functionality (SOTIF, ISO 21448)** — it deals with "failures caused by insufficient functionality," not "failures caused by system faults." For example, a camera losing the lane marking in backlight, a lidar misclassifying in heavy rain, a deep neural network producing wrong inferences in scenarios outside the training distribution. These are not "faults"; they are "the function is not good enough." The perception-system failure modes and scenario coverage analysis in SOTIF use a fundamentally different methodological framework from the traditional chassis safety mechanisms discussed in this book, and deserve a book of their own.
