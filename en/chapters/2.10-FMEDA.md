# 2.10 FMEDA: Failure Analysis at the Component Level


## Scenario

Three in the afternoon. A 47-row Excel sheet is spread across your desk, every cell packed with numbers, percentages, and abbreviations. The air-conditioning vent points straight at your neck, but you can't be bothered to adjust it. You're staring at the CAN transceiver in row 23. In the "residual fault" column, the number is red.

Your hardware engineer colleague told you that afternoon: "We can choose this transceiver from three suppliers. Supplier A has the longest MTBF." He's pleased with himself, because supplier A's datasheet proudly states an MTBF of 476 million hours. Sounds reassuring, doesn't it?

But you know better. MTBF is a reliability concept. What you have to calculate is safety.

You turn supplier A's datasheet to the reliability chapter and find the FIT value: 2.1 FIT. That means the part fails 2.1 times per 10⁹ hours on average. Then you call the supplier and ask a question that leaves their technical support silent for three seconds:

"If this CAN transceiver fails, how does it fail? What's the probability of a short? Of an open? Of parameter drift?"

After the silence, they say: "Well... we'd need to confirm that with R&D."

You know this is where FMEDA starts. It's also the watershed where most engineers go from "intro to functional safety" to "intro to functional safety, and giving up."


## What FMEDA is

FMEDA stands for Failure Mode, Effects and Diagnostic Analysis. The hardware part of ISO 26262 sets out an explicit requirement: for every safety-related hardware element at every ASIL, do an inductive analysis (FMEA, strongly recommended); at ASIL C and D, strongly recommended to add a deductive analysis (FTA, fault tree analysis).

First, the difference between "inductive" and "deductive," because these two words come up again and again, and many engineers still can't explain the difference after three years.

Inductive analysis (FMEA): start from component failures at the bottom, work upward, and ask "if this resistor opens, what happens to the system?"

Deductive analysis (FTA): start from the top-level hazard, work downward, and ask "what underlying causes could produce unintended steering torque?"

The immune system uses both strategies. Innate immunity is inductive: a macrophage engulfs whatever pathogen it meets, no matter where it came from. Adaptive immunity is deductive: first recognize that "this thing shouldn't be in the blood," then trace back through the antibodies to work out which antigen it is. The two strategies complement each other. You need both.

FMEDA sits on the inductive side. Its special feature is that it doesn't stop at qualitative analysis. It's a quantitative craft.


## Back to your CAN transceiver

We'll use a CAN transceiver as the example and walk through the FMEDA workflow step by step.

### Step 1: Get the failure rate and the failure mode distribution

A CAN transceiver is a mixed-signal device. You get its failure rate λ from the supplier (or from an industry standard such as IEC TR 62380 or SN 29500). Assume λ = 2.1 FIT.

Next you need the failure mode distribution (Failure Mode Distribution, DFM) for the part. The annex on failure modes in ISO 26262-5 lists the failure modes that common component types need to be analyzed for; the actual distribution percentages come from the data sources above. For a "communication interface" device, a typical distribution might be:

| Failure mode | Distribution (DFM) | 
|---------|---------------|
| Output stuck high (short to VCC) | 25% |
| Output stuck low (short to GND) | 20% |
| Open (high impedance) | 25% |
| Parameter drift (timing deviation, abnormal slew rate) | 15% |
| Leakage to ground | 8%  |
| Power-on reset failure | 5%  |
| Other | 2%  |

**Note: these percentages are not pulled out of thin air.** They come from three sources: (1) the component manufacturer's internal data from accelerated life tests and failure analysis; (2) the failure mode distribution recommendations in industry databases such as SN 29500 and IEC TR 62380; (3) statistical regression on historical field failure data for the same component family. If you have to use default values (engineering judgment), you need to justify them fully in the safety case. That falls under the proven-in-use argument.

### Step 2: Assess the effect of each failure mode on the safety goal

Now you work row by row: if the transceiver output sticks high on the CAN bus (short to VCC), what does that do to your safety goal?

Suppose your safety goal is "prevent unintended steering." A CAN transceiver shorted high means it sends dominant bits forever and the whole bus locks up. No other node can communicate. The EPS (electric power steering) controller receives neither the vehicle speed signal nor the steering wheel angle plausibility signal.

What is that? ISO 26262-1 defines a key concept: a **single-point fault (SPF)** is a fault that directly leads to a violation of a safety goal and is not covered by any safety mechanism.

If a CAN short to high locks up the bus and your EPS controller does **not** detect "CAN communication lost" and enter the safe state, that is a single-point fault.

But suppose you design a safety mechanism: when the EPS controller receives no valid CAN frame within 100 ms, it drops assistance to zero (limp-home mode relies only on the local angle sensor for basic steering). Then the failure mode "CAN short to high" no longer directly violates the safety goal. It is covered by a safety mechanism.

### Step 3: Assess the diagnostic coverage

Diagnostic coverage (DC) quantifies how well a safety mechanism detects faults. The annex on diagnostic coverage in ISO 26262-5 provides a reference table; you assess against it:

- If CAN transmit timeout is detected by a watchdog timeout: DC might be rated "medium" (90%).
- If the CAN receiver implements frame counter, CRC error, and timeout detection: DC might be rated "high" (99%).
- If there is no detection at all: DC = 0%.

You assign DC = 99% to the "CAN communication lost" safety mechanism. That means:

- 99% of "CAN short to high" faults are detected in time and the system enters the safe state.
- The remaining 1% is called a **residual fault**: the fault occurs, but the safety mechanism fails to detect it.

After this step, the original single-point fault is split into a "covered part" and a "residual fault." **Only the uncovered part enters the numerator of the SPFM (single-point fault metric).**

### Step 4: Separate residual faults from latent faults

This is the pair of concepts most easily confused, and a frequent interview question in functional safety.

**Residual fault**: the portion of a fault in a function monitored by a safety mechanism that the safety mechanism fails to detect. In the CAN example above, the 1% is the residual fault.

**Latent fault**: a multiple-point fault that is neither detected by a safety mechanism nor perceived by the driver within the multiple-point fault detection interval. Its most common cause lies in the **safety mechanism itself**: the monitoring channel fails silently while the monitored function keeps running normally.

For example, the watchdog timer you use to detect loss of CAN communication can itself fail. If the watchdog fails silently while CAN communication is fine, you notice nothing. The watchdog fault lies "latent" until one day CAN really breaks, and the watchdog can no longer trigger the safe state.

The hardware part of ISO 26262 explicitly requires that, for every safety-related hardware element, you assess not only residual faults against the safety goal but also latent faults in the safety mechanisms. You **must** assess both diagnostic coverages: for residual faults and for latent faults.

### Step 5: The multiple-point fault detection interval and the fault tolerant time interval

Two more headache-inducing abbreviations: MPFDI (Multiple-Point Fault Detection Interval) and FTTI (Fault Tolerant Time Interval).

The FTTI is the upper bound of your time budget: the shortest time from the occurrence of a fault to a possible hazard. If your EPS system can send the car out of its lane 500 ms after CAN communication is lost, the FTTI is 500 ms. Your safety mechanism must detect the fault and enter the safe state inside that window.

The MPFDI is the self-test interval of the safety mechanism. A watchdog doesn't check every nanosecond. It has a patrol cycle. That cycle must be significantly shorter than the FTTI, or the hazard will have happened by the time you notice.

Map this onto the immune system: after a wound gets infected with bacteria, disinfecting it within an hour can stop a systemic infection. That's the FTTI. The immune system patrols the blood every few minutes. That's the MPFDI.


## How FMEDA feeds SPFM, LFM, and PMHF

After the FMEDA, you aggregate all faults according to the fault classification in ISO 26262-5:

- **λ_SPF** = sum of the failure rates of all single-point faults (faults not covered by any safety mechanism)
- **λ_RF** = sum of the failure rates of all residual faults (covered, but not fully)
- **λ_MPF,L** = sum of the failure rates of all latent multiple-point faults (the part neither detected by a safety mechanism nor perceived by the driver within the multiple-point fault detection interval)
- **λ_MPF,DP** = detected or perceived multiple-point faults

Then you get to the homework you actually have to hand in: the three hardware architectural metrics:

**SPFM (single-point fault metric)** = 1 - (λ_SPF + λ_RF) / λ_total

> SPFM measures the share of all safety-related failures that are neither single-point nor residual faults. ASIL B requires ≥90%, ASIL C requires ≥97%, ASIL D requires ≥99%.

**LFM (latent fault metric)** = 1 - λ_MPF,L / (λ_total - λ_SPF - λ_RF)

> LFM measures how low the share of latent faults is among the failures left after removing single-point and residual faults. ASIL B requires ≥60%, ASIL C requires ≥80%, ASIL D requires ≥90%.

**PMHF (probabilistic metric for random hardware failures)** ≈ λ_SPF + λ_RF + λ_DPF,det × λ_DPF,latent × T_Lifetime

> PMHF adds the single-point and residual failure rates that "directly violate the safety goal" to a two-point combination term: one failure rate times another, times the exposure time. The result is the average probability of violation per hour over the operating life. The failure rate of a latent fault, λ_MPF,L, must be multiplied by the failure rate of another independent fault before it can enter this term; adding two failure rates directly doesn't produce a dimension of 1/h. The ASIL D limit is 10⁻⁸/h, which is 10 FIT.

**SPFM, LFM, and PMHF are not numbers the safety manager types into an Excel formula. They come from 47 rows, every failure mode in every row, and every safety-mechanism coverage assessment, aggregated systematically.**


## Summary

1. FMEDA breaks down every failure mode at the component level: open, short, drift, stuck-at. Each has its own fault type and safety effect.
2. Classify by safe fault, single-point fault, residual fault, and multiple-point fault (perceived/detected/latent). Faults that cannot violate the safety goal are safe faults; those that can fall into the later categories.
3. Diagnostic coverage measures how effective the safety mechanism you designed for each dangerous fault really is.
4. All three hardware metrics — SPFM, LFM, PMHF — come from FMEDA base data: the failure mode distribution and diagnostic coverage assessment of each of the 47 rows.
5. FMEDA accuracy doesn't depend on the Excel formula. It depends on whether the source of each failure mode's share is reliable.
