# 2.8 LFM and Latent Faults: Is Your Watchdog Still Alive?


Three in the afternoon. Your SPFM has finally reached 99.2%, just over the ASIL D threshold. You pick up your coffee cup to celebrate. Then a thought hits you like a current:

"Wait. That external watchdog chip. What is it marked as in my FMEDA?"

You flip to that row. The watchdog chip's total failure rate is λ_WDG = 15 FIT. If it fails (its internal oscillator stops, say) while the main MCU happens to be running normally, what happens? **Nothing.** The system works as usual, no alarm, no anomaly, everything as calm as the abyss. The failure is not detected, because the watchdog is a latent guardian by nature. When it is not failing, you cannot feel that it exists.

Then, one afternoon a week later, a voltage dip makes the main MCU's program run away. It should be reset by the watchdog. But the watchdog is already dead. Two independent faults, watchdog failure + MCU runaway, stack up perfectly, and the safety goal is breached in an instant.

This watchdog fault is a **latent fault**. Your SPFM pays no attention to it, because SPFM looks only at SPF and RF, the faults that kill in one step. A latent fault is a kind of multiple-point fault: one that needs a partner to kill. ISO 26262-5:2018 sets up a separate metric for it: the **LFM (latent fault metric)**.

You quietly put the coffee cup down and reopen the FMEDA. The work is far from over.


## 1. Latent Faults: Functional Safety's "Asymptomatic Carriers"

### 1.1 Exact Definition

The standard's vocabulary defines a latent fault as follows:

> **Latent fault:** a multiple-point fault whose presence is not detected by a safety mechanism and not perceived by the driver within the specified time period.

A few key terms:

- **"Multiple-point fault":** on its own it does not violate the safety goal. It becomes a threat only **in combination** with another independent fault. That property separates it from SPF and RF by definition.
- **"Specified time period":** the multiple-point fault detection interval (MPFDI). ISO 26262-5 requires MPFDI to be set during design and reflected in the LFM calculation. Typical MPFDIs are the power-on self-test cycle (every power-up), the start of a driving cycle, or a fixed interval (a self-test every hour, for example).
- **"Neither detected by a safety mechanism nor perceived by the driver":** if a safety mechanism finds the fault within the MPFDI, it becomes a "detected multiple-point fault" (MPF_D); if the driver can notice it (the function is obviously abnormal, say), it is a "perceived multiple-point fault" (MPF_P). Only a fault that completely hides from both the system and the person is a latent fault (MPF_L).

### 1.2 Typical Latent-Fault Scenarios

In real design you will meet these typical latent-fault scenarios:

| Hardware element | Latent failure mode | Why it stays latent | Fault it must combine with |
|---------|------------|--------------|-------------|
| External watchdog | Internal oscillator stops | While the main MCU runs normally, the watchdog never needs to step in | Main MCU program runs away |
| Redundant power monitoring | Monitoring comparator fails | While the main supply is good, the redundant channel has nothing to say | Main supply drops out |
| ECC memory controller | ECC logic for single-bit correction fails | With no bit flip in memory, the ECC logic has no correction to do | An uncorrectable multiple-bit error occurs in memory |
| Safety shutdown path MOSFET | Drive transistor in the shutdown path opens | Normal operation needs no shutdown, so the MOSFET never acts | A fault requiring emergency shutdown appears |
| ADC reference voltage monitoring | Monitoring comparator threshold drifts | While the reference is in range, the monitor does not trigger | The reference drifts abnormally and ADC measurements become inaccurate |

The common feature of all these scenarios: **a protector that is always online but never speaks up, whose own failure no one knows about.** This maps perfectly onto the medical "asymptomatic carrier": carrying a pathogen, no symptoms, normal life, yet at any moment able to infect someone else (combine with another fault) and cause severe disease.

## 2. The LFM Formula: Latent Risk Down to Every FIT

### 2.1 Definition

The LFM equation in the normative annex of ISO 26262-5:2018:

$$LFM = 1 - \frac{\sum_{SR,HW}(\lambda_{MPF,L})}{\sum_{SR,HW}(\lambda - \lambda_{SPF} - \lambda_{RF})} = \frac{\sum_{SR,HW}(\lambda_{MPF,DP} + \lambda_S)}{\sum_{SR,HW}(\lambda - \lambda_{SPF} - \lambda_{RF})}$$

The formula is elegantly constructed. Let's take it term by term:

**Denominator: Σ_{SR,HW}(λ − λ_SPF − λ_RF)**
- The subscript SR,HW means the sum runs only over safety-related hardware elements.
- Σ_{SR,HW}(λ − λ_SPF − λ_RF) is equivalent to Σ_{SR,HW}(λ_MPF + λ_S).
- That's because for each safety-related hardware element, λ = λ_SPF + λ_RF + λ_MPF + λ_S.
- So the denominator is exactly **the sum of the failure rates of all multiple-point faults and safe faults**.
- Put another way: the denominator contains only faults that are not fatal in one step. SPF and RF are excluded, because LFM doesn't care about them. SPFM owns them.

**The subtrahend in the numerator: Σ(λ_MPF,L), the sum of the failure rates of all latent multiple-point faults**
- This is the part that actually lies in wait for an accomplice.
- Note: detected (MPF_D) and perceived (MPF_P) multiple-point faults do not enter the numerator, because they have already been found or noticed and are no longer latent.

**Equivalent form: Σ(λ_MPF,D + λ_MPF,P + λ_S) / Σ(λ_MPF + λ_S)**
- The numerator is all the benign, non-latent share: detected multiple-point faults, driver-perceived multiple-point faults, and safe faults.
- This view is more intuitive: among all non-single-point-fatal faults, what share cannot stay latent?

### 2.2 LFM vs. SPFM: Why Is the LFM Threshold Lower?

You may wonder: SPFM requires 99% for ASIL D, while LFM requires only 90% for the same ASIL D. Nine percentage points apart. Why is the standard more lenient with "the latent watchdog" than with "a direct single-point fault"?

The answer has two layers:

**First, the consequences differ.** SPF and RF are fatal in one step: their occurrence equals a safety goal violation, and the consequence is catastrophic. MPF_L needs to wait for another independent fault before it can cause a violation. That conditional nature lowers its instantaneous danger in probability terms. Statistically, the joint probability of two independent events is far below that of a single event.

**Second, the detection window differs.** SPF/RF may go from occurrence to harm in milliseconds, with no time to react. MPF_L has a chance to be caught by the MPFDI: a power-on self-test, for example, can "wake up" a batch of latent faults. That gives the designer an extra safety net.

So the LFM target sits one notch below SPFM. That matches engineering intuition. But don't relax:

| ASIL | Minimum LFM | Minimum SPFM (for comparison) | Gap |
|---------|-----------|-------------------|------|
| ASIL B | ≥ 60% | ≥ 90% | 30 percentage points |
| ASIL C | ≥ 80% | ≥ 97% | 17 percentage points |
| ASIL D | ≥ 90% | ≥ 99% | 9 percentage points |

Note the pattern: the higher the ASIL, the smaller the gap between SPFM and LFM. ASIL B is 30 points apart; ASIL D only 9. The implication: **in high-ASIL systems, latent faults are treated almost as seriously as single-point faults.** System complexity and potential fault-coupling paths grow sharply with ASIL.


## 3. LFM Worked Example: Calculating Your Watchdog Risk

Back to your steering ECU. The safety-goal-related hardware failure distribution in the FMEDA is as follows (continuing the example from section 2.7):

| Fault class | Symbol | Value (FIT) |
|---------|------|-----------|
| Single-point fault | λ_SPF | 2.3 |
| Residual fault | λ_RF | 1.7 |
| Detected multiple-point fault | λ_MPF,D | 18.0 |
| Perceived multiple-point fault | λ_MPF,P | 2.0 |
| Latent multiple-point fault | λ_MPF,L | 15.0 |
| Safe fault | λ_S | 61.0 |
| **Total failure rate** | **λ_total** | **100.0** |

LFM calculation:

**Step 1: Compute the denominator**

Denominator = Σ(λ − λ_SPF − λ_RF) = Σ(λ_MPF + λ_S) = 18.0 + 2.0 + 15.0 + 61.0 = 96.0 FIT

**Step 2: The subtrahend in the numerator**

Σ(λ_MPF,L) = 15.0 FIT (the sum of latent faults in the three watchdog-related hardware elements: the external watchdog chip, the window comparator, and the MOSFET drive circuit in the reset path)

**Step 3: Substitute into the formula**

$$LFM = 1 - \frac{15.0}{96.0} = 1 - 0.15625 = 0.84375 \approx 84.4\%$$

84.4%. Against the ASIL D requirement of ≥ 90%, that is 5.6 points short. **LFM fails.**

That means that in the 96 FIT of "non-single-point-fatal" failure space, 15 FIT of latent faults are caught by nothing at all (not power-on self-test, not periodic diagnostics, not driver perception). Those 15 FIT are like 15 sleeping moles, ready to be activated by another independent fault.

### 3.1 How to Raise LFM: Three Targeted Moves

Faced with an LFM of 84.4%, you open the 15 FIT of latent-fault detail and start cleaning up:

**Move 1: Add periodic self-test (turn MPF_L into MPF_D)**

Your watchdog chip currently gets only a simple "kick-and-read-back" test at power-up. If it fails while running after power-up, it becomes latent. Change to a full watchdog function self-test **every driving cycle (or every hour)**: deliberately let the watchdog time out and verify that it produces a reset correctly. That needs extra software logic (a fault-injection trigger), but it raises the watchdog's latent-fault detection coverage from "no detection" to "medium coverage".

Suppose the watchdog's original λ_MPF,L = 8.0 FIT, and periodic self-test raises K_DC,MPF,L to 90%:
- remaining latent share = 8.0 × (1 − 90%) = 0.8 FIT
- saving = 8.0 − 0.8 = 7.2 FIT

**Move 2: Add redundant hardware monitoring (compare redundant paths)**

For latent failure of the power-monitoring comparator, add a second independent voltage reference and comparator, and check the two comparator outputs for consistency (XOR logic). If the two voltage-monitoring results disagree, raise an alarm. This can raise that component's K_DC,MPF,L to high coverage (99%), leaving under 0.1 FIT latent.

**Move 3: Use the driver-perception channel (turn MPF_L into MPF_P)**

For some fault scenarios, the safety mechanism does not catch the fault, but the driver can indirectly notice the anomaly in normal operation. For example, if the current sensor of the steering assist motor drifts without crossing a detection threshold, the driver may indirectly "perceive" it through abnormal steering feel together with a dashboard warning. Marking such scenarios as MPF_P rather than MPF_L raises LFM.

Suppose you reassess 2.5 FIT of latent faults as "perceived". Then:

new Σ(λ_MPF,L) = 15.0 − 7.2 − 0.9 − 2.5 = 4.4 FIT

$$LFM_{new} = 1 - \frac{4.4}{96.0} = 1 - 0.0458 = 0.9542 \approx 95.4\%$$

95.4%, comfortably above the ASIL D requirement of ≥ 90%. You have plenty of margin to pass the audit.


## 4. MPFDI: The Time Dimension of LFM

The LFM calculation carries an implicit timing assumption: **the MPFDI must be short enough to expose a latent fault within what you consider an acceptable time.** ISO 26262-5:2018 requires MPFDI to be defined during design and justified in the safety case.

The choice of MPFDI directly affects LFM: change no hardware, just shorten the self-test period, and LFM rises. But a shorter MPFDI has costs:

- **Longer power-on self-test:** running the full self-test sequence at every power-up extends system initialization.
- **Runtime self-test disturbs safety functions:** while a self-test runs, the safety mechanism is temporarily out of service. If you "fake-kick" the watchdog to test it, a real fault in that instant will not be responded to.
- **More software complexity:** the self-test logic can itself introduce new system faults.

Typical MPFDI strategies:
- **Low-risk systems (ASIL B):** power-on self-test plus a runtime self-test every few driving cycles; the MPFDI may be tens of hours.
- **Medium- and high-risk systems (ASIL C/D):** power-on self-test + self-test at the start of every driving cycle + a runtime self-test every one to two hours; the MPFDI is usually kept within a few hours.

## 5. Diagnostic Coverage for Latent Faults

Like the residual-fault diagnostic coverage K_DC,RF we discussed for SPFM, LFM uses **the diagnostic coverage for latent multiple-point faults, K_DC,MPF,L**:

$$\lambda_{MPF,L} \leq \lambda \times \left(1 - \frac{K_{DC,MPF,L}}{100\%}\right)$$

The coverage levels come from the diagnostic coverage annex in ISO 26262-5 and again fall into low (60%), medium (90%), and high (99%). But note: **the coverages for SPFM and LFM are assessed independently.**

A safety mechanism may have high coverage for residual faults (the lockstep CPU checking the main MCU reaches 99% for transient faults) but only low coverage for its own latent faults (a failure of the lockstep comparator itself is hard to expose while the main MCU is healthy). Both K_DC values must be recorded separately in the FMEDA.


## 6. The Immune Mapping: Latent Faults Are "Asymptomatic Carriers"

The exact medical-immunology counterpart of a latent fault is the **asymptomatic carrier**. The mapping is deep:

| Functional safety concept | Immunological counterpart | Explanation |
|------------|----------|------|
| **Latent fault (MPF_L)** | Asymptomatic carrier | The person carries a pathogen with no clinical symptoms, lives a normal social life, and can infect others |
| **Periodic self-test of safety mechanisms** | Regular checkups / screening | Finds carriers in the asymptomatic stage so they can be isolated or treated |
| **MPFDI (detection interval)** | Checkup interval | The more frequent the test, the shorter the window in which a carrier goes unnoticed |
| **Detected multiple-point fault (MPF_D)** | A carrier already found by screening and isolated | The carrier has moved from "asymptomatic and latent" to "under surveillance" |
| **Perceived multiple-point fault (MPF_P)** | Mild symptoms already raise alarm | Non-specific symptoms such as low fever and fatigue prompt the person to seek care |
| **Combining to violate the safety goal** | Carrier meets an immunosuppressed person → severe outbreak | A latent tuberculosis carrier meets a person with HIV/AIDS |
| **LFM** | Detection rate of asymptomatic carriers in a population | What share of all carriers have already been found by screening |
| **LFM ≥ 90%** | 90% of asymptomatic carriers are registered and tracked | The remaining 10% undetected carry residual community transmission risk |

The mapping reveals LFM's deeper logic: you cannot detect every latent fault (just as public health cannot screen 100% of asymptomatic carriers), but you must detect enough that **even in the worst case, the combined probability of a remaining latent fault and an independent fault triggering together stays below the acceptable risk threshold**.

The quantitative measure of that acceptable risk threshold is the PMHF we take up in the next section: the average failure probability per hour.

## Summary

1. A latent fault is a kind of multiple-point fault: not fatal alone, needing another independent fault to combine with
2. LFM = 1 − Σ(λ_MPF,L) / Σ(λ − λ_SPF − λ_RF)
3. LFM targets for ASIL B/C/D: ≥ 60% / ≥ 80% / ≥ 90%
4. Three ways to raise it: add periodic self-test, add redundant hardware monitoring, use driver perception
5. MPFDI is the time dimension of LFM
