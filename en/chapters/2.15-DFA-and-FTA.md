# 2.15 DFA and FTA: Digging into the Root System of Failure

## The immune metaphor: tracing the cause — from symptom to pathogen

When your body has a fever, a cough, and fatigue, you sit in front of a doctor. An experienced doctor doesn't reach straight for an antipyretic. She performs a **differential diagnosis**. A fever could be a bacterial infection, a viral infection, an autoimmune disease, or even a tumor. Starting from the symptom (the top event), the doctor lists all possible causes (intermediate events), rules out a cold and pneumonia (pruning), and finally narrows it to a streptococcal infection (the basic event). That is FTA: fault tree analysis.

At the same time, the doctor considers a deeper question: why didn't your immune system stop the streptococcus? Because you are taking immunosuppressants? Because a sudden cold snap damaged your mucosal barrier? Because aging has weakened your immune function? These factors are not the pathogen itself, but they are **coupling factors**. They turn individually non-fatal weaknesses into a fatal combination. That is DFA: dependent failure analysis.

FTA asks "what would cause the safety goal to be violated." DFA asks "why do things that look independent fail together." The two complement each other and together form the "root system" of safety analysis. When you cannot answer "what is the root cause" in functional safety, you don't have a safety argument. You have a guess.

## Scenario: a fault tree for steering system failure

You're in the safety analysis office, and the wall in front of you is one whiteboard from ceiling to floor. Marker in hand, you are drawing an upside-down tree.

At the top of the tree you write the top event: **"Loss of steering assistance: SG-001" (Safety Goal 001)**. This is the output of HARA (hazard analysis and risk assessment): loss of steering assistance at highway speed can cause an ASIL D hazard. Your task: start at the top of the tree and trace every fault path that could cause the top event downward, until you reach basic events that cannot be decomposed further.

**Level one: direct causes under an OR gate**

Below the top event you draw a bold OR gate. Loss of steering assistance can have three direct causes:
- MCU system failure
- Power supply system failure
- Motor drive system failure

These three intermediate events are connected by an OR gate: if any one occurs, the top event holds. This is the first level of your analysis. Each intermediate event represents a subsystem that needs further analysis.

**Level two: expanding MCU system failure**

Below "MCU system failure" you draw another OR gate. Possible causes of MCU system failure:
- Main control MCU failure
- Safety monitor MCU failure
- Inter-MCU communication (SPI) failure
- Shared peripherals (crystal oscillator, reset circuit) failure

Note the last one: "shared peripherals failure." It already hints at dependence, but for now you continue with OR logic.

**Level two: expanding power supply system failure**

- Main power rail (12 V) failure
- DC-DC converter failure
- Supply monitoring/management IC failure
- Decoupling capacitor aging causing excessive ripple

**Level two: expanding motor drive system failure**

- Three-phase bridge drive failure
- Phase current sensor failure
- Power MOSFET thermal shutdown
- Bus voltage droop

**Level three: expanding "safety monitor MCU failure" — an AND gate appears**

You continue into "safety monitor MCU failure." The monitor MCU itself has redundant protection built in, so you need to draw what combination inside it would leave the monitor unable to detect a main-control fault when it matters.

Below "safety monitor MCU failure" you draw an AND gate:
- `main core computation error (bit-flip)`
- `lockstep comparator fails to detect the bit-flip`
- `watchdog fails to detect the lockstep comparator's failure`

These three sub-events are connected by an AND gate. This is a key node: only when all three fail **at the same time** does the monitor MCU fail to do its job when needed. The AND gate represents redundant protection: the lockstep comparator can detect the bit-flip, and the watchdog can detect the lockstep comparator's failure. Only when all three layers of protection are breached at once does the monitor MCU fail.

Every path that reaches the top event is a **cut set**. If the path passes through an AND gate, the cut set contains several basic events. If it contains only OR gates, the cut set is a single point. A **minimal cut set** is one that cannot be reduced further: remove any one basic event and the top event no longer holds.

You expand level by level on the whiteboard until every branch reaches a basic event: an underlying fault that cannot or should not be decomposed further. Basic events might be:
- Fatigue crack in a MOSFET package solder joint (physical failure, probability estimated from FIT data)
- Crystal oscillator failing to start (manufacturing defect)
- MCU register bit-flip (single-event upset, probability estimated from cosmic-ray flux and die area)
- Supply IC output overvoltage (component aging)

A complete fault tree fills an entire wall. OR gates form the trunk: single-point faults propagate straight upward. AND gates are the tree's "safety net": a fault propagates upward only when multiple redundant elements fail at once.

## FTA methodology: qualitative and quantitative analysis

FTA has two analysis modes, serving different stages of the safety argument.

**Qualitative FTA** focuses on logical structure. By analyzing minimal cut sets, you can answer:
- Which single-point faults can directly violate the safety goal? (These are the most dangerous: a single-point fault must be eliminated or controlled to a sufficiently low probability.)
- Which fault combinations (multi-event cut sets under AND gates) are needed to cause a violation? (These are latent faults: you must show that each event has enough diagnostic coverage to expose it.)
- Are there "unexpected" minimal cut sets — fault paths that architecture design did not anticipate? (If so, you need to change the design or add a safety mechanism.)

Qualitative FTA helps you understand the **logical paths of failure**. It doesn't care about probability. It cares only about "can it happen." If qualitative FTA reveals any single-point fault that reaches the top event and is not covered by any safety mechanism, that is a defect that must be fixed at the design level. You cannot paper over it with a probabilistic argument.

**Quantitative FTA** introduces probability. Each basic event is assigned a failure rate, usually expressed in FIT (Failures In Time, failures per billion hours). For several basic events connected by an AND gate, the top event's failure rate is the product of the basic event failure rates (assuming independence). For an OR gate, it is the sum of the basic event failure rates (under the low-probability approximation). Watch the dimensions here: multiplying two failure rates gives h⁻², and to get back to a failure rate you still have to multiply by the time the two coexist.

An example: if the monitor MCU's bit-flip probability is 100 FIT (10⁻⁷/h), the lockstep comparator's failure probability is 10 FIT (10⁻⁸/h), and the watchdog's failure probability is 10 FIT (10⁻⁸/h), then the probability of all three occurring together (the top-event probability at the AND gate output) is roughly:
- 100×10⁻⁹ × 10×10⁻⁹ × 10×10⁻⁹ = 10⁻²³ h⁻³ — still completely negligible after multiplying by the coexistence time factor.

But if the lockstep comparator and the watchdog share the same clock source, and that clock source has a failure probability of 100 FIT (10⁻⁷/h), then when the clock fails, the lockstep comparator and the watchdog fail together. The AND gate now has only two independent events (bit-flip and clock failure), and the probability jumps to:
- 100 FIT × 100 FIT ≈ 10⁻¹⁴ h⁻² — numerically still very low; using a one-hour time factor, it is a billion times higher than the three-term product.

That is why DFA must be used together with FTA: **FTA assumes basic events are independent, and DFA verifies whether that assumption holds.** If DFA finds a coupling factor, the quantitative result of FTA may severely underestimate the real risk.

**Quantitative FTA has a key limit on its scope: it handles only random hardware failures, not systematic failures.** You have no FIT data that can describe the probability of a software bug: a bug is not random, it is deterministically present in the code. If the code always divides by zero for a particular input, the failure rate is not "low probability" but "100% when the condition is triggered." Quantitative FTA cannot assign a probability to a systematic error. This is a constraint stated in ISO 26262-9. Systematic failures must be controlled through process (the other parts of ISO 26262), not "diluted" with probability calculations.

## DFA methodology: common-cause and cascading failures

FTA looks from the top down. DFA looks sideways from coupling factors.

A **common-cause failure (CCF)** is a single event that causes multiple elements to fail at the same time. A classic example: two redundant MCUs share one external watchdog chip. The watchdog chip fails, both MCUs lose supervision, and the system goes blind to both failures at once. Another example: two sensors share one power rail, and an overvoltage transient on the rail destroys both.

A **cascading failure** is the failure of one element propagating to another and causing it to fail too. For example: a power MOSFET shorts, causing a large current; the large current burns through a PCB copper trace; the burnt trace cuts power to the MCU. This is not "one cause hits two at once" but "one failure triggers a domino effect."

ISO 26262-9 requires you to systematically identify and assess dependent failures at the architecture level. You don't guess. You check item by item against the **classification of coupling factors (the annex on coupling factors)**:

**1. Shared resource**: power, clock, reset, PCB substrate, heatsink, connectors... any physical resource shared by multiple safety-related elements is a potential coupling factor. In your FTA you assumed the MCU and the monitor are independent. But if they share the same 12 V to 3.3 V DC-DC converter, that converter is a candidate for common-cause failure.

**2. Shared information input**: two safety elements depend on the same sensor signal or the same CAN frame. If a sensor fails and outputs a value that "looks normal but is actually wrong," both elements are misled. That is why critical signals need redundant sensors or diversity (different measurement principles) to break the coupling of a shared information input.

**3. Insufficient environmental immunity**: if two elements have similar tolerance to environmental stress (temperature, vibration, EMI, humidity), that stress can break through both at once. Your MCU selection table says "operating temperature -40 to 125 °C," but if the actual ambient temperature can exceed 125 °C, every chip will have problems. EMI is the most insidious environmental stress: a strong electromagnetic pulse can flip registers in two MCUs at the same time, no matter how independent their architectures are.

**4. Systematic coupling**: the same design error, the same development tool defect, the same production process problem. Homogeneous redundancy — two MCUs of the same type running the same firmware — is the textbook case of systematic coupling. The same compiler bug produces the same wrong output in both code bases. That is why the standard is cautious about homogeneous redundancy justifying an ASIL downgrade: without DFA evidence, it generally is not enough.

**5. Identical type components**: two electrolytic capacitors of the same type, with the same electrolyte formula. If the formula has a systematic defect (say a batch of electrolyte with insufficient purity), both capacitors fail at roughly the same time. This is both systematic coupling (same-batch defect) and physical coupling (same failure mechanism). The antidote is **diversity**: use different types, different suppliers, or even different component technologies (tantalum capacitors instead of electrolytic).

**6. Communication**: the communication link between two elements is itself a coupling source. SPI, I2C, CAN — any communication protocol can have failure modes (bit flips, lost frames, timeouts), and a communication fault affects sender and receiver at once. That is why safety-related communication must use end-to-end protection (E2E): CRC detects data corruption, a sequence counter (SQC) detects lost or replayed frames, and a timeout detects a broken link. E2E protection breaks the coupling effect of the communication link.

**7. Unintended interface**: a physical interaction that arises unexpectedly between two elements that are not supposed to interact by design. Crosstalk between PCB traces, noise coupling between power domains inside a chip, heat conduction through a heatsink. These "interfaces" do not exist in the design documents but do exist in the physical world, which makes them the most dangerous coupling factors: routine analysis will not find them.

Beside the whiteboard you open another area and mark the DFA result for each coupling factor in a different color. Power rail: assessed — two independent DC-DC converters plus input overvoltage protection; independence confirmed. Clock: assessed — each MCU uses its own crystal and its own PLL; independence confirmed. Communication (SPI): assessed — E2E Profile 1 enabled, CRC-8, DataID, and a 4-bit counter, plus timeout protection. Systematic coupling: assessed — heterogeneous MCUs (Infineon + TI), heterogeneous compilers, independent development teams. Shared information input: **problem found** — both MCUs receive steering angle sensor data over the same CAN channel. If the sensor output drifts, both MCUs are misled.

You draw a red exclamation mark next to this finding. This is the typical output of a DFA: a coupling factor that FTA missed. The fix: add a second steering angle sensor (different supplier, different measurement principle — for example Hall effect plus magnetoresistance), let each MCU read one channel, and cross-check them.

## DFA and FTA working together in ASIL decomposition

Now you can look at DFA and FTA together. In the previous chapter you performed ASIL decomposition: you split ASIL D into B(D)+B(D). At the time you promised to "prove sufficient independence." DFA is the tool that delivers on that promise. FTA is the tool that verifies "the decomposed architecture still satisfies the safety goal."

The flow goes like this:
1. **FTA builds the logical model of failure**: start from violation of the safety goal and derive every possible fault path downward. This is the logical verification of the safety concept.
2. **ASIL decomposition establishes the redundant architecture**: which elements work independently and together achieve the safety goal.
3. **DFA verifies that the redundancy really is independent**: check each coupling factor category item by item to make sure no common cause or cascade can defeat the redundancy.
4. **Revise the FTA. If DFA finds coupling, it must be reflected in the FTA.** For example, if a shared-power coupling factor is identified by DFA but cannot be eliminated, you must add a common parent node "shared power failure" in the FTA and place every element that depends on that supply under it.
5. **Quantitative FTA assesses residual risk**: based on the revised fault tree (including the coupling factors found by DFA), calculate whether PMHF meets the target.

This closed loop reveals the iterative nature of safety analysis: architecture design → FTA → DFA → find coupling → modify the architecture or the FTA → re-verify — until every assumption is confirmed and every residual risk is acceptable.

## Summary

1. FTA starts from the safety goal: OR gates expand single-point fault paths, AND gates depict the logical barriers of redundant protection. A single minimal cut set gives a sufficient condition for the top event; only the union of all minimal cut sets is both sufficient and necessary.
2. DFA checks seven categories of coupling factors one by one (shared resources, shared information input, environmental immunity, systematic coupling, identical type components, communication, unintended interface) and exposes the fragility of the "independence" assumption.
3. FTA + DFA form a closed loop: FTA models → ASIL decomposition → DFA verifies → revise FTA → quantitative assessment → iterate to convergence.
